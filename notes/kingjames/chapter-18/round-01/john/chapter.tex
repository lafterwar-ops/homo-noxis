\begin{center}\small\itshape Mountain - inside the Greater Seal\end{center}

\begin{room}

\textit{In Noxius}

(Paul): --- The hearing, Kailan. The sealed chamber, the five, the ruling. I need your account beside the recording.

(Kailan): --- No. Put it away. It fell three days since, and it is falling still out there, on people who never heard our names. Who is left above to read how carefully we decided?

(Paul): --- I cannot tell you who remains. I can tell you what is missing from this reel. It keeps what the five said. It does not keep what you could see from the wall, or what you understood when they asked for your finding.

(Kailan): --- Then write what you saw. Or ask Sarah. Ask Va-Sheva. I stood with my hands bound and said almost nothing.

(Paul): --- Va-Sheva will not speak to me. Sarah gives me her back. The Keepers are turning air and counting fish. I have asked them. At present I have my account, and I was occupied with the machine for much of it.

(Kailan): --- Not now. I have a shift on the vents and nothing in me to give you.

\centerline{[\,---\,]}

(Paul): --- The road, then. Could you give me that much? From Blackwood to the Mountain.

(Kailan): --- \ldots{} You can do that yourself. You kept the record. I will take it up once we reach the Mountain. Let me rest.

(Paul): --- Very well. The road is Paul Morgan's account. What I saw, what I was told, and the records I could examine. We stop at the Justice approach.

\end{room}

I left Blackwood with a coat, my notebooks and the travelling recorder. The dressing Kailan had put round my right hand caught under the carrying strap. At the lawn's edge I stopped to free it, and Va-Elise came back for me. The helicopter was turning. Behind her, doors I had never seen open admitted men carrying cases into the halls. Every window was lit.\par

At the house steps a man tried to give Sarah three files. She chose one and sent the other two back. Another asked what should become of the instruction rooms.\par

``Lock them. The instruction reels stay in Elise's fire case. Nothing else leaves except Mr Morgan's sealed interview reels and notebooks.''\par

``The telephones?''\par

``Let them ring.''\par

That was the last order I heard given inside the Blackwood Estate. The travelling tape reached its clear leader before we boarded. I wrote the hour on the box, sealed the reel in its tin and gave it to Va-Elise. She set it with the sealed interviews, separate from the instruction boxes inside the fire case. The single route packet lay above them. I fitted the fresh spool she gave me while a crewman loaded Sarah's chair. Kailan stood beside the door until Sarah was aboard.\par

The house door remained open. A sheet of paper crossed the lawn, rose into the rotor wind and vanished over the roof.\par

The helicopter lifted in a lurch that put my stomach through my boots. For two years I had walked across the landing marks without seeing them. Sarah, ninety-three, spent the flight on a headset, making clipped calls in three languages. Her right thumb shook against the mouthpiece until she closed it beneath her left hand. She asked why the private aircraft was still on the ground, listened, and gave someone a number of minutes in which to correct that.\par

The headset passed to Va-Elise. I could hear her confirming fuel and vehicles; she used numbers for the places. Sarah took it back and called the outer stations separately. Two calls ended with a spoken acknowledgment. During the third she repeated a number, waited and wrote a small cross beside it. She began the fourth with her pencil still against the page. I could not hear the replies through her earpiece.\par

Kailan sat opposite me. I kept the machine between my boots with its carrying strap around my wrist. Each time the helicopter banked, I drew it back against my ankles. I put on the recorder's headset and heard our breath under the engine, Sarah's numbers, and the fire case's buckle tapping against Va-Elise's wrist.\par

The helicopter put us down at Geneva beside the private aircraft. We waited in an old freight hangar. When the clearance lamp turned green, Urs remained behind the red line and we boarded. I had flown to digs in the cargo netting of transports older than myself and had never entered such a cabin. There were beds and a shower. Food arrived through a door I failed to notice, and I ate all of it. Kailan touched nothing. He watched the window. Sarah ate half a piece of toast and slept upright. Once she opened her eyes. ``Finish your meal, Mr Morgan. I do not know when any of us will eat properly again.'' Then she shut them again.\par

Va-Elise worked at the table with three maps that did not join. On the first she marked the aircraft's descent. On the second she marked the road the vehicles were to take. The third began where the road failed and contained no destination, only wells and strips of stone. I asked which of the wells still answered to Horizon. Va-Elise put her finger beside one.\par

``We meet Amastan here. He tells us which wells we can use.''\par

``And if no man comes?''\par

``Then we turn back.''\par

Sarah opened her eyes. ``Send no one looking for him. If he cannot reach his own meeting place, we have no safe road past it.''\par

When Va-Elise folded the maps, I lay down. Kailan was already asleep. We flew through the night; I woke as we began our descent.\par

\ornament

At dawn we landed on old colonial tarmac. Sweepings lay in little ridges beside the broken edge, where lamps burned inside jars. Beyond a windsock and two fuel drums I could see no building. Three black armoured vehicles waited with their engines turning. Jean-Charles came from the middle one. Kailan spoke his name and raised a hand. Va-Elise passed each driver his map and made him repeat his road and stopping place. I heard no destination beyond them.\par

Men in work clothes unloaded water, cloth, medical cases and sacks of feed. Weapons came last. Jean-Charles opened the long case across the quiet man's knees, inspected what lay inside and closed it without removing anything.\par

Sarah refused the chair. She descended the aircraft steps with Kailan one pace below her and Va-Elise behind, and stood at the bottom until her breathing settled. Then she asked the drivers whether anyone had approached the strip. One had seen lights beyond the pan. Another had heard an engine and no aircraft. She assigned the first report to the lead vehicle and the second to the rear.\par

Va-Elise took the lead. Jean-Charles rode beside our driver in the middle vehicle; Sarah, Kailan and I were strapped behind him, with the quiet man and his cased weapon in the rear seat. The third held our tail.\par

The sun cleared the horizon as we left the tarmac. Jean-Charles used no map. At each fork he waited for Va-Elise's rear lamp to show once and disappear. The driver spoke only to repeat a distance. Sarah held the station book closed on her knees. Kailan held the fire case between his boots after Va-Elise passed it through the window.\par

On the straight tar across the pan, speed shook the words out of my mouth. Vehicles appeared far to the east. The quiet man opened his case. Through the front glass I saw a weapon rise in the lead car. The distant vehicles crossed behind us without firing, and Jean-Charles kept them in his mirror until the road rose between us.\par

Va-Elise turned off at the next rise. We followed into a dry drainage cut, armour striking stone beneath my feet. The rear car continued along the tar. I watched its dust go north until Jean-Charles pushed my head below the window. We waited with the engine running. Another engine passed on the road above, then diminished. I could no longer distinguish it from our third vehicle. When that vehicle appeared behind us in the cut, Va-Elise moved again.\par

The tar ended beyond the pan. We went slowly over broken ground, stopping twice to change drivers with the engines still running. The quiet man slept with one hand through the case handle. Jean-Charles woke him after forty minutes and gave him the forward seat. Through the rear glass I watched our own dust; when the wind cleared it, the track was empty.\par

\ornament

They drove through the morning on a track a wheel could barely hold. Near its end Va-Elise stopped and pointed to an opening low in the rock. Sarah asked for the vehicles to wait while Jean-Charles examined it. He came back and spoke through her window. She undid her belt.\par

On the aircraft Sarah had described her stations: small buried rooms, two or three people, an instrument bank, supplies against a failed delivery. We entered this one through an open door. She stopped with her hand against its frame.\par

``They had orders to keep this shut. Direct clearance for every visitor.''\par

Inside, cool air came from the wall. The tap worked. Three cups stood beside the kettle, each marked differently at the handle; three names remained on the duty board. Blood had dried along the table lip and on the floor beneath it. Jean-Charles opened the rear room, checked the generator recess and came back shaking his head. I saw no bodies.\par

I held the recorder away from my coat and began with the door. No split wood, no broken hinge, no fresh scoring around the lock. Beside the drum one chair lay on its side; another had a broken back rail; the third held a dark handprint on its seat. A box of papers had burned in the corner. The upper leaves were black and curled. Brown sheets survived beneath them. I gave each place a number before I moved anything.\par

Five strokes arrived on the drum while I worked. Kailan stood beside it without touching the paper. Each rose evenly and fell away. After the fifth, the stylus resumed the ground's thin irregular line. Sarah watched it and checked her wristwatch.\par

``Again in forty minutes,'' she said. ``They are sounding on a programme. Not prospecting. Prospecting changes the question after each answer. This asks the same question until the answer alters.''\par

The burned box had held more paper than its depth allowed. Its lid was scorched on the inside and still caught beneath a bent clasp. I lifted the surviving sheets with forceps. Fuel. Water. Watch changes. A delivery entered four weeks before and cancelled in another hand. No station except this one appeared on the sheets I could separate. A corner crumbled and left a black crescent on the table.\par

The unfinished message lay beside them, written on the back of a stores request. Its first lines reported the five-stroke signal. The last legible words were \emph{door opened with}. A downward stroke began the next letter. The pencil rested in the fold; blood lay beneath the writer's side of the page and had spared the words. I left the ending incomplete in my notebook.\par

Va-Elise called us to the entrance. She showed Sarah a length of pale thread, then pointed out the heavier tread over earlier prints in the dust. Two sets continued away from the door. Beside them a scuff became parallel heel cuts. Beyond the rise she had photographed tyre marks. I could follow the marks once she placed my eye above them; I could not assign them to the names on the board.\par

``Taken, probably,'' Sarah said. ``We act on the chance that they are alive. Three people who can read my notation and show where these lines are buried. None can give another station's location, but these instruments and their own approaches will be enough to begin with.'' She put her hand on the table until the shaking stopped. ``Elise. If anyone has them, I want them back.''\par

She read the three names from the duty board into the recorder. After each name she gave an office, years of service and the person to whom news should be carried if there remained anyone able to carry it. On the third she had to look back at the board before she could give the years.\par

Va-Elise found the route drawer unlocked and empty. Sarah checked its label: this station's access and withdrawal papers. She returned to the entrance and examined the undamaged lock.\par

``A visitor they admitted?'' I asked.\par

``Possibly. Or someone with a key. Or someone they opened to under threat. Record the condition of the lock. We have no witness to the opening.''\par

She opened her own station book beside the empty drawer and worked down a page of call signs with the scorched pencil. Six received a cross. Va-Elise copied those six onto a separate list, read them back and waited for the order.\par

``Those share outside support with this post,'' Sarah said. ``The connection is in my book. Go back with the vehicles. Have each cell recalled separately through the central office, by its own cleared channel. No cell sends a messenger to another. No resupply on these roads. People first; break the instruments they cannot carry.''\par

``The house?'' Va-Elise asked.\par

``No one returns to Blackwood. Use the withdrawal arrangements. This is my authority; do not wait for a second order from me.''\par

``And if a post has already opened?''\par

Sarah pressed a finger against the list. ``Bring out anyone you can. Do not send another crew through an open door to find out what happened.''\par

Va-Elise put the list away. I held the machine towards Sarah while she repeated the three missing names. Under her voice it kept the generator tick, the stylus, and wind coming through the door she had ordered shut.\par

The next five strokes began while we were carrying the water out. All of us stopped. Dust moved in the split of the table. The kettle gave a small metallic answer. Sarah counted the interval aloud, then told us to keep moving.\par

\ornament

The vehicles stopped where the track entered folded rock. A young man waited there with two others and seven animals, his face bound against the glare. Sarah called him Amastan.\par

He counted us and our loads before greeting her. He rejected two water tins whose caps rang against the handles, wrapped the recorder's catches in felt and separated the documents from the food. His companions worked in the shade. One moved my spare boots twice before finding a place where they would not strike a tin. Jean-Charles brought the last load from the middle vehicle. The quiet man remained beside its open door with his weapon case.\par

``My grandfather carried that name for you,'' he said. ``He has been thirty years in the ground. I carry it now.'' He drew the last strap through its ring. ``Your officers put that name in their treaties before my grandfather was born. They spelled it wrong.''\par

Va-Elise opened a map on the bonnet of the lead vehicle. Amastan looked once, drew a different line in the dust with the butt of his switch and put three stones across the route she had marked.\par

``That road was safe when my father held the name. It has tyre cuts now. Men watched it two nights ago.'' He moved the line west through a blank place on her map. ``We go here.''\par

Jean-Charles crouched beside the drawing. Amastan made him repeat the first turns, then rubbed them out with his foot. Va-Elise gave Jean-Charles the tactical packet she had withheld until this handover. He opened it against his knees. I saw a strip of ground and two abort points. Amastan checked them and folded the page before I could read farther.\par

Va-Elise opened the fire case and transferred the sealed reel tins into a felt wrap. The interviews kept their seals; the instruction boxes remained separate. She checked each against my notebook before putting the wrap into my hands. The route papers stayed with her. ``This goes on your body when the animals stop,'' she said. ``Keep it dry. There is no house to send it back to.''\par

She collected the drivers' maps and returned to the lead vehicle. No return route passed to Jean-Charles. The vehicles divided on the first open ground, leaving three dust lines. Amastan watched until the last thinned into the glare. He shifted Sarah's saddle one hole and gave the word to move.\par

We set out at night. Amastan walked before the first animal without a light, over stone in a wind that filled the recorder's catches with grit. A companion followed last and brushed the places where a hoof slipped. Jean-Charles twice offered a bearing. Amastan heard him and continued along his chosen line. Before dawn he stopped beneath an overhang. His companions stretched ground-coloured cloth across it and weighted the edges. We lay behind it through the day.\par

By the second night I could tell a halt from a concealment. At a halt, the animals lowered their heads and someone checked the loads. At concealment, Amastan raised one closed hand and no buckle moved. Once we remained that way while an engine passed beyond the ridge. Its sound came first through the soles of my boots and only later through the air. I held the recorder against my coat to stop its fittings from clicking.\par

We reached a well before dawn, a crack at the back of a cleft. Amastan removed six fitted slabs, drew water with a skin narrow enough to pass between them, watered the animals and replaced the slabs in reverse order. His companion brushed the rock with a branch. When they had finished, I could not find the opening from three paces away.\par

``Does the Mountain keep this well?'' I asked.\par

``My people keep it.''\par

``For the Mountain?''\par

He coiled the line. ``For those who arrive thirsty and do not arrive with guns.''\par

Sarah rode in a sling they fitted around the saddle. At ninety-three she could still mount on the first night. By the third, Amastan and Kailan had to lift her. She allowed it without speaking. At noon her hand shook against the rim of her cup. I held the cup while she drank. When it was empty she said, ``Mr Morgan, if that appears in your account, at least spell my name correctly.''\par

``I always do.''\par

``One useful quality.''\par

Amastan shortened the passages after he and Kailan had lifted her. He put the longest climb in the coldest hour and placed Kailan to the left of her animal, beside the drop. Sarah looked from him to the ridge.\par

``We have already lost half a day.''\par

``You lose all the days if you fall.''\par

``I have crossed this country since before your father was born.''\par

``Then save your breath for the climb. I need you awake when we reach the rock.''\par

He turned away before she could answer. We left at the hour he had chosen.\par

On the third night he stopped before a cairn and counted its stones without touching them. He asked his companion to count again. Then he turned the animals.\par

``One too many,'' he told Sarah. ``We go round.''\par

The detour cost six hours. Kailan called the folds the Mountain's outer wall while Amastan retied a load.\par

``That wall has a well on each side,'' Amastan said. ``My people keep both. Tell Shield who took you past the watched one.''\par

I asked him to repeat the name of the well for the recorder. He pulled the strap through its ring and looked at the machine until I lowered it. I entered the six lost hours on a reel band and left the place blank. During the daylight hides I recorded speech when permission was given. At night I ran the machine for a few minutes when the ground changed: hoof-fall on sand, a shoe striking buried stone, wind at a narrow passage. I dated each reel and wrapped it before loading the next. Every interval I did not record received an entry in my notebook.\par

On the fourth night I felt the five strokes through the ground. At the first, Sarah's animal raised its head. Four more followed at equal distances, too low to hear. Amastan put his fingers to the rock while Sarah counted. After the fifth he ordered us down. We covered the metal fittings, gave the animals the final water and went forward on foot.\par

\ornament

We reached the camp overlook in the last dark of the fourth night. Amastan's companions took the animals into a high cleft while he brought us up through a dry wash and stopped us below its rim. He pressed a palm to the ground.\par

``From there onward, their instruments can hear a stone roll.''\par

He took Jean-Charles forward on his belly. They returned together, and Jean-Charles pointed between two listening posts.\par

``That is the gap? Show me the route down.''\par

``I take you through the first turns,'' Amastan said. ``At the last bend you go alone. If I am seen, the road dies with me.''\par

Jean-Charles removed his coat, covered the pale skin of his hands and face, and tightened the cloth at his collar. He saw me watching.\par

``You will want an account when I get back,'' he said.\par

``Including where anything you bring us came from. The safe-house questions remain unresolved.''\par

He pulled the cloth across his mouth. ``Sarah heard them. She has given me this ground.''\par

``She has. I am depending on you to bring us through it.''\par

``Then keep the machine dry.''\par

Amastan led him over the rim and returned alone. I heard nothing from Jean-Charles before the light came.\par

Grey light entered the pan while we lay against cold rock. Dishes, tents and parked vehicles separated from the ground. Beyond them stood the drilling rig. Two pale flecks crossed the sky without sound. Sarah watched them, checked her watch and gave Amastan the next times she expected overhead coverage. A dish beside the command tents turned slowly and stopped. I kept my face below the rim.\par

The men I could see on the ridges faced inward. Tripods stood among them, pointed into the rock. Red survey stakes crossed the pan in straight lines. Sarah named a western truck's position as the northern mouth of an old drovers' track to the Oasis. Shield had allowed it to pass out of use, she said; no Outsider was supposed to know where that mouth lay.\par

``They were told,'' Kailan said.\par

``I think so.'' Sarah pointed below the rig. ``West of your Hall. What lies deeper along that line?''\par

``The reserve.''\par

``Water?''\par

He nodded.\par

A double ring of tents occupied the camp's centre. Generator housings stood beside it, and men were loading stretchers into long vehicles. Sarah passed me the glass. Through it I watched an orderly carry a folded blanket past the inner cordon. Armed men at its entrance kept a truck waiting while its driver showed a paper. I handed the glass back.\par

Sarah kept her eye to the glass. ``A good half of what you see was written in my hand.'' After a while she lowered it.\par

``I wanted a way to hold one of their Keepers,'' she said. ``The men below have allowed for more.'' She rested the glass on the rock and rubbed the pressed skin around her eye. ``I paid people to prepare that work. I knew they would have to use it on a person.''\par

We waited through the daylight. Amastan pulled cloth over the hollow behind our ledge and weighted it without raising an edge above the rock. Sarah lay beneath it. Whenever he brought her water, she asked whether Jean-Charles had returned. He shook his head and let her drink. I kept the recorder in the shade and my notebook against my chest, where the wind could not turn its pages.\par

The camp changed watches. A line formed outside the cooking tents and broke apart; vehicles moved between the rig and stacked lengths of pipe. Heat shook the space above them until I could no longer keep a man's outline in the glass. At intervals Amastan went to the wash and returned by another fold. When the shadow reached our ledge again, Sarah sat up without help.\par

We heard no shot. Late in the day, Jean-Charles appeared in the wash with a man bent in front of him. Amastan went down to meet them, placing their steps as they climbed. The prisoner wore the camp's grey clothes. His wrists were bound; a gag held his mouth. Jean-Charles carried a flat hard case and a black field radio.\par

``Post nine,'' he said. ``The seam is open beyond it. Not for long.''\par

He put the case on the rock. Sarah reached for a catch, and I asked her to wait while I took its receipt. She checked the camp through the glass.\par

``Briefly, Paul.''\par

I gave the hour, the place as far as I could describe it, and the condition of both catches. Jean-Charles named the command tent and a locked drawer. I asked whether anyone else had handled it after he took it. He said no. I put his answer beside his name. I had seen him come up the wash carrying it; his account supplied the earlier custody.\par

Sarah opened the catches. One stock of paper filled the case, punched in one sequence, with consecutive issue marks. A correction on one sheet had impressed the sheet beneath. I compared another pair under the glass and found the same fit. I stopped the recorder to read, intending to restart it when Sarah began translating. In the next four minutes I failed to do so.\par

She found a small mark in the first sheet's upper corner.\par

``French strategic service,'' she said. ``A compartment descended from the arrangements Martinez and my father made in this desert. The mineral company is a cover. This is a state operation.''\par

Sarah translated the next leaves. They carried a joint Algerian groundwater and rare-earth concession; she checked the number, drilling permits and customs entries and judged them genuine. The distribution table allocated fuel, airspace and explosives approvals to separate offices and survey blocks. She followed each distribution column with her nail. The central coordinates appeared in none of the Algerian recipients' copies listed there. A personnel annex counted three thousand and eighty-four people across nine contractors: drilling, transport, aviation, medicine, camp labour and security. It listed three hundred and eighty-six French service numbers. I checked the addition of its subtotals.\par

She read the operational headings and working schedule: seismic sounding, bore depths, dates, capture medicine, water control, approach denial, compartmented command. She set her watch beside the drilling entry. ``The industrial work is real. That is why the cover survives inspection. These central instructions concern a mechanism they mean to take intact. They are using my grid and my methods to reach it.''\par

Three leaves into the logistics annex, Sarah stopped at a northern track among forty routes. She read its local name aloud. It was the name she had used on the ridge. She translated the entry beside it: \emph{source: internal, reliability high}.\par

``Who had that corridor after Martinez was shut out?'' I asked.\par

She kept her eyes on the page. ``Jean-Charles handled that channel.''\par

I looked for a name in the entry and asked her whether it supplied one.\par

``No. Whoever wrote this called the source internal. We do not know whose inside they meant.'' She folded the page once. ``Keep it with the questions he asked Kailan. Keep his name off the entry itself.''\par

She put the page inside her coat. Jean-Charles stood beside the prisoner, looking down the wash. I checked the recorder, found it stopped and entered the four-minute gap. Sarah repeated the track's name and the source entry into it. I described the folds she had made and said that she held the page.\par

The field radio lit. A young voice spoke French. Jean-Charles covered the set until the light died. Sarah translated for Kailan and me: post nine had missed its check; someone called Farid was threatened with the sergeant if he had gone behind a rock to sleep.\par

Amastan cut the prisoner's gag and gave him water. He answered to Farid. Sarah asked the questions in French and gave us his replies, then translated Kailan's questions back. Farid said he guarded a mineral survey. He knew his watch and the two adjacent posts. He had been told that ``primitives'' lived underground and were to be brought out \emph{without loss of life}. Sappers were to preserve what they found intact.\par

Kailan crouched before him. ``Who gave the order to take no life?''\par

``A medical officer. I do not know his name.''\par

``The same officer who sets the charges?''\par

``No.''\par

``Who can stop the digging?''\par

``Operations. Not our command.''\par

``Who among you may speak to the men on the western ridge?''\par

Farid looked toward it. ``No one at my post. They have their own command.''\par

Kailan asked Sarah for the live-capture orders. She found the sheet and translated five priorities, coded by task. She read no person's name. Kailan repeated three in our language: the one who reads the deep water; the one who keeps the hours; the one who holds the seal. Sarah pointed to the other two entries and gave him their wording again.\par

``Five functions,'' he said. ``They have understood that the mechanism is worked by five hands. They mean to seize the complete set alive and force it to answer to a new hand.''\par

Sarah read the page over his shoulder. ``Can they?''\par

``The division was built so that no one could. They are coming to commit the one seizure it exists to prevent.''\par

Sarah put the codes to Farid. He shook his head at each and asked her what we were saying. When she went on with Kailan, he looked from one face to the next.\par

The radio spoke again. Sarah identified the sergeant's reply. Lights came on at two posts.\par

``The road is open,'' Jean-Charles said. ``Amastan knows a seam beyond post nine their instruments do not yet cover. Farid's missed check has shortened our hour to minutes.''\par

One of Amastan's companions bound Farid's hands in front and led him into the high cleft. ``We will keep him until your road is shut,'' Amastan said. ``After that his road is his.''\par

Jean-Charles gave Sarah the case and me the field radio. ``We go now, or we do not go.''\par

We left the animals, blankets and empty water skins in the high cleft. I put the reel tins inside my coat and bound the recorder against my chest with its carrying strap. Sarah refused to leave the station book until Kailan showed her there was no way to crawl with it and the hard case. She tore out the crossed page of call signs and put it beside the annex inside her sleeve. The book remained under a stone.\par

Amastan returned from Farid's cleft and led us to the rim. Three lights moved along the wash Jean-Charles had climbed; another swept the ridge above post nine. Men called to one another. The drill's regular iron turning covered their voices, then stopped without warning. Amastan closed his hand. We held still until the turning began again.\par

At the first turn Amastan placed our feet in his own marks. At the second he took Sarah's weight across a slab that sounded hollow when my knee touched it. At the third he pointed to a black cut no wider than a doorway.\par

``That cut reaches the old seam,'' he said. ``My name goes no farther.''\par

Sarah gripped his wrist. ``Close the northern well.''\par

``It was closed before you came.''\par

``The other two?''\par

``My brother takes one. My sister takes the other.''\par

He looked at Kailan. ``Tell Shield the roads outside the Mountain are held by those who walk them. If they forget again, no wall will teach them.''\par

Kailan repeated the words. Amastan made him repeat them a second time, then withdrew over our path, lifting the marks with a brush of hide.\par

Jean-Charles entered the cut first. Kailan guided Sarah behind him; I followed. We moved on elbows where the roof came down. The radio against my ribs gave a burst of French through its cloth. We stopped. Jean-Charles reached back and turned a control until the voice vanished. Sarah whispered the order she had heard: posts eight through twelve were to close the western arc and find Farid.\par

``Did you answer?'' Sarah whispered.\par

``No.''\par

``Could you have?''\par

``I know the language of the post. I do not know the man's alarm word.''\par

He passed it back to me. Sarah looked at the hand that released it, then crawled on.\par

The cut opened above a slope of broken shale. Across it stood post nine: two men, a low tent and a tripod turning by degrees. Beyond the post, the ground met the Mountain in an unbroken face. I could see no seam. A white lamp moved along the base of the rock, withdrew and returned.\par

Jean-Charles counted the tripod's turns with one finger. On the fifth he took us across. For twelve paces the shale shifted under every knee. We placed each stone in the space the hand ahead had left. Sarah's breath became audible. Kailan stopped and drew her arm across his shoulders. The tripod turned away. We moved again.\par

A voice challenged from the post. Jean-Charles put us flat and answered in French. I have no French, and he was not loud. The voice asked a second question. He gave a shorter reply. A man stepped beyond the tent and raised his lamp toward us. At that moment the drilling rig began again. Jean-Charles pulled Sarah to her feet and ran bent beneath her arm. Kailan took her other side. I went after them with the recorder striking my ribs. The lamp crossed the shale behind us, found the place where we had lain and passed over the moving stones.\par

Jean-Charles reached into a shadow beneath the rock face. His hand disappeared to the wrist. Kailan knelt beside him and cleared gravel from a narrow bronze plate with five shallow cups. Jean-Charles guided his fingers: the outer two, the centre, the outer two again. Something clicked behind the stone. The face remained shut.\par

``Again?'' Sarah whispered.\par

``They must open from inside,'' Jean-Charles said. ``Keep the centre held.''\par

Kailan kept his hand on the plate. Jean-Charles stood between Sarah and the lamp crossing the shale. It withdrew, returned, and stopped near the place where we had lain. My knee slid on gravel. I caught the rock with my cut hand and held myself against it.\par

A line opened beside Jean-Charles's shoulder. Cold air reached us. Hands came through the dark and took Sarah's arms. Kailan passed the case after her; Jean-Charles put his palm between my shoulders and drove me through.\par

The white lamp found the bronze plate as he entered. A shot struck the rock above the opening. The hands inside pulled him clear; the seam closed on a fall of dust, leaving the shot and the camp outside. No weapon answered from within.\par

For several breaths there was only our breathing and the recorder turning under my coat. Then a lamp was uncovered.\par

Va-Sheva stood beside a lamp on the floor of a low chamber, with two wardens behind her. She wore Shield grey, belted above the curve of her belly. She was with child. Her eyes moved from Kailan's face to Jean-Charles, then over Sarah and me, the case, the radio and the recorder. She sent one warden to the seam and the other to the inner door.\par

The wardens searched us in the order we had entered. Jean-Charles laid down a pistol, two knives, a length of black cord and three objects I could not name. Sarah showed them the folded papers and kept the pages in sight. Kailan carried no weapon. When a warden reached for the recorder, I asked him to state his name and office before he broke its custody. Va-Sheva stopped his hand.\par

``It stays closed,'' she said. ``Shield will hear what it contains with Justice present, or destroy it with the man who carried it.''\par

The warden wrapped the field radio and hard case separately and set both inside a stone recess without opening them. Sarah kept the folded pages. Blood from the shale had soaked through one knee of her trousers. Va-Sheva pointed to it.\par

``After the hearing,'' Sarah said.\par

``Bind it while she speaks,'' Va-Sheva told the warden. ``If she falls, carry her. Do not stop her answer.''\par

``You were sent to find whether the enemy within was real,'' she told Kailan. ``You were to report through me. You have brought two Outsiders before the five could hear them, and a machine carrying our speech, through a seam no Veil is shown. Your errand did not authorise any of that. By the law I hold, I should kill you here and close the stone over all four.''\par

Kailan looked at the warden by the inner door. ``Then hear the finding before you carry out the law. You are the ear they gave me.''\par

``I am listening.''\par

``The men outside mean to take the five alive. Sarah has their orders. The bore is aimed below the Hall. She can give you its depth and their working schedule.''\par

Sarah held out the folded papers. ``My methods brought them this far. I can show Shield what they are doing with them.''\par

Va-Sheva faced Sarah. ``How deep?''\par

Sarah gave her the measure. Va-Sheva asked the angle, the number of rigs and the interval between the five strokes. Sarah answered each.\par

Va-Sheva turned to Jean-Charles. ``You brought them through the outer posts?''\par

``Yes. They have challenged us. The seam was seen.''\par

She faced Kailan again. ``I know his work. I have to examine hers.'' She sent the inner warden for Shield and Justice, then pointed to my recorder. ``That remains in your hand, Outsider. If it opens, you die before the lid clears.''\par

``Understood,'' I said.\par

``Under necessity of Shield, I admit the four of you as far as the sealed floor and no farther. Justice will decide whether the five hear you. The seam you used is dead from this hour. I answer for the admission.''\par

The warden drove a bronze pin into the five-cup plate. Three descending blows sounded inside the wall. Va-Sheva waited for the third before lifting the lamp. Jean-Charles took Sarah's uninjured side, Kailan the other. I followed them towards the inner door.\par

At each crossing Va-Sheva stopped beside a warden. One went downward after hearing her; another ran ahead. The field radio and hard case stayed behind under guard, their wrappings unbroken when I lost sight of them. Sarah kept the folded leaves. Va-Sheva asked her questions as we climbed.\par

``How many bores?''\par

``Three active. One deep. Two shallow.''\par

``How much time?''\par

``The case gives a date for reaching the Hall. Have it brought to Justice. I cannot certify how closely they have kept the schedule.''\par

``Can you stop it from this side?''\par

``Not by meeting the bit. You may collapse a throat ahead of it, if you know the rock and can lose the chambers beyond. You may foul a water bore, but then you lose the water. You may let them enter a false void and close it behind them. None is a clean answer.''\par

Va-Sheva sent a warden for the keepers of the north-western galleries and another for the oldest survey kept by Stone. She asked whether the deep reserve shared a wall with the lower wards. Kailan could place the reserve broadly; Sarah could add nothing from her restricted visit. At the next crossing a warden brought a black marking stone. Sarah drew the ridge, rig and angle on the passage wall. Kailan placed the Hall, then held the stone above the depth he could not fix. Va-Sheva studied the marks, sent another message to Stone and wiped the drawing away with her sleeve.\par

``Justice will require the order of it,'' she told Kailan. ``Your errand. What you found. What she can support. I shall give the five Shield's reason for admitting you. I cannot give them yours.''\par

``I will give it.''\par

``Keep the papers where Justice can see them, Sarah. And keep walking.''\par

Where the stair turned, Sarah and Jean-Charles had gone several steps ahead. Va-Sheva lowered her voice. The recorder remained against my ribs.\par

``Elise wrote of eleven days without speech at the house. Is it true?''\par

``Yes.''\par

``Could you drink?''\par

``When she put the cup in my hands.''\par

Va-Sheva stopped one step above him. The lamp lit the dust on his mouth.\par

``Can you speak to them now?''\par

``I have come to.''\par

``Kay. I need an answer.''\par

``Yes.''\par

She held the lamp towards him until he moved. ``Stay beside me at Justice's door. Do not enter before they call you.''\par

Kailan climbed after her. His hand hovered near her elbow at the next turn, then returned to the wall.\par

The route crossed an open stair above the common wards. Below it, evening went on. Lamps burned over food stalls. A woman walked twelve children in pairs; the smallest held the back of the child's tunic ahead of him and dragged one sandal. A seller weighed roots in a hanging pan while two men disputed his weights. Someone beat dust from a mat over a rail and earned a shout from the level beneath. Water ran in a channel cut through the centre of the floor.\par

No one looked upward at first. The stair ran against the wall above their lamps. Beneath me a pot was being mended with wire. I could see the holes bored on either side of its split and the man's thumb pressing the ends flat. I had measured such repairs for Sarah, under a glass, without finding the hand that made them. I slowed at the rail. A woman folded a stone blade into a cloth and passed it to a boy with a scraped palm. Farther along, a man pressed his thumb into rounds of dough, turned each one and pressed again.\par

The evening bread reached us before we crossed the centre of the stair. It smelled of yeast, warm grain and the scorched flour at the edge of an oven. I knew charred cakes from sites nine thousand years old. I knew the pits in which the grain had been stored, the querns on which it had been broken and the changes in human bone after people began to eat it every day. Below me, a baker struck flour from his hands and called to a child to carry six loaves home.\par

The recorder knocked against the rail. Several faces lifted. Va-Sheva caught my sleeve before it struck again.\par

``They do not know,'' she said. ``They will not learn it from you on a stair. Walk.''\par

The child with the six loaves had stopped beneath us. One loaf slipped against his chest; he trapped it with his chin and laughed. I put the machine under my coat and walked. At the upper turn the stalls disappeared. The smell remained for twelve steps more.\par

\ornament

The first blow took the stair sideways.\par

Stone struck my shoulder. The recorder drove into my ribs beneath my coat. A lamp went out below; another rolled in a yellow circle until a hand caught it. Then the sound arrived through the floor: one flat concussion, a single force driven into the Mountain without the patient interval of the five-stroke sounding. Dust fell from the joints overhead. Far inside the rock, worked stone ground against worked stone.\par

Sarah put both hands on the wall. ``A charge. Down a shallow bore, close enough to break a void or test what breaks around it. North-west, near the surface. What lies there?''\par

Va-Sheva turned towards the sound and opened her mouth. No order came.\par

``What lies there?'' Sarah repeated.\par

``Faith,'' Va-Sheva said. ``A Faith passage. The road to the Hall.''\par

She ran. Jean-Charles went with her and the nearest warden. Sarah called for a lamp, a plan of the north-western arc and the keeper of Stone. The ward children below had begun to cry. The seller's hanging scale knocked against its post with each movement underfoot.\par

Kailan put his palm to the wall. I put mine beside it. A tremor travelled through the stone, stopped, then returned deeper and slower. Grit moved under my fingers. From beyond the turn came a sharper failure and Va-Sheva shouting for the passage to be cleared.\par

I lifted the recorder toward the grinding. Its reels turned. Kailan took the lamp left on the stair. We ran after the others.\par
