\textit{In Noxius}

(Paul): --- I need your account of the hearing. The sealed chamber, the five, the ruling. Will you begin with where you stood?

(Kailan): --- No. Put the thing away. It fell three days since. It is falling still, Paul, on people who never heard our names. Who is left above to read how carefully we decided?

(Paul): --- I cannot answer that. I can keep the accounts together. If we leave it, someone will eventually supply the parts we refuse to give.

(Kailan): --- You were in the room. Your machine ran the whole while. Ask the Keepers. Ask Sarah, ask Va-Sheva --- they spoke. I stood at the wall with my hands bound. What do you need from me that is not already turning in that thing?

(Paul): --- What you could see from the wall. I was watching the table. Va-Sheva refuses me; Sarah turns away; the Keepers are turning air and counting fish. I have asked them. I am asking you now.

(Kailan): --- I have a shift on the vents. Before it, I need to lie still. There is nothing in me to give you.

\centerline{[\,---\,]}

(Paul): --- Then leave the hearing. The road from Blackwood. Could you give me that?

(Kailan): --- \ldots{} You kept the record. Give them the road yourself. I will take it up inside the Mountain. Paul. Let me rest.

\begin{center}\small\itshape Account of Paul Morgan\end{center}

My training is in strata, middens, worked flint, bone laid down in silt forty thousand years ago. I had worked in deserts. I had never been responsible for a record made while the people in it were under pursuit. The dates and intervals here come from the reel boxes and my notebooks. Where the machine was stopped, I have used my own memory.\par

At dusk the rotor drowned the voices on the lawn. Behind us the house stood lit at every window. Doors I had never seen opened onto the halls; men I had never met carried cases through them. Va-Elise came down the steps with papers under one arm, stopped a crewman and sent him back to the aircraft. Sarah called to her from the door. I carried the machine and a coat and received one order: keep up.\par

The travelling tape reached its clear leader while Kailan was being taken across the grass. I stopped it, wrote the hour on the box and sealed the reel in its tin. Va-Elise placed it in the fire case beside the fixed instruction reels and the one route packet. She gave me a fresh spool. A crewman loaded Sarah's chair and helped her aboard.\par

At the house steps a man tried to give Sarah three files. She chose one and sent the other two back. Another asked what should become of the instruction rooms.\par

``Lock them. The instruction reels stay in Elise's fire case. Nothing else leaves except Mr Morgan's sealed interview reels and notebooks.''\par

``The telephones?''\par

``Let them ring.''\par

That was the last order I heard given inside the Blackwood Estate. The front door remained open as we lifted. A sheet of paper crossed the lawn, rose into the rotor wind and vanished over the roof.\par

The helicopter lifted from the lawn in a lurch that put my stomach through my boots. For two years I had walked across the landing marks without seeing them. Sarah, ninety-three, spent the flight on a headset, making clipped calls in three languages. Her right thumb shook against the mouthpiece until she closed it beneath her left hand. A man's voice asked why the private aircraft was still on the ground. She told him how many minutes he had to correct that.\par

The headset passed once to Va-Elise, who confirmed fuel at one strip, vehicles at another and no names at either. Sarah took it back and called the outer stations. I could hear her side of the calls. She gave two instructions and received answers too faint for me to distinguish. At the third number she repeated the call, listened through twenty turns of the rotor and wrote a small cross. Her finger moved to the fourth number. The third remained uncovered beneath it.\par

Kailan sat opposite me. I kept the machine between my boots with its carrying strap around my wrist. Each time the helicopter banked, I drew it back against my ankles. I put on the recorder's headset and heard our breath under the engine, Sarah's numbers, and the fire case's buckle tapping against Va-Elise's wrist.\par

The helicopter put us down at Geneva beside the private aircraft. We waited in an old freight hangar. When the clearance lamp turned green, Urs remained behind the red line and we boarded. I had flown to digs in the cargo netting of transports older than myself and had never entered such a cabin. There were beds and a shower. Food arrived through a door I failed to notice, and I ate all of it. Kailan touched nothing. He watched the window. Sarah ate half a piece of toast and slept upright. Once she opened her eyes. ``Finish your meal, Mr Morgan. I do not know when any of us will eat properly again.'' Then she shut them again.\par

Va-Elise worked at the table with three maps that did not join. On the first she marked the aircraft's descent. On the second she marked the road the vehicles were to take. The third began where the road failed and contained no destination, only wells and strips of stone. I asked which of the wells still answered to Horizon. Va-Elise put her finger beside one.\par

``We meet Amastan here. He tells us which wells we can use.''\par

``How long do the drivers wait for him?'' I asked.\par

Va-Elise looked across at Sarah. ``They need a limit.''\par

Sarah opened her eyes. ``Keep them under cover. Nobody goes looking. If Amastan cannot reach that well, the road has gone.''\par

Va-Elise put a mark beside the well and folded the maps separately. I lay down. Kailan was already asleep. We flew through the night; I woke as we began our descent. Va-Elise kept the maps until the drivers met us.\par

\ornament

At dawn we came down onto a strip of old colonial tarmac, swept and lit; from the timing of Sarah's calls, I judged the work had been done while we were in the air. There was no building, only a windsock, two fuel drums and lamps set in jars along the broken edge. Three black armoured vehicles waited with their engines turning. Jean-Charles came from the middle vehicle to meet us. Kailan spoke his name and raised a hand. Va-Elise walked the line once and made each driver repeat one road and one stopping place; none was asked to name the destination.\par

Men in work clothes unloaded water, cloth, medical cases and sacks of feed. Weapons came last. Jean-Charles opened the long case across the quiet man's knees, inspected what lay inside and closed it without removing anything.\par

Sarah refused the chair. She descended the aircraft steps with Kailan one pace below her and Va-Elise behind, and stood at the bottom until her breathing settled. Then she asked the drivers whether anyone had approached the strip. One had seen lights beyond the pan. Another had heard an engine and no aircraft. She assigned the first report to the lead vehicle and the second to the rear.\par

Va-Elise took the lead. Jean-Charles rode beside our driver in the middle vehicle; Sarah, Kailan and I were strapped behind him, with the quiet man and his cased weapon in the rear seat. The third held our tail.\par

The sun cleared the horizon as we left the tarmac. Jean-Charles used no map. At each fork he waited for Va-Elise's rear lamp to show once and disappear. The driver spoke only to repeat a distance. Sarah held the station book closed on her knees. Kailan held the fire case between his boots after Va-Elise passed it through the window.\par

They ran a straight strip of tar across the pan at a speed that shook speech out of us. A knot of vehicles appeared far to the east. Va-Elise's rear lamps showed. Jean-Charles turned in his seat; behind me a catch opened and the long case touched my elbow. The distant vehicles crossed behind us. No one fired. Jean-Charles watched them diminish in his mirror, then gave the driver two fingers forward.\par

At the next rise the lead vehicle left the road without warning. We followed it down a dry drainage cut, the armour striking stone under my feet. The third vehicle continued along the tar. Through the rear slit I watched its dust pass the rise and disappear. Jean-Charles held one hand against the roof. Our driver stopped. An engine sounded above us, dwindled, then another followed it. We stayed in the cut until our rear vehicle appeared behind us. Jean-Charles lowered his hand and Va-Elise moved again.\par

Beyond the pan the tar ended. The desert changed from open pan to broken ground, and our speed fell from violence to a steady grinding walk. The drivers traded places without stopping the engines. The quiet man slept for forty minutes with one hand through the handle of the long case, woke at the time named to him and took the forward seat. No one asked who had followed the decoy. No vehicles followed.\par

\ornament

They drove on through the morning along a track a wheel could barely hold. Near its end stood one of Sarah's stations.\par

On the aircraft she had described them: small buried rooms, two or three people, a drum, a border and a fuel gauge. This one had three cups beside its kettle.\par

The door stood open to the desert. Sarah said no door of hers had stood that way in sixty years. Cool air still came from the wall; water ran from the tap; the coffee had gone cold. The instruments continued their work. On the paper feeding from the nearest drum, five strokes arrived through the ground beneath us. Blood had dried on the floor and the table lip. No bodies remained. A message stopped inside a word, its pencil in the page gutter. In one corner a box of records had burned badly: black edges, brown centres, dates still readable. I numbered each object aloud for the machine.\par

The station had been built for three habits: three hooks beside the inner door, three names on the duty board, three enamel cups marked differently at the handle. One chair had fallen on its side. A second had been pushed hard enough into the table to split its back rail. The third remained before the drum, its seat holding a dark print from a wet hand.\par

Va-Elise went outside and circled the entrance. Jean-Charles checked the rear room, the generator recess and the duct wide enough for an arm but not a body. Kailan stood before the five marks coming off the drum. He did not touch the paper. The strokes were evenly spaced and identical in their rise. When the fifth ended, the stylus resumed the thin irregular work of the ground. Sarah watched the blank interval and checked her wristwatch.\par

``Again in forty minutes,'' she said. ``Elise, put down the time. These are the same five strokes. Same height, same spacing.'' She held her watch beside the moving paper. ``They are sounding on a programme. We leave before they finish the next set.''\par

The record box's lid had closed across the files. Paper projected from its seam. The outer leaves broke into black scales when I raised them; underneath, the sheets remained the colour of old tea. I lifted them in their order and spoke their dates. Fuel. Water. Watch changes. A delivery entered four weeks before and cancelled in another hand. The corner crumbled between my forceps and left a black crescent on the table.\par

The unfinished message was written on the back of a stores request. Its first lines reported the five-stroke signal. The last legible words were \emph{door opened with}. The next letter was a single downward stroke. The pencil had rolled into the fold and remained there. Blood lay beneath the writer's side of the page but not across the words.\par

Va-Elise returned with a length of pale thread and her small camera. She took Sarah and me to the doorway. In the dust beside it were lighter boot marks crossed by heavier tread. She pointed out two tracks going away and a scuff which continued in two parallel heel cuts. ``The tyres are beyond the rise,'' she said. ``I photographed them. You cannot see that ground through the slit.''\par

Sarah went back to the table. ``Taken. At least two could walk. This one was dragged.'' She put her hand beside the burned box and held it there. ``They tried to destroy the records. They knew what would be asked of them.''\par

``Their routes?'' Va-Elise asked.\par

``Their own ground. Their own line to the house. They cannot give the other stations. They can give my notation.'' Sarah lifted her hand. ``Mr Morgan. The duty board, please. All three names.''\par

She read the three names from the duty board into the recorder. After each name she gave an office, years of service and the person to whom news should be carried if there remained anyone able to carry it. On the third she had to look back at the board before she could give the years.\par

Va-Elise found the station's route drawer unlocked and empty. Sarah checked the door herself. No wood was split, no hinge forced, no metal scored around the lock. She closed it and kept the key in her palm.\par

Va-Elise tried the latch. ``No force here.''\par

``It was supposed to be locked,'' Sarah said.\par

``They knew to set it.''\par

Sarah turned the key over in her palm, then opened her own station book. She drew the six call signs toward her and crossed each with the burned pencil. ``You go back with the vehicles. Close these six posts. Send each its own order. No transmission through the old net, no resupply on a listed road. Move the people. Break the instruments they cannot carry. That authority is yours now.''\par

Va-Elise read the list. ``Blackwood?''\par

``Blackwood is an address they know. It is finished.''\par

``And if one of the six has already opened?''\par

Sarah put the station key into her palm. ``Then bring out whom you can. Burn the rest correctly.''\par

Va-Elise closed her fingers over it and looked at Sarah. Then she put the list inside her sleeve and went to prepare the return.\par

I held the machine low while Sarah repeated the missing people's names for Va-Elise. The generator ticked beneath her voice. Wind came through the open door, lifting the edge of the unfinished message against its pencil.\par

The next five strokes began while we were carrying the water out. All of us stopped. Dust moved in the split of the table. The kettle gave a small metallic answer. Sarah counted the interval aloud, then told us to keep moving.\par

\ornament

The vehicles stopped where the track entered folded rock. A young man waited there with two others and seven animals, his face bound against the glare. Sarah called him Amastan.\par

He did not greet her until he had counted us and our loads. He rejected two water tins because their caps rang against the handles, wrapped the recorder's metal catches in felt and divided the documents from the food. The two people with him repacked each animal by touch in the shade of the folds. Only when nothing metal could strike metal did he uncover his face.\par

``My grandfather carried that name for you,'' he said. ``He has been thirty years in the ground. I carry it now.'' He drew the last strap through its ring. ``Your officers put that name in their treaties before my grandfather was born. They spelled it wrong.''\par

Va-Elise opened a map on the bonnet of the lead vehicle. Amastan looked once, drew a different line in the dust with the butt of his switch and put three stones across the route she had marked.\par

``That road was safe when my father held the name. It has tyre cuts now. Men watched it two nights ago.'' He moved the line west through a blank place on her map. ``We go here.''\par

Jean-Charles crouched beside the drawing. Amastan tapped each turn with his switch and made him repeat it. At the third, Jean-Charles pointed farther north. Amastan tapped the same place again. Jean-Charles named it. Amastan erased the whole with his foot.\par

Va-Elise put the tactical packet into Jean-Charles's hand. Sarah had kept it closed until this handover. ``The station approach and two abort points,'' Va-Elise said. ``No onward route.'' She pointed to Amastan, who had begun checking the animal loads. ``From here, ask him.''\par

Before she left, Va-Elise opened the fire case, transferred the reel tins into a felt wrap and put it into my hands. The route papers stayed with her. ``One copy. No house behind us. Do not let the desert have it.''\par

Jean-Charles folded his packet while Va-Elise returned to the vehicles. She gave him no paper from the fire case. At the first open ground the vehicles divided: one north, one east, one back toward the old tarmac. Amastan stood beside Sarah's animal until the three dust lines had thinned beyond sight. Then he shifted her saddle one hole and gave the word to move.\par

We travelled by night and lay under ground-coloured cloth by day. Amastan chose the hours, the wells and the order of drinking. The animals drank before us.\par

The first night was stone underfoot and a wind that filled the recorder's catches with grit. Amastan walked ahead of the first animal without a light. One of his companions walked last and erased the places where a hoof slipped. Jean-Charles twice offered a bearing. Amastan heard him and continued on his own.\par

By the second night I could tell a halt from a concealment. At a halt, the animals lowered their heads and someone checked the loads. At concealment, Amastan raised one closed hand and no buckle moved. Once we remained that way while an engine passed beyond the ridge. Its sound came first through the soles of my boots and only later through the air. I held the recorder against my coat to stop its fittings from clicking.\par

We reached a well before dawn, a crack at the back of a cleft. Amastan removed six fitted slabs, drew water with a skin narrow enough to pass between them, watered the animals and replaced the slabs in reverse order. His companion brushed the rock with a branch. When they had finished, I could not find the opening from three paces away.\par

``Does the Mountain keep this well?'' I asked.\par

``My people keep it.''\par

``For the Mountain?''\par

He coiled the line. ``For those who arrive thirsty and do not arrive with guns.''\par

Sarah rode in a sling they fitted around the saddle. At ninety-three she could still mount on the first night. By the third, Amastan and Kailan had to lift her. She allowed it without speaking. At noon her hand shook against the rim of her cup. I held the cup while she drank. When it was empty she said, ``Mr Morgan, if that appears in your account, at least spell my name correctly.''\par

``I always do.''\par

``One useful quality.''\par

Amastan shortened each passage. He moved the longest climb into the coldest hour and put Kailan at the left side of Sarah's animal, where the ground fell away. Sarah caught the guide's sleeve before he could go ahead.\par

``We have already lost half a day.''\par

He looked down at her hand. ``You could not lift your foot into the sling.''\par

``I have crossed this country since before your father was born.''\par

``Then let him hold you on the climb.'' Amastan waited. Sarah released his sleeve and put her hand on the saddle.\par

We left at the hour he had chosen. On the third night he stopped at a cairn and counted its stones with his switch. ``One has been added,'' he said. He left it standing and took us six hours around.\par

At the next halt Kailan called the folds the Mountain's outer wall, the last country no state had crossed. Amastan had the end of a load strap between his teeth. He drew it tight, fastened it and came round the animal to face Kailan.\par

``Whose men changed that cairn?'' he asked.\par

Kailan looked back along the fold. ``I do not know.''\par

``Nor do I. That is why we walked around.'' Amastan picked up his switch. ``Your Mountain may call this its wall. Ask it who keeps the wells.''\par

Kailan moved back to the left of Sarah's animal. I wrote the words about the wells inside my wrist.\par

The pencil wore away under my sleeve. I wrote the words again on the paper band around the fresh reel and put the reel into the machine. During the daylight hides I recorded only speech. At night I let the machine turn for a few minutes at each change of ground: soft hoof-fall on sand, iron striking buried stone, wind through a slit too narrow to see. I had counted my batteries at the folds. I numbered every interval I did not record.\par

On the fourth night the five strokes reached us without paper or instrument. At the first, Sarah's animal stiffened its forelegs and raised its head. I felt four more at equal distances, too low to hear and distinct through the ground. Amastan put his fingers to the rock. Sarah counted. When the last had passed, we covered the metal fittings, gave the animals the final water and went forward on foot.\par

\ornament

We reached the camp overlook in the last dark of the fourth night. Amastan's companions took the animals into a high cleft while he brought us up through a dry wash and stopped us below its rim. He pressed a palm to the ground.\par

``From there onward, their instruments can hear a stone roll.''\par

He took Jean-Charles forward on his belly and showed him a seam between two listening posts. When they returned, Jean-Charles asked for the route.\par

``I take you through the first turns,'' Amastan said. ``At the last bend you go alone. If I am seen, the road dies with me.''\par

Jean-Charles removed his coat, covered the pale skin of his hands and face, and tightened the cloth at his collar. He saw me watching.\par

``Still taking notes on me, professor?''\par

``Your questions at the safe house are on the record. So is your request that Kailan keep them from Elise.''\par

He pulled the cloth across his mouth. ``And Sarah heard him tell it. She still put the field movement in my hands.''\par

``That is on the record too.''\par

``Good. Keep the machine dry.''\par

Amastan led him over the rim and came back alone. ``He is going down now,'' he said. ``Before the light.''\par

We waited on cold rock while grey light entered the pan, exposing dishes, tents, vehicles and a drilling rig one degree at a time. Two flecks crossed the high sky without sound. Sarah named the times at which they would return. The dish beside the command tents moved after each passage and stopped on the same bearing.\par

Men occupied the ridges and the throats of the approaches, all facing inward, with tripods among them pointed into the rock. Survey stakes made straight red lines across ground on which no natural line ran straight. A western truck covered an old drovers' track to the Oasis. Sarah said Shield had allowed the track to pass out of use because no Outsider had ever found its northern mouth. The truck stood over it exactly.\par

``They were told,'' Kailan said.\par

``Yes.'' Sarah pointed below the rig. ``That bore is west of your Hall and deeper. What lies there?''\par

``The reserve.''\par

``Water?''\par

He nodded.\par

At the camp's centre stood a double ring of tents with separate generators. Long vehicles waited beside them, each fitted to carry a body flat. Men from the command tents crossed the open ground with covered papers and returned by different paths. No one except the medical orderlies passed the double ring.\par

Sarah kept her eye to the glass. ``A good half of what you see was written in my hand.'' After a while she lowered it.\par

``Some part of me wanted the Mountain found. I wanted not to be the last one holding it.'' She set the glass on the rock. ``This is what found looks like.''\par

We heard no shot. Before direct sun reached the wash, Jean-Charles appeared in it with a man bent in front of him. Amastan left cover, chose every step of their ascent and brought them through the listening ground. The prisoner wore the camp's grey clothes. His wrists were bound; a gag held his mouth. Jean-Charles carried a flat hard case and a black field radio.\par

``Post nine,'' he said. ``The seam is open beyond it. Not for long.''\par

He put the case on the rock between us. Sarah bent over the catches. I brought the recorder beside her hand.\par

``Before you open it. Where did you take it from, Jean-Charles?''\par

``A locked drawer in the command tent.''\par

Sarah kept her fingers on the catches. ``How long, Mr Morgan?''\par

``Long enough to identify him and the condition in which it reached us. He is one of the people whose access we are testing.''\par

She took her hand away. ``Do it.''\par

I stated the place and hour, described the catches and gave Jean-Charles's name. He repeated where he had found the case. I opened it beside the recorder, keeping the lid toward the camp so the paper would not show white above the rock.\par

The papers carried one stock, one sequence of punch holes and consecutive issue marks. Corrections on separate sheets had been made in the same ink and impressed the leaves beneath them. I kept the leaves in their order and turned the first sheet toward Sarah. She read the French aloud in English, stopping when I asked her to repeat a heading.\par

She found a small mark in its upper corner.\par

``French strategic service,'' she said. ``A compartment descended from the arrangements Martinez and my father made in this desert. The mineral company is a cover. This is a state operation.''\par

The next leaves carried a joint Algerian groundwater and rare-earth concession. Sarah judged the concession number, drilling permits and customs entries genuine. A distribution table sent fuel, airspace and explosives approvals through separate offices for separate survey blocks; none of the Algerian recipients received the central coordinates. A personnel annex counted three thousand and eighty-four people across nine contractors: drilling, transport, aviation, medicine, camp labour and security. Three hundred and eighty-six carried French service numbers.\par

She read the operational headings and put her finger against the rock toward each part of the camp: seismic sounding, capture medicine, water control, approach denial, compartmented command. At the last she went back to the distribution table. ``The drilling and the concessions will survive inspection. Each office can show its own permits. None needs to see this page.''\par

Kailan leaned across the case. ``What do they know of the mechanism?''\par

``That there is one. These are orders to reach it. My grid, my methods.'' She held the page at its lower corners. ``They have not written down what they expect it to do.''\par

Three leaves into the logistics annex, I found the local name Sarah had used for the northern track. It stood among forty routes. I asked her to read the entry beside it. In a quartermaster's hand: \emph{source: internal, reliability high}.\par

I gave the page to Sarah and touched the line.\par

``Who held that corridor after Martinez was shut out?'' I asked.\par

Sarah kept her face over the page. ``One channel. One pair of hands.''\par

``It does not name him.''\par

``No.''\par

``And he brought us the case. He may not know the annex is here.''\par

She folded the page once. ``Serious. Not proof.''\par

She put it inside her sleeve. Jean-Charles had moved beside the prisoner, facing the wash. He had asked us no question about the papers. I stopped the machine while we read the remaining leaves. By my watch the gap was four minutes; the case lay open behind him throughout. I started the machine again before the radio lit.\par

The field radio gave a young French voice. Jean-Charles covered the set until its light died. Sarah whispered the message to us: post nine had missed its check. If Farid had crawled behind a rock to sleep again, he could answer to the sergeant.\par

Amastan cut the prisoner's gag and held water to his mouth. At the sound of Farid the man raised his head. Sarah spoke to him in French, then gave us his answers in the Mountain's language. He guarded a mineral survey. He knew his own watch and the two posts beside it. He had been told there were ``primitives'' underground, a lost tribe to be handled \emph{without loss of life}. The sappers were to bring everything out intact. He could name no person who had issued those instructions.\par

Kailan crouched where Farid could see his face. Sarah stayed beside them to translate.\par

``Who told you to take no life?'' Kailan asked.\par

``A medical officer. I do not know his name.''\par

``The same officer who sets the charges?''\par

``No.''\par

``Who can stop the digging?''\par

``Operations. Not our command.''\par

``Who among you may speak to the men on the western ridge?''\par

Farid looked toward it. ``No one at my post. They have their own command.''\par

Kailan drew the case toward his knees. Sarah found the live-capture orders and read the five priorities, each coded by task with the name omitted. He stopped her at three and repeated them in his own words: the one who reads the deep water; the one who keeps the hours; the one who holds the seal. Two more remained in codes I could not interpret.\par

``Read those to him,'' he said.\par

Sarah did. Farid shook his head at each. He tried to lean away from the case; his bound hands caught against his knee.\par

``Five functions,'' Kailan said to Sarah. ``They want all five alive. Put them together under guard and make them work it.''\par

``Can they?'' Sarah asked.\par

Kailan kept the page between his hands. ``That is what the division is meant to prevent.''\par

Farid looked from Kailan to Sarah. She had stopped translating.\par

The radio spoke again, in a lower voice. ``The sergeant,'' Sarah said. Lights came on at two posts.\par

``The road is open,'' Jean-Charles said. ``Amastan knows a seam beyond post nine their instruments do not yet cover. Farid's missed check has shortened our hour to minutes.''\par

One of Amastan's companions bound Farid's hands in front and led him into the high cleft. ``We will keep him until your road is shut,'' Amastan said. ``After that his road is his.''\par

Kailan put the page back and closed the case. Jean-Charles took its handle, put it into Sarah's hand and gave me the field radio. ``We go now, or we do not go.''\par

We left the animals, blankets and empty water skins in the high cleft. I put the reel tins inside my coat and bound the recorder against my chest with its carrying strap. Sarah refused to leave the station book until Kailan showed her there was no way to crawl with it and the hard case. She tore out the crossed page of call signs and put it beside the annex inside her sleeve. The book remained under a stone.\par

Amastan returned from Farid's cleft and led us to the rim. The camp had changed. Three lights moved along the wash Jean-Charles had used; another swept the ridge above post nine. Men called in pairs. The drilling continued beneath them, a regular iron turning that covered smaller sounds and then stopped without warning. Each time it stopped, we stopped with it.\par

At the first turn Amastan placed our feet in his own marks. At the second he took Sarah's weight across a slab that sounded hollow when my knee touched it. At the third he pointed to a black cut no wider than a doorway.\par

``That cut reaches the old seam,'' he said. ``My name goes no farther.''\par

Sarah gripped his wrist. ``Close the northern well.''\par

``It was closed before you came.''\par

``The other two?''\par

``My brother takes one. My sister takes the other.''\par

He looked at Kailan. ``Tell Shield the roads outside the Mountain are held by those who walk them. If they forget again, no wall will teach them.''\par

Kailan repeated the words. Amastan made him repeat them a second time, then withdrew over our path, lifting the marks with a brush of hide.\par

Jean-Charles entered the cut first, Sarah behind him, Kailan behind her, and I last. We moved on elbows where the roof came down. The radio against my ribs gave one burst of French through its cloth. Kailan's foot stopped beside my hand. Ahead, Sarah whispered, ``Eight through twelve. Close the western arc. Find Farid.''\par

I worked the set out of its wrapping and passed it to Kailan. He slid it along the rock beside Sarah; she pushed it into Jean-Charles's waiting hand. He turned a control and the voice vanished.\par

``Did you answer?'' Sarah whispered.\par

``No.''\par

``Could you have?''\par

``I know the language of the post. I do not know the man's alarm word.''\par

He set it on the rock for Sarah. She kept her hand beside his until he released it, then passed it back through Kailan. I wrapped it against my ribs again. We crawled on.\par

The cut opened above a slope of broken shale. Across it stood post nine: two men, a low tent and a tripod turning by degrees. Beyond the post, the ground met the Mountain in an unbroken face. I could see no seam. A white lamp moved along the base of the rock, withdrew and returned.\par

Jean-Charles counted the tripod's turns with one finger. On the fifth he took us across. For twelve paces the shale shifted under every knee. We placed each stone in the space the hand ahead had left. Sarah's breath became audible. Kailan stopped and drew her arm across his shoulders. The tripod turned away. We moved again.\par

A voice challenged from the post. Jean-Charles put us flat and answered in French. I have no French, and he was not loud. The voice asked a second question. He gave a shorter reply. A man stepped beyond the tent and raised his lamp toward us. At that moment the drilling rig began again. Jean-Charles pulled Sarah to her feet and ran bent beneath her arm. Kailan took her other side. I went after them with the recorder striking my ribs. When I looked back, the lamp crossed the place where we had lain. Small stones were still rolling down from it.\par

The rock face remained whole until Jean-Charles put his hand into a shadow below it. His arm disappeared to the wrist. Kailan knelt, cleared three handfuls of gravel and uncovered a narrow bronze plate with five shallow cups. He pressed the outer two, the centre, then the outer two again. Nothing moved.\par

Jean-Charles kept his shoulder at the stone. ``Nothing.''\par

``The return sign changes with the hour,'' Kailan said. He held his fingers above the cups.\par

Sarah drew her feet clear of his knees. ``You have room. Try.''\par

Kailan set the pattern a second time and held the centre cup. Jean-Charles leaned harder against the stone. A line opened without sound. Cold air came through it. Two hands reached from the dark. Sarah passed the case to Kailan and took the hands; they pulled her inside. He passed the case through and followed it. Jean-Charles caught my coat at the shoulder and pushed me after him.\par

The white lamp found the bronze plate as he entered. A shot struck the rock above the opening. The hands inside pulled him clear; the seam closed on a fall of dust, leaving the shot and the camp outside. No weapon answered from within.\par

For several breaths there was only our breathing and the recorder turning under my coat. Then a lamp was uncovered.\par

Va-Sheva stood beside the uncovered lamp in Shield grey. The chamber was low enough that Jean-Charles remained bent until he had stepped clear of the seam. Two wardens held us along one wall. Kailan gave Sarah back the case. Va-Sheva looked from it to the radio in my hands, then to the recorder under my coat. She sent one warden to the seam and kept the other between us and the inner door. She was with child; her belt lay above the swelling.\par

The inner warden searched us while the other held the seam. Sarah showed the folded pages in her sleeve and laid the case at her feet. Kailan carried no weapon. I put down the field radio and drew my coat clear of the recorder. The warden reached for its strap.\par

``Your name and office, please,'' I said. ``If you take it, I must record the transfer.''\par

Va-Sheva caught his wrist. He let the strap fall against my chest. He went on to Jean-Charles, who laid down a pistol, two knives, a length of black cord and three objects I could not name.\par

``It stays closed,'' she said. ``Shield will hear what it contains with Justice present, or destroy it with the man who carried it.''\par

The warden wrapped the field radio and hard case separately and set them in a stone recess without opening either. Sarah returned the folded pages to her sleeve. Blood from the shale had soaked through one knee of her trousers. Va-Sheva pointed to it.\par

``After the hearing,'' Sarah said.\par

``Bind it while she speaks,'' Va-Sheva told the warden. ``If she falls, carry her. Do not stop her answer.''\par

The warden knelt to bind Sarah's knee. Va-Sheva faced Kailan across the lamp.\par

``Shield's writ sent you out to learn whether the enemy within was real. The council sealed it. You return with two Outsiders we have never weighed and a machine full of our speech. You have used a seam no Veil is shown.'' She waited until he looked up. ``By the law I hold, I should kill you at this lamp, kill them beside you and seal the seam over all four.''\par

Sarah held her leg still under the warden's hands. ``One of those Outsiders made the field net. I did. I can identify what they have kept and where they are drilling.''\par

Va-Sheva kept her eyes on Kailan. ``You saw it?''\par

``The rig and trucks. The strokes reached us on the ridge.''\par

``How close?''\par

``The bore is below your Hall. Hear her measure.''\par

Va-Sheva faced Sarah. ``How deep?''\par

Sarah gave her the measure. Va-Sheva asked the angle, the number of rigs and the interval between the five strokes. Sarah answered each.\par

``Jean-Charles vouches for the breach,'' Va-Sheva said to Kailan.\par

``He does.''\par

Va-Sheva looked at Jean-Charles's empty hands. ``I trust your hands. I have worked beside them. You remain with us.'' When the inner warden had tied Sarah's bandage, she sent him to summon Shield and Justice, then pointed at my recorder. ``That remains on you, Outsider. If it opens, you die before the lid clears.''\par

``Understood,'' I said.\par

``Under necessity of Shield, I admit the four of you as far as the sealed floor and no farther. The seam you used is dead from this hour. If your warning is false, I answer for opening it. If it is true, we count every other answer after the Mountain is still standing.''\par

She gave the order. The warden at the seam drove a bronze pin into the five-cup plate. From inside the wall came three descending blows. The way by which we had entered ceased to be a way.\par

She turned back to Kailan. ``I sent you out alive. You have put yourself within my law again.''\par

Kailan bowed his head. Va-Sheva took the lamp. At the inner door she waited for him to move, then led us upward.\par

Wardens met us beyond the chamber door. Va-Sheva sent one back to guard the radio and hard case in their recess. At the crossings she stopped others with a low word; one went downward and another ran ahead. Sarah kept pace beside her lamp. Every question went to Sarah.\par

``How many bores?''\par

``Three active. One deep. Two shallow.''\par

``How long has the deep one worked?''\par

``Not less than six days. Not more than nine.''\par

``Can you stop it from this side?''\par

``Not by meeting the bit. You may collapse a throat ahead of it, if you know the rock and can lose the chambers beyond. You may foul a water bore, but then you lose the water. You may let them enter a false void and close it behind them. None is a clean answer.''\par

Va-Sheva sent a warden for the keepers of the north-western galleries and another for the oldest survey kept by Stone. She asked how many people the capture hospital could hold. Sarah gave an estimate, stopped at a smooth patch of wall and held out her hand. A warden gave her a black marking stone. She drew the ridge and rig, ran a line down at an angle, then asked Kailan for the reserve and Hall. He put his finger against the wall. She joined the marks. Va-Sheva held the lamp close, studied the lines and wiped them out with her sleeve.\par

``Justice will require the order of discovery,'' she said. ``What you saw, what she measured, what the papers say. Keep those separate.'' She turned to Kailan. ``All five cast you out. I have to bring you before them with these two. Have your answer ready.''\par

``Bring the case,'' he said.\par

``With its guard. It will be opened before Justice.''\par

Where the stair turned, Sarah and Jean-Charles had gone several steps ahead. Va-Sheva lowered her voice. The recorder remained against my ribs.\par

``Va-Elise wrote that you did not speak for eleven days after containment. Is it true?''\par

``Yes.''\par

``Could you drink?''\par

``When she put the cup in my hands.''\par

``Sleep?''\par

``Not enough.''\par

She put the lamp on the next step. ``Can you stand through a hearing?''\par

``Yes.''\par

``Look at me when you answer.''\par

Kailan raised his head. ``I can stand.''\par

She took up the lamp. ``I was right to contain you. I know what it cost. Do not ask me for more now. I have to get you through this hearing.''\par

She climbed after the others. Kailan waited until the space of one step had opened between them, then followed.\par

The route crossed an open stair above the common wards. Lamps burned over food stalls. A woman walked twelve children in pairs; the smallest held the back of the child's tunic ahead of him and dragged one sandal. A seller weighed roots in a hanging pan while two men disputed his weights. Someone beat dust from a mat over a rail and earned a shout from the level beneath. Water ran in a channel cut through the centre of the floor.\par

No one looked up at first. The stair ran close against the wall, above the stalls. I had spent two years comparing tool marks, copied signs and the mineral residue on objects Sarah put before me. Below my hand on the rail, a man was binding a cracked pot with wire. A woman folded a stone blade into a cloth and passed it to a boy with a scraped palm. At a table under three lamps, a man pressed his thumb into rounds of dough, turned each one and pressed again.\par

The smell of bread reached us before we crossed the centre of the stair: yeast, warm grain and the scorched flour at the edge of an oven. I knew charred cakes from sites nine thousand years old, the storage pits and broken querns. In bones laid beside those settlements I had examined the worn teeth. Below me, a baker struck flour from his hands and called to a child to carry six loaves home.\par

The recorder knocked against the rail. Several faces lifted. Va-Sheva caught my sleeve before it struck again.\par

``They do not know,'' she said. ``They will not learn it from you on a stair. Walk.''\par

The child with the six loaves had stopped beneath us. One loaf slipped against his chest; he trapped it with his chin and laughed. I put the machine under my coat and walked. At the upper turn the stalls disappeared. The smell remained for twelve steps more.\par

\ornament

The first blow took the stair sideways.\par

Stone struck my shoulder. The recorder drove into my ribs beneath my coat. A lamp went out below; another rolled in a yellow circle until a hand caught it. Then the sound arrived through the floor: one flat concussion, a single force driven into the Mountain without the patient interval of the five-stroke sounding. Dust fell from the joints overhead. Far inside the rock, worked stone ground against worked stone.\par

Sarah put both hands on the wall. ``A charge. Down a shallow bore, close enough to break a void or test what breaks around it. North-west, near the surface. What lies there?''\par

Va-Sheva had already turned toward the sound. Her next order failed to come at once.\par

``What lies there?'' Sarah repeated.\par

``Faith,'' Va-Sheva said. ``A Faith passage. The road to the Hall.''\par

She ran. Jean-Charles went with her and the nearest warden. Sarah called for a lamp, a plan of the north-western arc and the keeper of Stone. The ward children below had begun to cry. The seller's hanging scale knocked against its post with each movement underfoot.\par

Kailan put his palm to the wall. I put mine beside it. A tremor travelled through the stone, stopped, then returned deeper and slower. Grit moved under my fingers. From beyond the turn came a sharper failure and Va-Sheva shouting for the passage to be cleared.\par

I lifted the closed recorder toward the grinding and felt its motor through the case. A warden set his lamp beside Kailan and ran ahead. Kailan took it. We ran after the others.\par
