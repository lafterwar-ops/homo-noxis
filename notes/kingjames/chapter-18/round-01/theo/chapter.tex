\textit{In Noxius}

(Paul): --- The hearing. The sealed chamber, the five, the ruling. I need your account of it, Kailan, in your own words.

(Kailan): --- No. Put the thing away. It fell three days since, and it is falling still out there, on people who never heard our names. Who is left above to read how carefully it was decided?

(Paul): --- I cannot answer that. I can keep an exact record of the decision. Without one, in a hundred years the hearing may be whatever the survivors needed it to have been.

(Kailan): --- You were there. Your machine ran the whole while; it holds every word already. Write what you saw. Or have it from the Keepers, from Sarah, from Va-Sheva. They did the speaking. I stood at the wall with my hands bound and said almost nothing.

(Paul): --- Va-Sheva will not speak to me. Sarah gives me her back. The Keepers are turning air and counting fish. None has an hour for this. The reel cannot supply what you saw from the wall, or what you knew while the others spoke.

(Kailan): --- Not now. I have a shift on the vents and nothing in me to give you.

\centerline{[\,---\,]}

(Paul): --- May we try the road? What happened after we left the Blackwood Estate.

(Kailan): --- \ldots{} You can do that yourself. You kept the record. I can take it up again once we reach the Mountain. Let me rest.

\end{room}

My name is Paul Morgan. This is my account of the return. My training is in strata, middens, worked flint and bone laid down in silt forty thousand years ago. I had no training in pursuit, or in a desert, or in war. I have the travelling reels and my notebooks before me. Where I stopped recording, I entered the interval; where I could not understand a speaker, I have named the person who supplied the words.\par

The lawn reel ended under the rotor at dusk. The Blackwood Estate stood lit at every window. Doors I had never used were open onto the halls, and men I had never met carried cases through them. Va-Elise went between the telephone, the papers on the hall table and the aircraft crew. I carried the machine and a coat. She told me to keep up.\par

The tape reached its clear leader while Kailan was being taken across the grass. I stopped it, wrote the hour on the box and sealed the reel in its tin. Va-Elise placed it in the fire case with the fixed instruction reels and one route packet. She put a fresh spool in my hand. A crewman loaded Sarah's chair and helped her aboard.\par

At the house steps a man offered Sarah three files. She chose one. He tried to give her the others again, and she pointed him back through the door. Another man asked what should become of the instruction rooms.\par

``Lock them. The instruction reels stay in Elise's fire case. Nothing else leaves except Mr Morgan's sealed interview reels and notebooks.''\par

``The telephones?''\par

``Let them ring.''\par

I heard no further order from inside the house. The front door remained open. A sheet crossed the lawn, lifted in the rotor wind and went over the roof. Then the ground fell away beneath my boots.\par

For two years I had walked across the landing marks. From the air I could see their whole outline. Sarah, ninety-three, sat on a headset beside me, making clipped calls in three languages. Her right thumb shook against the mouthpiece. She closed it beneath her left hand and continued. When the headset passed to Va-Elise I heard her confirm fuel at one strip, vehicles at another, and no names at either.\par

Sarah took it back and called the outer stations. Two answered. At the third she listened through twenty turns of the rotor and wrote a small cross beside a number. She made the fourth call with the station book still open at the third. I could hear the ringing through her earpiece. She held it close enough to press the edge into her cheek.\par

Kailan sat opposite me. I had put the fresh spool in the recorder, and the machine stood between my boots with its strap around my wrist. At every bank I drew it back against my ankles. Through its headset I heard our breath under the engine, Sarah's numbers, and the buckle of the fire case tapping against Va-Elise's wrist.\par

The helicopter put us down at Geneva beside the private aircraft. We waited in an old freight hangar until a clearance lamp turned green. Urs remained behind the red line when we boarded. I had flown to digs in the cargo netting of transports older than myself; this cabin had beds and a shower. Food came through a door beside the galley. I ate everything on my tray. Kailan watched the window and touched nothing. Sarah ate half a piece of toast, then slept upright.\par

Once she opened her eyes. ``Finish your meal, Mr Morgan. I do not know when any of us will eat properly again.'' Then she closed them.\par

Va-Elise worked at the table with three maps that did not join. One held the aircraft's descent. Another held a road. The third began beyond the road and showed wells and strips of stone, with no destination marked. I asked which wells still answered to Horizon. She put her finger beside one.\par

``We meet Amastan here. He tells us which wells we can use.''\par

``Who confirms that he will be there?''\par

``He has the meeting place,'' Sarah said. Her eyes remained closed. ``Elise cannot confirm his route for him.''\par

``And if he does not come?''\par

Sarah opened her eyes. ``Then we are already too late.''\par

Va-Elise folded the maps separately. Kailan had fallen asleep. I lay down, and woke as we began our descent.\par

\ornament

At dawn we came down onto a strip of old colonial tarmac. Lamps in jars marked its broken edge. Beyond them stood a windsock and two fuel drums; there was no building. Three black armoured vehicles waited with their engines turning. Jean-Charles came out of the middle vehicle to meet us. Kailan spoke his name and raised a hand.\par

Va-Elise took a map to each driver. I followed her down the line and heard each repeat one road and one stopping place. She asked none of them for the destination. Men in work clothes unloaded water, cloth, medical cases and sacks of feed. The weapons came last. Jean-Charles opened a long case across the quiet man's knees, inspected it and closed it without taking anything out.\par

Sarah refused the chair. Kailan went one pace below her on the aircraft steps and Va-Elise followed. At the bottom Sarah stood until her breathing settled. Then she asked whether anyone had approached the strip. One driver had seen lights beyond the pan. Another had heard an engine without seeing an aircraft. She assigned the lights to the lead vehicle and the engine to the rear.\par

Va-Elise took the lead. Jean-Charles rode beside our driver in the middle vehicle. Sarah, Kailan and I were strapped behind him; the quiet man sat with his cased weapon in the rear seat. The third vehicle held our tail. Before we moved, Va-Elise passed the fire case through our window and Kailan braced it between his boots.\par

The sun cleared the horizon as we left the tarmac. At each fork Jean-Charles waited for Va-Elise's rear lamp to show once and disappear. He used no map. Our driver repeated a distance, then drove until Jean-Charles lifted a hand. Sarah held the station book closed on her knees.\par

Across the pan they took a straight strip of tar at a speed that shook speech out of us. A knot of vehicles appeared far to the east. Through the windscreen I saw a weapon rise in Va-Elise's vehicle; the quiet man opened his case behind me. The vehicles crossed the road at our backs. No one fired. Jean-Charles watched them diminish in the mirror, then gave our driver two fingers forward.\par

At the next rise the lead vehicle left the road. We followed down a dry drainage cut, the armour striking stone under my feet. Our third vehicle continued along the tar. Its engine dwindled. We waited beneath the road until I heard two engines going north, one farther off than the other. Jean-Charles kept a hand down while the sound lasted. When our rear vehicle appeared in the cut behind us, he lifted it.\par

Beyond the pan the tar ended. Our speed fell to a grinding walk over broken ground. At a stop the drivers exchanged places with the engines still turning. The quiet man was told to sleep for forty minutes. He put one hand through the handle of his case and closed his eyes. When Jean-Charles woke him, he went forward into the driver's seat. I looked back along our track. Nothing followed us.\par

\ornament

Near the end of the track stood one of Sarah's stations. On the aircraft she had described the buried rooms: two or three people, a drum, a border and a fuel gauge. The door of this one stood open to the desert.\par

``Not in sixty years,'' Sarah said. She stayed outside while Jean-Charles went in. He called us after him.\par

Three enamel cups stood beside a kettle. Cool air came from the wall, water ran from the tap, and the coffee in the cups had gone cold. Blood had dried on the floor and the table lip. There were no bodies. On the back of a stores request someone had written a message. The pencil lay in the page gutter. I held the recorder over the table and read the last legible words: \emph{door opened with}.\par

The next letter was a downward stroke. Blood lay under the writer's side of the page. None crossed the words. Beside the inner door were three hooks and a duty board bearing three names. One chair lay on its side. Another had been driven against the table hard enough to split its back rail. The chair before the drum held the dark print of a hand.\par

Sarah examined the outer door. I held the lamp while she turned its edge toward her. There was no split wood, no bent hinge, no scoring around the lock. She closed it, opened it again with the key and kept the key in her palm.\par

Va-Elise went out to examine the approach. Jean-Charles checked the rear room, the generator recess and the duct wide enough for an arm. I could hear each small door open from where I stood at the table. In the corner a record box had burned. The leaves along its edges were black; the middle of the files remained brown and readable.\par

The drum kept turning. Five strokes came up from the ground beneath us and drew themselves on its paper. Kailan stood before them without touching the sheet. They were evenly spaced, with the same rise. When the fifth ended, the stylus went back to its thin, irregular line. Sarah watched the interval and checked her wristwatch.\par

``Again in forty minutes,'' she said. ``They are sounding on a programme. Not prospecting. Prospecting changes the question after each answer. This asks the same question until the answer alters.''\par

I lifted the burned files with forceps. Whole bundles had been packed under the lid, pressed so tightly that I had to prise the leaves apart. Fuel. Water. Watch changes. I spoke the dates into the recorder. A delivery four weeks before had been cancelled in another hand. The corner crumbled and left a black crescent on the table. I set the stores request beside the surviving records; its first lines reported the same five-stroke signal.\par

Va-Elise came back with a length of pale thread and a photograph from her small camera. She laid the thread beside the pencil and described the marks outside. The station's boot prints lay under heavier tread. Two people had walked away; a third track began in a scuff at the door and continued as two parallel heel cuts. Tyres stood beyond the rise, out of sight of the station slit.\par

``Taken,'' Sarah said. ``They tried to burn the records while they were being made to leave. These three knew the quarter, my notation and the buried lines. Whoever holds them will be asking for all of it.'' She set her free hand on the table. It shook there until she spread her fingers.\par

I turned the machine toward her. She read the first name from the duty board, gave the office, the years of service and the person who should receive the news. Then the second. On the third she looked back at the board before giving the years. After the name of the person to be informed she stopped. The generator ticked behind the wall. I let the reel turn.\par

Va-Elise found the drawer of local approach routes unlocked and empty. She pulled it all the way out and held it upside down. Nothing fell.\par

``The key was used,'' she said.\par

Sarah looked at the unfinished message. ``Or the door was never locked.''\par

``These people would lock it.''\par

``Yes.'' Sarah opened her own station book. With the burned pencil she crossed out six call signs. ``You go back with the vehicles. Close these six posts. No transmission through the old net. No resupply on a listed road. Move the people and break the instruments they cannot carry. No one waits for a further order from me.''\par

Va-Elise read the list. ``Blackwood?''\par

``Blackwood is an address they know. It is finished.''\par

``If one of the six has already opened?''\par

Sarah put the station key into her hand. ``Bring out whom you can. Burn the rest correctly.''\par

Va-Elise closed her fingers over the key. She put the list inside her sleeve and went out to the drivers. Sarah remained at the table while I finished numbering the sheets. I asked whether she wanted to add anything to the three names. She shook her head.\par

The next five strokes began as we carried the water out. We stopped in the doorway. Dust moved in the split of the table, and the kettle gave a small metallic answer. Sarah counted the interval aloud. Then she told us to keep moving.\par

\ornament

The vehicles stopped where the track entered folded rock. A young man waited with two others and seven animals, his face bound against the glare. Sarah called him Amastan. He counted us and our loads before he answered her.\par

He rejected two water tins whose caps rang against their handles. He wrapped the recorder's metal catches in felt and had the documents unpacked from the food. His companions worked by touch in the shade of the folds, passing straps beneath the animals without making them turn. When the loads were quiet he uncovered his face.\par

``My grandfather carried that name for you,'' he said. ``He has been thirty years in the ground. I carry it now.'' He drew a strap through its ring. ``Your officers put it in their treaties before my grandfather was born. They spelled it wrong.''\par

Va-Elise opened a map on the bonnet. Amastan looked at it, drew a line in the dust with his switch and laid three stones across the route she had marked.\par

``That road was safe when my father held the name. There are tyre cuts now. Men watched it two nights ago.'' He drew west through a blank part of her map. ``Here.''\par

Jean-Charles crouched beside the drawing. Amastan made him repeat every turn, then erased it with his foot. Va-Elise took the tactical packet from the fire case. Sarah had told her to hold it until this meeting; I heard her repeat the order as she gave it to Jean-Charles. He opened it beside her. It contained the station approach and two abort points. The destination was absent.\par

Va-Elise put the reel tins into a felt wrap and placed it in my hands. The route papers went back into her case.\par

``One copy. No house behind us. Do not let the desert have it.''\par

She checked the water and each animal's load, then folded her map. Amastan put his switch across it before she could point to the road again.\par

``From here I take them.''\par

She nodded and shut the case. Jean-Charles received no return route. Va-Elise went back with the vehicles. At the first open ground they divided: one north, one east, one toward the old tarmac. Amastan stood watching until the separate dust lines had thinned out of sight. He shifted Sarah's saddle one hole and told us to move.\par

The first night was stone underfoot and a wind that filled the recorder's catches with grit. Amastan went ahead of the first animal without a light. One companion walked last, brushing out the places where a hoof slipped. Jean-Charles offered a bearing twice. Amastan listened and continued along his own line. Sarah rode in a sling they had fitted around the saddle. She had mounted without help, and held herself upright until we stopped.\par

We lay beneath ground-coloured cloth by day. Before setting it, Amastan made us lie down with our loads where he placed them; after it was spread, we did not rise beneath it. He chose the hours and the order of drinking. The animals drank before us. I kept the wrapped tins against my side and the recorder where I could reach it without lifting the cloth.\par

By the second night I could recognise a halt. The animals lowered their heads, and someone checked the loads. When Amastan raised a closed hand, no buckle moved. We once remained that way while an engine passed beyond a ridge. I felt its approach through the soles of my boots before I heard it through the air. My hand went around the recorder's fittings and stayed there after the engine had passed.\par

Before dawn we reached a well in a cleft. Amastan lifted six fitted slabs and lowered a skin narrow enough to pass between them. He watered the animals and replaced the slabs in reverse order. His companion brushed the rock with a branch. When he moved aside I could not find the opening from three paces away.\par

``Does the Mountain keep this well?'' I asked.\par

``My people keep it.''\par

``For the Mountain?''\par

He coiled the line. ``For those who arrive thirsty and do not arrive with guns.''\par

On the third night a cairn stopped us. Amastan counted its stones with the end of his switch. One had been added, he told Sarah. He left it standing and took us six hours around. By then he and Kailan had to lift Sarah into the sling. She let them do it without speaking.\par

At noon her hand shook against the cup. I held it while she drank, lowering it when she lifted one finger. When it was empty she said, ``Mr Morgan, if that appears in your account, at least spell my name correctly.''\par

``I always do.''\par

``One useful quality.''\par

Amastan shortened the next passage and put the longest climb in the coldest hour. He told Kailan to walk on the left of Sarah's animal where the ground fell away. Sarah asked to see the route. He drew it for her with one finger in the cloth.\par

``We have already lost half a day,'' she said.\par

``I can take you by this climb. I cannot carry you up the other one if you fall.''\par

She studied his finger. ``How much longer?''\par

He gave her the hours, then smoothed the cloth. We left when he had said.\par

Kailan called the folds the Mountain's outer wall, the last country no state had crossed. Amastan was tying a load.\par

``The Mountain tells itself that,'' he said. ``The sand keeps no walls and no masters. It lets a man cross, closes behind him and forgets his name by morning. A country that forgets your name is owned by no one.''\par

I wrote the sentence inside my wrist. The pencil rubbed away beneath my sleeve. At our next hide I copied what remained onto the paper band around the fresh reel and asked him for the words I had lost. He gave them while he worked on Sarah's sling. I fitted the reel into the machine.\par

During the daylight hides I recorded speech. At night I let the machine turn for a few minutes at each change of ground: soft hoof-fall, iron on buried stone, wind through a slit too narrow to see. I counted the batteries each morning and numbered the intervals I had left unrecorded.\par

On the fourth night the five strokes reached us through the ground. Sarah's animal raised its head at the first. I felt four more at equal distances, too low to hear. Amastan put his fingers to the rock. Sarah counted. We covered the metal fittings, gave the animals the final water and went forward on foot.\par

\ornament

Amastan brought us to the camp overlook in the last dark of the fourth night. His companions took the animals into a high cleft. He led us up a dry wash and stopped beneath its rim, with his palm on the ground.\par

``From there onward, their instruments can hear a stone roll.''\par

He took Jean-Charles forward on his belly. When they returned, Jean-Charles asked him to repeat the passage between two listening posts. Amastan traced the first turns against the rock.\par

``At the last bend you go alone. If I am seen, the road dies with me.''\par

Jean-Charles removed his coat, covered the pale skin of his hands and face and tightened the cloth at his collar. He looked at the recorder.\par

``Still taking notes on me, professor?''\par

``Your questions at the safe house are on the record. So is your request that Kailan keep them from Elise.''\par

He drew the cloth over his mouth. ``Sarah heard him tell it. She still put the field movement in my hands.''\par

``I have recorded that too.''\par

``Good. Keep the machine dry.''\par

Amastan took him over the rim and came back alone. I lay on the cold rock while the light entered the pan. Dishes, tents, vehicles and a drilling rig became visible below us. Two flecks crossed the high sky without sound. Sarah gave the times at which they would return. The dish beside the command tents moved after each passage and stopped on the same bearing.\par

Men held the ridges and the mouths of the approaches. Tripods between them pointed into the rock. Red survey stakes crossed the broken ground in straight lines. On the western side a truck stood over the entrance of an old drovers' track. Sarah gave its local name and pointed it out to Kailan.\par

``To the Oasis,'' she said. ``Shield allowed it to go out of use. No Outsider had found the northern mouth.''\par

Kailan took the glass. The truck covered the mouth exactly.\par

``They were told.''\par

``Yes.'' Sarah pointed beneath the rig. ``That bore is west of your Hall and deeper. What lies there?''\par

``The reserve.''\par

``Water?''\par

He nodded and gave back the glass.\par

At the centre of the camp stood a double ring of tents with separate generators. Long vehicles waited beside them, fitted to carry a body flat. Men crossed from the command tents with covered papers and returned by other paths. Through the glass I watched the entrances of the double ring. Medical orderlies passed in and out; I saw no one else enter.\par

Sarah kept the glass after I returned it. ``A good half of what you see was written in my hand.''\par

She watched until the grey outlines of the tents had colour. Then she lowered it.\par

``Some part of me wanted the Mountain found. I wanted not to be the last one holding it.'' She set the glass on the rock. ``This is what found looks like.''\par

We heard no shot. Before direct sun reached the wash, Jean-Charles appeared in it with a man bent in front of him. Amastan went down to them and brought them up through the listening ground. The man wore the camp's grey clothes. His wrists were bound and his mouth gagged. Jean-Charles carried a flat hard case and a black field radio.\par

``Post nine,'' he said. ``The seam is open beyond it. Not for long.''\par

He set the case on the rock. Sarah reached for its catches.\par

``A moment,'' I said. ``I need the place it came from, and who handled it.''\par

``The command tent,'' Jean-Charles said. ``A locked drawer. No one has handled it since me.''\par

I stated his name into the recorder, the hour, the place where he had brought it to us and the condition of the catches. Sarah waited. I opened the case with the machine beside it. The papers had the same stock, a sequence of punch holes and consecutive issue marks. On several sheets, corrections in the same ink had pressed into the leaves beneath. I described these before passing the first sheet to Sarah.\par

She found a small mark in its upper corner.\par

``French strategic service. A compartment descended from the arrangements Martinez and my father made in this desert. The mineral company is a cover. This is a state operation.''\par

The next leaves bore a joint Algerian groundwater and rare-earth concession. Sarah read the concession number, drilling permits and customs entries to us, translating as she went. She judged them genuine. A distribution table sent fuel, airspace and explosives approvals through separate offices for separate survey blocks. The Algerian recipients' copies omitted the central coordinates. A personnel annex counted three thousand and eighty-four people in nine contractors: drilling, transport, aviation, medicine, camp labour and security. Three hundred and eighty-six had French service numbers.\par

Sarah read the operational headings against the camp below: seismic sounding, capture medicine, water control, approach denial, compartmented command.\par

``The industrial work is real. It would survive inspection. They know there is a mechanism under the rock; they do not know what it does. They are using my grid and my methods to reach it.'' She turned back to the concession. ``The yellow rock was never the prize.''\par

Three leaves into the logistics annex I found the northern track among forty routes. I checked its local name against what I had written when Sarah pointed out the truck. Sarah read the entry beside it, in a quartermaster's hand: \emph{source: internal, reliability high}.\par

I laid the page before her and put my finger beneath the line.\par

``After Martinez was shut out, who held this corridor?''\par

``One channel. One pair of hands.'' She did not turn toward Jean-Charles.\par

``The paper does not name him.''\par

``No.''\par

``It came in the case he brought us. I can establish that these sheets belong together. I cannot establish the truth of this entry.''\par

Sarah folded the page once. ``Serious. Not proof.''\par

She put it inside her coat. Jean-Charles stood beside the prisoner, watching the camp through the wash. He had not asked what we had found. For four minutes I had read without dictating an entry. The reel had continued; it held the rustle of those pages and what was said beside it.\par

The field radio lit. A young man spoke in French. Sarah translated while Jean-Charles covered the set: post nine had missed its check. If Farid had crawled behind a rock to sleep again, he could answer to the sergeant.\par

Amastan cut the gag. He gave the prisoner water and waited for him to swallow before asking his name. It was Farid. Sarah translated his answers for us. He guarded a mineral survey. He knew his own watch and the two posts beside it. He had been told there were ``primitives'' in the ground, a lost tribe to be handled \emph{without loss of life}. His sappers were to bring everything out intact and break nothing.\par

Kailan crouched before him. ``Who gave the order to take no life?''\par

``A medical officer. I do not know his name.''\par

``The same officer who sets the charges?''\par

``No.''\par

``Who can stop the digging?''\par

``Operations. Not our command.''\par

``Who among you may speak to the men on the western ridge?''\par

Farid looked toward it. Sarah repeated the question.\par

``No one at my post. They have their own command.''\par

Kailan opened the case to the live-capture orders. Five priorities were coded by task; every name was omitted. He read three aloud: the one who reads the deep water; the one who keeps the hours; the one who holds the seal. Two followed under codes I could not translate.\par

``Five functions,'' he said. ``They know the mechanism is worked by five hands. They mean to seize the complete set alive and force it to answer to a new hand.''\par

Sarah read over his shoulder. ``Can they?''\par

``The division was built so that no one could. They are coming to commit the one seizure it exists to prevent.''\par

Sarah asked Farid about the codes. He shook his head at each one. His eyes followed the page when Kailan lowered it into the case. The radio spoke again. Sarah said the sergeant had answered. Lights came on at two posts.\par

``Amastan knows a seam beyond post nine,'' Jean-Charles said. ``Their instruments do not cover it yet. Farid's missed check has shortened our hour to minutes.''\par

One of Amastan's companions bound Farid's hands in front and led him toward the high cleft.\par

``We keep him until your road is shut,'' Amastan said. ``After that his road is his.''\par

Jean-Charles closed the case and gave it to Sarah. He put the field radio in my hand.\par

``We go now.''\par

We left the animals, blankets and empty water skins in the high cleft. I put the reel tins inside my coat and bound the recorder against my chest with its carrying strap. Sarah tried to take the station book as well as the case. Kailan showed her the height of the cut we had to crawl through. She tore out the crossed page of call signs and put it beside the annex inside her sleeve. The book stayed beneath a stone.\par

Amastan returned from Farid's cleft. Three lights moved along the wash Jean-Charles had used. Another swept the ridge above post nine. Men called in pairs. The drilling covered their voices with a regular iron turning, then stopped without warning. At each silence Amastan put his hand down and we stopped with him.\par

He placed our feet in his own marks at the first turn. At the second he took Sarah's weight across a slab that sounded hollow under my knee. At the third he showed us a black cut no wider than a doorway.\par

``That reaches the old seam. My name goes no farther.''\par

Sarah took his wrist. ``Close the northern well.''\par

``It was closed before you came.''\par

``The other two?''\par

``My brother takes one. My sister takes the other.'' He turned to Kailan. ``Tell Shield the roads outside the Mountain are held by those who walk them. If they forget again, no wall will teach them.''\par

Kailan repeated it. Amastan required him to say it again, then withdrew over our path with the brush of hide in his hand. I watched him lift our last marks before Jean-Charles told me to follow.\par

Jean-Charles entered the cut first. Kailan guided Sarah behind him, and I went last. Where the roof came down we moved on our elbows. The radio gave a burst of French against my ribs. Jean-Charles reached back and took it. He turned a control until the voice stopped.\par

Sarah whispered the message to Kailan: posts eight through twelve were to close the western arc and find Farid.\par

``Did you answer?'' she asked Jean-Charles.\par

``No.''\par

``Could you?''\par

``I know the language of the post. I do not know the man's alarm word.''\par

He passed the set back. Sarah watched the hand releasing it. Then she put her elbows forward and crawled.\par

The cut opened above broken shale. Post nine stood across the slope: two men, a low tent and a tripod turning by degrees. Beyond it the ground met an unbroken rock face. A white lamp travelled along the base of the Mountain, withdrew and returned. I could see no seam.\par

Jean-Charles counted the tripod's turns on one finger. On the fifth he took us across. The shale shifted under my knees for twelve paces. I put each loose stone into the space Kailan's hand had left. Sarah's breath grew audible. Kailan stopped, drew her arm across his shoulders and waited for the tripod to turn away. Jean-Charles raised his hand and we moved.\par

A man challenged from the post. Jean-Charles put us flat and answered him in French. I understood neither question nor answer. The man asked again. Jean-Charles gave a shorter reply. A figure stepped beyond the tent and raised a lamp toward us.\par

The drilling rig started. Jean-Charles pulled Sarah to her feet and ran bent beneath her arm. Kailan took her other side. I followed with the recorder striking my ribs. The white beam crossed the shale behind us. When I looked back it lay on the place where we had been, with small stones still moving through it.\par

Jean-Charles put his hand into a shadow at the rock face. His arm disappeared to the wrist. Kailan knelt and cleared three handfuls of gravel from a narrow bronze plate. It held five shallow cups. He pressed the outer two, the centre, then the outer two again. Nothing moved.\par

``Again,'' Sarah said.\par

``The return sign changes with the hour.''\par

``Then give it the hour.''\par

He set the pattern again and held the centre cup. Jean-Charles pressed his shoulder against the rock. A line opened without sound. Cold air reached my face. Two hands came from inside, seized Sarah and pulled her through. Kailan passed the case after her, entered, and turned to take my arm. Jean-Charles pushed me from behind.\par

The white lamp found the bronze plate. A shot struck above the opening while Jean-Charles was still outside. The hands reached past me and caught him. He came through under falling dust; the seam closed. No weapon answered. For several breaths I heard our breathing and the recorder turning under my coat. Then a lamp was uncovered.\par

\ornament

Va-Sheva stood in a low chamber with two wardens behind her. She wore Shield grey. The lamp was on the floor, lighting the rounded cloth over her belly. She was with child. She looked from Kailan to Jean-Charles, Sarah and me, then down at the case and the radio. Before speaking she placed one warden beside the seam and one at the inner door.\par

They searched us in the order we had entered. Sarah held the folded pages where the warden could see them. Kailan carried no weapon. Jean-Charles laid down a pistol, two knives, black cord and three small objects I could not name. When the warden reached for the recorder I asked him to state his name and office before taking it from me.\par

Va-Sheva stopped his hand. ``It stays closed. Shield will hear it with Justice present, or destroy it with the man who carried it.''\par

The warden wrapped the radio and the hard case separately. He put them inside a stone recess without opening either. Sarah kept the folded pages. Blood had come through one knee of her trousers. Va-Sheva pointed at it.\par

``After the hearing,'' Sarah said.\par

``Bind it while she speaks,'' Va-Sheva told the warden. ``If she falls, carry her. Do not stop her answer.''\par

She faced Kailan.\par

``Shield's writ sent you out to learn whether the enemy within was real. The council that would not keep you sealed it. You return with two Outsiders the Mountain has never weighed. One made the net now closing on us. You bring a machine full of our speech up a seam no Veil is shown, during an attack the Mountain does not know has begun. By the law I hold, I should kill you at this lamp, kill them beside you and seal the seam over all four.''\par

``I made the net,'' Sarah said. ``I can identify what they are using, how it is supplied and where the bores are aimed.''\par

Va-Sheva kept her eyes on Kailan. ``I know her name.''\par

``Then hear her,'' he said. ``She can tell you how far the bore has come. We have brought their papers.''\par

Va-Sheva turned to Sarah. ``How deep?''\par

Sarah gave the measure. Va-Sheva asked the angle, the number of rigs and the interval between the five strokes. Sarah answered each. The warden worked at her knee while she spoke. He pulled the bandage tight; she stopped on a number, then gave it again.\par

``Jean-Charles vouches for the breach,'' Va-Sheva said to Kailan.\par

``He does.''\par

``I trust his hands. I have worked beside them.'' She looked at Sarah. ``I have not worked beside yours.''\par

Sarah waited. Va-Sheva ordered the inner warden to summon Shield and Justice. Then she looked at my recorder.\par

``That remains in your hand, Outsider. If it opens, you die before the lid clears.''\par

``Understood.''\par

``Under necessity of Shield, I admit the four of you as far as the sealed floor and no farther. The seam you used is dead from this hour. If your warning is false, I answer for opening it. If it is true, we count every other answer after the Mountain is still standing.''\par

At her order the warden drove a bronze pin into the five-cup plate. Three descending blows sounded from inside the wall. He tested the centre cup with his thumb. It did not move. Va-Sheva took up the lamp and told Kailan to follow her.\par

We went through Shield's passages. At each crossing she gave a low word to a warden. One went downward, another ran ahead, and a third stayed with the wrapped radio and case. The last I saw of them, both packages were closed in his hands. Va-Sheva directed her questions to Sarah.\par

``How many bores?''\par

``Three active. One deep. Two shallow.''\par

``How long has the deep one worked?''\par

``Not less than six days. Not more than nine.''\par

``Can you stop it from this side?''\par

``Not by meeting the bit. You may collapse a throat ahead of it, if you know the rock and can lose the chambers beyond. You may foul a water bore, but then you lose the water. You may let them enter a false void and close it behind them. I need a survey before I can tell you which.''\par

Va-Sheva sent for the keepers of the north-western galleries and the oldest survey kept by Stone. She asked whether the deep reserve shared a wall with the lower wards. Sarah needed to draw the bore's approach. A warden gave her a black marking stone, and she put the ridge, the rig and the angle on the passage wall. She added the water and the Hall. Va-Sheva traced the distance with her finger, then wiped out the lines with her sleeve.\par

``The council must hear all of it,'' she said. ``You, Kailan. Her. The papers. The machine. Justice will want the order in which you learned each thing. All five cast you out. I am taking you back to their sealed floor with an Outsider at one side and the maker of this assault at the other.''\par

``Take them the case,'' Kailan said. ``Let them read it and listen to their own stone.''\par

``They will ask who carried it.''\par

Sarah and Jean-Charles went several steps ahead at the turn of the stair. Va-Sheva lowered her voice. I was close behind Kailan, with the recorder still against my ribs.\par

``Va-Elise wrote that you did not speak for eleven days after containment. Is it true?''\par

``Yes.''\par

``Could you drink?''\par

``When she put the cup in my hands.''\par

``Sleep?''\par

``Not enough.''\par

She kept climbing. ``Then the wall held. You are breathing.''\par

``I came back.''\par

Her lamp stopped one step above him.\par

``I was right to contain you. I will not forgive myself for being right. Do not make me say more. I have a Mountain to lose tonight, and I cannot lose you as well and remain of use to it.''\par

She climbed after the others. Kailan waited until the space of a step had opened between them, then followed.\par

\ornament

The route crossed an open stair above the common wards. Lamps burned over food stalls. A woman took twelve children along in pairs; the smallest held the back of the tunic in front of him and dragged a sandal. Two men disputed a seller's weights while he held a root over the hanging pan. Someone beat a mat over a rail and earned a shout from below. Water ran through a channel in the floor.\par

No one looked up at us. The stair lay against the wall above the lamps. I could see a pot being mended with wire. A woman folded a stone blade into cloth and passed it to a boy with a scraped palm. At a table under three lights, a man pressed his thumb into rounds of dough. He turned each one, pressed again and laid it on a board dusted with flour.\par

The smell of the evening bread reached us before the middle of the stair: yeast, warm grain, scorched flour from the oven's edge. I knew charred cakes from sites nine thousand years old, the pits where the grain had been stored and the querns that had broken it. Below me a baker struck flour from his hands and called a child to take six loaves home. The boy stacked them against his chest.\par

The recorder knocked on the rail. Faces lifted. Va-Sheva caught my sleeve before it struck again.\par

``They do not know,'' she said. ``They will not learn it from you on a stair. Walk.''\par

The child with the loaves had stopped beneath us. One slipped; he trapped it with his chin and laughed. I put the recorder under my coat and walked. At the upper turn the stalls disappeared. The smell stayed with me for twelve steps more.\par

\ornament

The first blow took the stair sideways.\par

Stone struck my shoulder. The recorder drove into my ribs. A lamp went out below, and another rolled in a yellow circle until a hand caught it. Then the sound reached me through the floor: one flat concussion, without the patient interval of the five-stroke sounding. Dust fell from the joints. Inside the rock I heard worked stone grinding against worked stone.\par

Sarah put both hands on the wall. ``A charge. Down a shallow bore, close enough to break a void or test what breaks around it. North-west, near the surface. What lies there?''\par

Va-Sheva had turned toward the sound. She stood with the lamp raised and gave no order.\par

``What lies there?'' Sarah asked again.\par

``Faith,'' Va-Sheva said. ``A Faith passage. The road to the Hall.''\par

She ran. Jean-Charles and the nearest warden went with her. Sarah called for a lamp, a plan of the north-western arc and the keeper of Stone. The children below were crying. The seller's scale knocked against its post with each movement underfoot.\par

Kailan put his palm to the wall. I put mine beside it. A tremor passed under my fingers, stopped, and returned deeper and slower. Grit shifted against my skin. Beyond the turn something broke with a sharper sound. Va-Sheva shouted for the passage to be cleared.\par

I lifted the recorder toward the grinding. Its reels turned. Kailan took the lamp left on the stair. We ran after the others.\par
