\begin{center}\small\itshape Mountain - inside the Greater Seal\end{center}

\begin{room}

\textit{In Noxius}

(Paul): --- The hearing: the sealed chamber, the five, the ruling they made. I need your account beside mine.

(Kailan): --- No. Put the thing away and leave me be. It is done. It fell three days since, and it is falling still out there, on people who never once heard our names. What is an account for now? Who is left above to read how carefully it was decided?

(Paul): --- I have their words. I need to know what you could see from the wall, and what you heard before I entered. I will mark where our accounts differ.

(Kailan): --- You were in the room, Paul. Your machine ran the whole while. Write what you saw. Or have it from the Keepers, from Sarah, from Va-Sheva --- they did the speaking. I stood at the wall with my hands bound and said almost nothing.

(Paul): --- Va-Sheva will not speak to me. Sarah gives me her back. The Keepers are turning air and counting fish. I have asked each of them. I am asking you again.

(Kailan): --- Not now. I have a shift on the vents and nothing in me to give you.

\centerline{[\,---\,]}

(Paul): --- Only the road, then. What happened after we left the Blackwood Estate.

(Kailan): --- \ldots{} Surely you can do that yourself, and better than I would. You kept the record. I can take it up again once we reach the Mountain. Now let me rest.

\end{room}

I am Paul Morgan. My training is in strata, middens, worked flint, bone laid down in silt forty thousand years ago. I had no training in living people under pursuit, or in a desert, or in war. This is my account of the journey. I have the travelling reels and my numbered gaps beside me. Where I depend on another person's reading, I give the reader's name.\par

At dusk the house stood lit at every window. Doors I had never seen opened onto the halls; men I had never met carried cases through them; Va-Elise moved between telephone, papers and aircraft crew. Sarah's last calls in the room had named the camp and the lost station. Outside, I carried the machine and a coat and received one order: keep up.\par

The travelling tape reached its clear leader while Kailan was being taken across the grass. I stopped it, wrote the hour on the box and sealed the reel in its tin. Va-Elise placed it in the fire case beside the fixed instruction reels and the one route packet. She gave me a fresh spool. A crewman loaded Sarah's chair, returned to the steps and helped her aboard.\par

At the house steps a man tried to give Sarah three files. She chose one and sent the other two back. Another asked what should become of the instruction rooms.\par

``Lock them. The instruction reels stay in Elise's fire case. Nothing else leaves except Mr Morgan's sealed interview reels and notebooks.''\par

``The telephones?''\par

``Let them ring.''\par

The man holding the rejected files put them under his arm to free a hand for her. She was already beyond his reach. The front door remained open as we lifted. A sheet of paper crossed the lawn, rose into the rotor wind and vanished over the roof.\par

The helicopter rose in a lurch that put my stomach through my boots. For two years I had walked across the landing marks. Sarah, ninety-three, spent the flight on a headset, making clipped calls in three languages. Her right thumb shook against the mouthpiece until she closed it beneath her left hand. A man's voice asked why the private aircraft was still on the ground. She told him how many minutes he had to correct that.\par

The headset passed once to Va-Elise, who confirmed fuel at one strip, vehicles at another and no names at either. Then Sarah called the outer stations. Two answered. At the third she listened through twenty turns of the rotor and wrote a small cross beside a number. She made the fourth call with the pencil still on the third.\par

Kailan sat opposite me. I kept the machine between my boots with its carrying strap around my wrist. Each time the helicopter banked, I drew it back against my ankles. Through the recorder's headset I heard our breath under the engine, Sarah's numbers, and the fire case's buckle tapping against Va-Elise's wrist. She put a finger between buckle and case. The tapping stopped.\par

The helicopter put us down at Geneva beside the private aircraft. We waited in an old freight hangar. When the clearance lamp turned green, Urs remained behind the red line and we boarded. I had flown to digs in the cargo netting of transports older than myself and had never entered such a cabin. There were beds and a shower. Food arrived through a door I failed to notice, and I ate all of it. Kailan touched nothing. He watched the window. Sarah ate half a piece of toast and slept upright. Once she opened her eyes. ``Finish your meal, Mr Morgan. I do not know when any of us will eat properly again.'' Then she shut them again.\par

Va-Elise worked at the table with three maps that did not join. On the first she marked the aircraft's descent. On the second she marked the road the vehicles were to take. The third began where the road failed and contained wells and strips of stone, with no destination. I asked which wells still answered to Horizon. Va-Elise put her finger beside one.\par

``We meet Amastan here. He tells us which wells we can use.''\par

``And if no man comes?''\par

She kept her finger on the mark. ``We wait under cover. We do not choose a well ourselves.''\par

Sarah opened her eyes. ``Keep enough fuel to take the drivers back. Have them repeat their roads before anyone leaves the strip.''\par

Va-Elise wrote below the map. When she folded it, I lay down. Kailan was already asleep. We flew through the night; I woke as we began our descent. Sarah's uneaten toast had been cleared away. The file she had chosen remained across her knees.\par

\ornament

At dawn we came down onto a strip of old colonial tarmac. Lamps stood in jars along the broken edge; a broom lay beside the last jar. Beyond them were a windsock, two fuel drums and three black armoured vehicles with their engines turning. Jean-Charles came from the middle vehicle to meet us. Kailan spoke his name and raised a hand. Va-Elise walked the line, gave each driver his map and made him repeat one road and one stopping place. None was asked to name the destination.\par

Men in work clothes unloaded water, cloth, medical cases and sacks of feed. Weapons came last. Jean-Charles opened a long case across a quiet man's knees, inspected what lay inside and closed it without removing anything.\par

Sarah refused the chair. She descended the aircraft steps with Kailan one pace below her and Va-Elise behind, and stood at the bottom until her breathing settled. Then she asked the drivers whether anyone had approached the strip. One had seen lights beyond the pan. Another had heard an engine and no aircraft. She assigned the first report to the lead vehicle and the second to the rear.\par

Va-Elise took the lead. Jean-Charles rode beside our driver in the middle vehicle; Sarah, Kailan and I were strapped behind him, with the quiet man and his cased weapon in the rear seat. The third vehicle held our tail.\par

The sun cleared the horizon as we left the tarmac. Jean-Charles used no map. At each fork he waited for Va-Elise's rear lamp to show once and disappear. The driver spoke to repeat a distance. Sarah held the station book closed on her knees. Va-Elise had passed the fire case through our window; Kailan held it between his boots. One of its corners pressed into his ankle whenever the vehicle leaned.\par

They ran the straight strip of tar across the pan at a speed that shook speech out of us. A knot of vehicles appeared far to the east. Va-Elise's vehicle slowed by one degree. The quiet man lifted the long case off his knees. Through the rear glass I saw a weapon rise in the vehicle behind us. The distant vehicles crossed our track and no one fired. Jean-Charles watched them diminish in his mirror, then gave the driver two fingers forward.\par

At the next rise the lead vehicle left the road. We followed it down a dry drainage cut, the armour striking stone under my feet. The third vehicle continued along the tar for another minute before turning elsewhere. We waited beneath the road. Two engines sounded above us, drawing north; after a while the sounds separated. Our rear vehicle appeared behind us and Va-Elise moved again.\par

Beyond the pan the tar ended. We ground through broken country at a walking pace. At a halt the drivers traded places without stopping the engines. The quiet man slept for forty minutes with one hand through the handle of the long case, woke at the time named to him and took the forward seat. The handle had left a red line across his palm. No vehicles came into view behind us.\par

\ornament

They drove through the morning along a track a wheel could barely hold. Near its end stood one of Sarah's stations.\par

On the aircraft she had described them: small buried rooms, two or three people, a drum, a border and a fuel gauge. This one had three cups beside its kettle.\par

The door stood open to the desert. Sarah said no door of hers had stood that way in sixty years. Cool air still came from the wall; water ran from the tap; the coffee had gone cold. On the paper feeding from the nearest drum, five strokes arrived through the ground beneath us. Blood had dried on the floor and the table lip. No bodies remained. A message stopped inside a word, its pencil in the page gutter. In one corner a box of records had burned badly: black edges, brown centres, dates still readable. I numbered each object aloud for the machine.\par

There were three hooks beside the inner door, three names on the duty board, three enamel cups marked differently at the handle. One chair lay on its side. A second had been pushed into the table hard enough to split its back rail. The third remained before the drum. On its seat a wet hand had dried into a dark print, with four separate fingertips along the edge.\par

Va-Elise went outside and circled the entrance. Jean-Charles checked the rear room, the generator recess and the duct wide enough for an arm but not a body. Kailan stood before the five marks coming off the drum. He did not touch the paper. The strokes were evenly spaced and identical in their rise. When the fifth ended, the stylus resumed the thin irregular work of the ground. Sarah watched the blank interval and checked her wristwatch.\par

``Again in forty minutes,'' she said. ``They are sounding on a programme. They keep putting the same load into the same ground. Look at the rise of each stroke.''\par

Whole files had been pressed into the record box. Its lid had charred on the inside. Beneath the black scales of the outer leaves, paper remained the colour of old tea. I lifted the sheets in their order and spoke their dates. Fuel. Water. Watch changes. A delivery entered four weeks before and cancelled in another hand. The corner crumbled between my forceps and left a black crescent on the table. I moved the machine out of the falling ash.\par

The unfinished message was written on the back of a stores request. Its first lines reported the five-stroke signal. The last legible words were \emph{door opened with}. The next letter was a single downward stroke. The pencil had rolled into the fold and remained there. Blood lay beneath the writer's side of the page but did not cover the words.\par

Va-Elise returned with a length of pale thread caught from the rock outside and a photograph taken with her small camera. She laid the thread on an unburned margin and described the tracks. The dust held the station's own boot marks, then heavier tread over them. Two people had walked away. A third track began in the scuff beside the door and became two parallel heel cuts. Tyres waited beyond the rise, below the station slit's field of view.\par

``Taken,'' Sarah said. She put a finger beside the heel marks in the photograph. ``One of them dragged. We do not know whether that person was alive.'' She set her hand on the table. ``If they have the three of them, they have people who can read this quarter, my notation and the buried lines. The camp has room to keep them.''\par

She read the three names from the duty board into the recorder. After each name she gave an office, years of service and the person to whom news should be carried. On the third she had to look back at the board before she could give the years. Her finger had left a grey mark on the edge of the photograph.\par

Va-Elise found the local route drawer unlocked and empty. Sarah checked the door herself. No wood was split, no hinge forced, no metal scored around the lock. She closed it and kept the key in her palm.\par

``The key was used,'' Va-Elise said.\par

``Or they opened for someone. The message does not give us the next word.'' Sarah took out her own station book and opened it to a page of call signs. With the burned pencil she crossed six of them. ``These six posts. Reach each through its own channel. Close it. No transmission through the old net, no resupply on a listed road. Move the people and break the instruments they cannot carry. No one waits for another order from me.''\par

Va-Elise copied the six signs. ``Blackwood?''\par

``The address is known. No one goes back to it.''\par

``And if one of the six has already opened?''\par

Sarah put the station key into her palm. ``Bring out whom you can. Burn what has to stay. Open the files before you put a flame to them.''\par

Va-Elise closed her fingers over the key and looked at Sarah. Then she put the list inside her sleeve and went to prepare the return.\par

I held the machine low: generator tick, wind at the open door, the stylus crossing paper. Sarah repeated the third name and corrected the years. Outside someone called for hands to carry water. I closed the machine's lid and went to the door with it hanging from my wrist. A man waited while I shifted the strap high enough to take the other handle of his tin.\par

The next five strokes began while we were carrying the water out. All of us stopped. Dust moved in the split of the table. The kettle gave a small metallic answer. Sarah counted the interval aloud, then told us to keep moving.\par

\ornament

The vehicles stopped where the track entered folded rock. A young man waited there with two others and seven animals, his face bound against the glare. Sarah called him Amastan.\par

He counted us and our loads before he answered her. He rejected two water tins because their caps rang against the handles, wrapped the recorder's metal catches in felt and divided the documents from the food. His companions repacked each animal by touch in the shade of the folds. One unfastened a bundle Va-Elise had tied and put its heavier half against the saddle. Amastan tested it with his knee. When nothing metal could strike metal, he uncovered his face.\par

``My grandfather carried that name for you,'' he said. ``He has been thirty years in the ground. I carry it now.'' He drew the last strap through its ring. ``Your officers put that name in their treaties before my grandfather was born. They spelled it wrong.''\par

Va-Elise opened a map on the bonnet of the lead vehicle. Amastan looked once, drew a different line in the dust with the butt of his switch and put three stones across the route she had marked.\par

``That road was safe when my father held the name. It has tyre cuts now. Men watched it two nights ago.'' He moved the line west through a blank place on her map. ``We go here.''\par

Jean-Charles crouched beside the drawing. Amastan made him repeat each turn, then erased the whole with his foot. Va-Elise gave Jean-Charles the tactical packet Sarah had ordered her to withhold until this handover. It held the station approach, two abort points and no destination. She transferred the water and checked the animal loads. Amastan took the packet, checked what it showed, and returned it to Jean-Charles. He gave us the order in which to follow his animals.\par

Va-Elise opened the fire case and put my sealed interview reels and the travelling tins into a felt wrap. The fixed instruction reels and route papers stayed with her. She placed the wrap in my hands and waited while I tied it shut.\par

``No house behind us, Mr Morgan. Keep these dry.''\par

She did not give Jean-Charles the return route. She took the vehicles back. Amastan watched them divide at the first open ground: one north, one east, one back toward the old tarmac. The three dust lines separated and thinned. He waited until the last could no longer be seen, shifted Sarah's saddle one hole and gave the word to move.\par

We travelled by night and lay under ground-coloured cloth by day. Amastan chose the hours, the wells and the order of drinking. The animals drank before us. On the third night he changed our line because a cairn had acquired one stone too many. He left it standing and took us six hours around.\par

The first night was stone underfoot and a wind that filled the recorder's catches with grit. Amastan walked ahead of the first animal without a light. One of his companions walked last and brushed the places where a hoof slipped. Jean-Charles twice offered a bearing. Amastan heard him and continued along his own line. At the next halt he retied the felt around my machine. The thread had worked loose where I kept putting my thumb beneath it to reach the switch.\par

By the second night I could tell a halt from a concealment. At a halt, the animals lowered their heads and someone checked the loads. At concealment, Amastan raised one closed hand and no buckle moved. Once we remained that way while an engine passed beyond the ridge. Its vibration came first through the soles of my boots. I held the recorder against my coat; the engine became audible and passed out of hearing before Amastan lowered his hand.\par

We reached a well before dawn, a crack at the back of a cleft. Amastan removed six fitted slabs, drew water with a skin narrow enough to pass between them, watered the animals and replaced the slabs in reverse order. His companion brushed the rock with a branch. When they had finished, I could not find the opening from three paces away.\par

``Does the Mountain keep this well?'' I asked.\par

``My people keep it.''\par

I pointed to a pale edge on one of the slabs. ``That has been cut recently.''\par

``My sister's son. The old stone split in the cold.'' He coiled the wet line against his arm. ``He carried that one here.''\par

Sarah rode in a sling they fitted around the saddle. At ninety-three she could still mount on the first night. By the third, Amastan and Kailan had to lift her. She allowed it without speaking. At noon her hand shook against the rim of her cup. I set the machine between my knees and held the cup while she drank. When it was empty she said, ``Mr Morgan, if that appears in your account, at least spell my name correctly.''\par

``I always do.''\par

She gave me the cup to put away. Kailan was tightening the sling. Its lower edge had rubbed through a fold of her trousers, and Amastan brought another strip of cloth to put beneath it.\par

He shortened each passage, moved the longest climb into the coldest hour and assigned Kailan to the left side of her animal where the ground fell away. Sarah objected once.\par

``We have already lost half a day.''\par

``That climb takes both your hands. You could not close the left one this morning.''\par

She opened it for him. The fingers stopped short of the palm when she tried to close them again. He went on adjusting the cloth. We left at the hour he had chosen.\par

Kailan called the folds the Mountain's outer wall, the last country no state had crossed. Amastan was tying a load. He asked Kailan to hold it while he passed the strap underneath.\par

``Keep your hand where it is,'' Amastan said.\par

Kailan held the load. Amastan drew the strap through and let him take his hand away. Sand came out of the fold they had lifted.\par

During the daylight hides I recorded speech. At night I let the machine turn for a few minutes at each change of ground: soft hoof-fall on sand, iron striking buried stone, wind through a slit too narrow to see. Then I stopped it and wrote the hour on the band around the reel. I numbered every interval I did not record. When I opened the battery compartment, Amastan held a corner of the ground-cloth over my hands until I had shut it again.\par

On the fourth night the five strokes reached us without paper or instrument. I felt the first through my feet. Sarah's animal raised its head. Four more followed at equal distances, too low to hear and distinct through the ground. Amastan put his fingers to the rock. Sarah counted. We covered the metal fittings, gave the animals the final water and went forward on foot.\par

\ornament

We reached the camp overlook in the last dark of the fourth night. Amastan's companions took the animals into a high cleft while he brought us through a dry wash and stopped us below its rim. He pressed a palm to the ground.\par

``From there onward, their instruments can hear a stone roll.''\par

He took Jean-Charles forward on his belly and showed him a seam between two listening posts. When they returned, Jean-Charles asked for the route.\par

``I take you through the first turns,'' Amastan said. ``At the last bend you go alone. If they see me, they will watch the way I came.''\par

Jean-Charles removed his coat, covered the pale skin of his hands and face, and tightened the cloth at his collar. He saw me watching.\par

``Still taking notes on me, professor?''\par

``Your questions at the safe house are on the record. So is your request that Kailan keep them from Elise.''\par

He pulled the cloth across his mouth. ``And Sarah heard him tell it. She still put the field movement in my hands.''\par

``I have recorded that too.''\par

``Good. Keep the machine dry.''\par

Amastan led him over the rim. He came back alone. Jean-Charles went down toward the camp before the light.\par

We waited on cold rock while grey light entered the pan, exposing dishes, tents, vehicles and a drilling rig one degree at a time. Two flecks crossed the high sky without sound. Sarah named the times at which they would return. The dish beside the command tents moved after each passage and stopped on the same bearing.\par

Men occupied the ridges and the throats of the approaches. Through the glass I saw their faces turned inward, with tripods among them pointed into the rock. Survey stakes made straight red lines across the broken ground. A western truck covered an old drovers' track to the Oasis. Sarah said Shield had allowed the track to pass out of use because no Outsider had found its northern mouth. The truck stood over the place she indicated.\par

``They were told,'' Kailan said.\par

``Yes.'' Sarah pointed below the rig. ``That bore is west of your Hall and deeper. What lies there?''\par

``The reserve.''\par

``Water?''\par

He nodded.\par

At the camp's centre stood a double ring of tents with separate generators. Long vehicles waited beside them, each fitted to carry a body flat. Men from the command tents crossed the open ground with covered papers and returned by different paths. During our watch the medical orderlies were the people who passed into the double ring. One carried a folded stretcher upright to get it between two vehicles.\par

Sarah kept her eye to the glass. ``A good half of what you see was written in my hand.'' She followed the orderly until he went inside a tent.\par

``Some part of me wanted the Mountain found. I wanted not to be the last one holding it.'' She lowered the glass into the space between us. It lay there until Jean-Charles returned.\par

We heard no shot. Before direct sun reached the wash, Jean-Charles appeared in it with a man bent in front of him. Amastan left cover, chose every step of their ascent and brought them through the listening ground. The prisoner wore the camp's grey clothes. His wrists were bound; a gag held his mouth. Jean-Charles carried a flat hard case and a black field radio.\par

``Post nine,'' he said. ``The seam is open beyond it. Not for long.''\par

He put the case on the rock. Sarah reached for the catches. I asked her to leave them closed while I recorded the custody.\par

``He took it from the camp,'' she said.\par

``I need the place, the hour and the condition in which he found it. His access is part of the examination.''\par

She took her hand away. Jean-Charles gave the command tent and the locked drawer. I repeated his name and inspected the catches. He waited until I had finished. I opened the case beside the machine and described the contents before moving a sheet.\par

The papers carried one stock, one sequence of punch holes and consecutive issue marks. Corrections on separate sheets had been made in the same ink and impressed the leaves beneath them. I held two pages against the light to match an impression, then placed them back in order.\par

``The leaves were together when these corrections were made,'' I said. ``I cannot certify their contents.''\par

Sarah found a small mark in the upper corner of the first sheet.\par

``French strategic service. A compartment descended from the arrangements Martinez and my father made in this desert. The mineral company is a cover. This is a state operation.''\par

Sarah read the French entries aloud for us. The next leaves carried a joint Algerian groundwater and rare-earth concession. She judged the concession number, drilling permits and customs entries genuine. A distribution table sent fuel, airspace and explosives approvals through separate offices for separate survey blocks; none of the Algerian recipients received the central coordinates. A personnel annex counted three thousand and eighty-four people across nine contractors: drilling, transport, aviation, medicine, camp labour and security. Three hundred and eighty-six carried French service numbers.\par

Sarah read the operational headings and pointed out their places in the camp: seismic sounding, capture medicine, water control, approach denial, compartmented command. ``They can let an inspector follow the fuel from the concession to the drill and find a proper receipt at every stage. They know there is a mechanism under the rock. They do not know what it does. These are my survey blocks.'' She turned to the medical annex. ``And these are the people they intend to bring out of them.''\par

Three leaves into the logistics annex, I found a northern track listed among forty routes. Its local name was the one Sarah had used on the ridge. Sarah translated the note beside it, entered in the quartermaster's hand: \emph{source: internal, reliability high}.\par

I gave the page to Sarah and touched the line.\par

``After Martinez was shut out,'' I said, ``who held that corridor?''\par

``One channel.'' She kept the page on the rock between us.\par

``Jean-Charles?''\par

``Yes.''\par

``The entry does not name him. And he brought us the case.''\par

She folded the page once. ``Keep that qualification in your account. I am keeping this.''\par

She put it inside her coat. Jean-Charles remained beside the prisoner, watching the camp through the wash. He had not asked what we had found. I had stopped the recorder to read. There is a gap of four minutes while the case lay open behind him. When I started the reel again, I spoke the times and the reason for the gap.\par

The field radio lit. A young voice spoke in French. Sarah translated: post nine had missed its check; if Farid had crawled behind a rock to sleep again, he could answer to the sergeant. Jean-Charles covered the set until its light died.\par

Amastan cut the gag and gave Farid water. He held the skin for him because his wrists were tied behind his back. Water ran into the hollow above Farid's collar; when Amastan lowered the skin, Farid bent after it. Amastan let him drink again.\par

Sarah put our questions to him in French and gave us his replies. He said he guarded a mineral survey. He knew his own watch and the two posts beside it. He had been told there were ``primitives'' in the ground, a lost tribe to be handled \emph{without loss of life}. His sappers had orders to bring everything out intact and break nothing. He did not know who issued either order.\par

Kailan crouched before him. ``Who gave the order to take no life?''\par

Sarah translated the question, then the answer. ``A medical officer. He does not know his name.''\par

``The same officer who sets the charges?''\par

``No.''\par

``Who can stop the digging?''\par

``Operations. Not his command.''\par

``Who among them may speak to the men on the western ridge?''\par

Farid looked toward it. Sarah gave his answer: ``No one at his post. They have their own command.''\par

Kailan opened the case to the live-capture orders. Sarah read five priorities coded by task, with every name omitted. Kailan repeated three: the one who reads the deep water; the one who keeps the hours; the one who holds the seal. Sarah left the two remaining codes untranslated.\par

``Five functions,'' he said. ``They have understood that the mechanism is worked by five hands. They mean to seize the complete set alive and force it to answer to a new hand.''\par

Sarah put her finger beside the five entries. ``Can they?''\par

``The division was built so that no one could. They are coming to commit the one seizure it exists to prevent.''\par

Sarah showed Farid the codes. He shook his head at each. He pulled his wrists against the binding and looked again at the water skin.\par

The radio spoke. Sarah identified the sergeant from his answer to the young man. Lights came on at two posts.\par

``The road is open,'' Jean-Charles said. ``Amastan knows a seam beyond post nine their instruments do not yet cover. Farid's missed check has shortened our hour to minutes.''\par

One of Amastan's companions moved Farid's bindings to the front and led him into the high cleft. He carried the water skin with them. ``We keep him until your road is shut,'' Amastan said. ``Then he goes.''\par

Jean-Charles gave Sarah the case and me the field radio. ``We go now.''\par

We left the animals, blankets and empty water skins in the high cleft. I put the reel tins inside my coat and bound the recorder against my chest with its carrying strap. Sarah tried the station book inside her coat. Its spine kept her elbow out from her body. Kailan took the hard case while she lay flat and tried to draw the arm beneath her. She got up, tore out the crossed page of call signs and put it beside the annex inside her sleeve. The book remained under a stone.\par

Amastan returned from Farid's cleft and led us to the rim. Three lights moved along the wash Jean-Charles had used; another swept the ridge above post nine. Men called in pairs. The drilling continued beneath them, a regular iron turning that covered smaller sounds and then stopped without warning. Each time it stopped, we stopped with it. The case handle pressed into Sarah's wrist where she had looped it through her sleeve.\par

At the first turn Amastan placed our feet in his own marks. At the second he took Sarah's weight across a slab that sounded hollow when my knee touched it. At the third he pointed to a black cut no wider than a doorway.\par

``That cut reaches the old seam,'' he said. ``I stop here.''\par

Sarah gripped his wrist. ``Close the northern well.''\par

``It was closed before you came.''\par

``The other two?''\par

``My brother takes one. My sister takes the other.''\par

He looked at Kailan. ``Tell Shield who is closing them. Tell them the northern road has tyre cuts. They will have to ask us before they send anyone that way again.''\par

Kailan repeated the message. Amastan listened to the end, then withdrew over our path, lifting the marks with a brush of hide.\par

Jean-Charles entered the cut first. Kailan guided Sarah behind him; I followed. We moved on elbows where the roof came down. The radio against my ribs gave one burst of French through its cloth. Everyone stopped. Sarah whispered the order it carried: posts eight through twelve were to close the western arc and find Farid. Jean-Charles reached back, took the set and turned one control until the voice vanished.\par

``Did you answer?'' Sarah whispered.\par

``No.''\par

``Could you have?''\par

``I know the language of the post. I do not know the man's alarm word.''\par

He passed it back. I put the set inside its cloth with the control facing away from my ribs. Sarah drew the case forward, pushed it flat and crawled after it.\par

The cut opened above a slope of broken shale. Across it stood post nine: two men, a low tent and a tripod turning by degrees. Beyond the post, the ground met the Mountain in an unbroken face. I could see no seam. A white lamp moved along the base of the rock, withdrew and returned.\par

Jean-Charles counted the tripod's turns with one finger. On the fifth he took us across. For twelve paces the shale shifted under every knee. We placed each stone in the space the hand ahead had left. Sarah's breath became audible. Kailan stopped and drew her arm across his shoulders. The tripod turned away. We moved again.\par

A voice challenged from the post. Jean-Charles put us flat and answered in French. I have no French, and he was not loud. The voice asked a second question. He gave a shorter reply. A man stepped beyond the tent and raised his lamp toward us. At that moment the drilling rig began again. Jean-Charles pulled Sarah to her feet and ran bent beneath her arm. Kailan took her other side. I went after them with the recorder striking my ribs. The lamp crossed the shale behind us and passed over the moving stones.\par

The rock face remained whole until Jean-Charles put his hand into a shadow below it. His arm disappeared to the wrist. Kailan knelt, cleared three handfuls of gravel and uncovered a narrow bronze plate with five shallow cups. He pressed the outer two, the centre, then the outer two again. Nothing moved.\par

``Again,'' Sarah said.\par

``The return sign changes with the hour.''\par

She held the case against her chest and waited. Kailan set the pattern a second time and held the centre cup while Jean-Charles pressed his shoulder to the stone. A line opened without sound. Cold air came through it. Two hands reached from the dark, seized Sarah and pulled her inside. Kailan passed the case through. Jean-Charles pushed me after it.\par

The white lamp found the bronze plate as he entered. A shot struck the rock above the opening. The hands inside pulled him clear; the seam closed on a fall of dust. No weapon answered from within.\par

For several breaths I heard our breathing and the recorder turning under my coat. Someone close to me coughed. Then a lamp was uncovered.\par

\ornament

Va-Sheva met us in a low stone chamber inside the skin of the Mountain. She wore Shield grey; two wardens stood behind her and a lamp was on the floor. Her eyes passed over Kailan, Jean-Charles, Sarah, me, the case under Sarah's arm, the field radio and the recorder. She placed one warden at the seam and one at the inner door. When she turned back into the lamplight, I could see the swelling beneath her robe. She was with child.\par

The wardens searched us. Jean-Charles laid down a pistol, two knives, a length of black cord and three objects I could not name. Sarah showed them the folded papers and kept the pages in sight. Kailan carried no weapon. When a warden reached for the recorder, I asked him to state his name and office before he broke its custody. Va-Sheva stopped his hand.\par

``It stays closed,'' she said. ``Shield will hear what it contains with Justice present, or destroy it with the man who carried it.''\par

The warden wrapped the field radio and hard case separately and set both inside a stone recess without opening them. Sarah kept the folded pages. Blood from the shale had soaked through one knee of her trousers. Va-Sheva pointed to it.\par

``After the hearing,'' Sarah said.\par

``Bind it while she speaks,'' Va-Sheva told the warden. ``If she falls, carry her. Do not stop her answer.''\par

The warden brought a strip of cloth and knelt. Sarah lifted the two folded pages clear of his hands. He pulled her trouser leg above the knee and began to bind it.\par

``You were sent out to learn whether the enemy within was real,'' Va-Sheva said to Kailan. ``Shield's writ sent you, sealed by the council that would not keep you. You return with two Outsiders the Mountain has never weighed. One made the net now closing on us. You bring a machine full of our speech up a seam no Veil is shown, during an attack the Mountain does not know has begun. By the law I hold, I should kill you at this lamp, kill them beside you and seal the seam over all four.''\par

``I made the net,'' Sarah said. ``I can show you where they have put the bores.''\par

The warden drew the cloth tight. Sarah stopped, took a breath and gave the depth. Va-Sheva asked the angle, the number of rigs and the interval between the five strokes. Sarah answered each. The warden tucked the end of the bandage under its last turn.\par

Kailan said, ``Hear her before you close the seam.''\par

``Jean-Charles vouches for the breach,'' Va-Sheva said to him.\par

``He does.''\par

``I have worked beside him.'' She faced Sarah. ``I will have each measure again before Justice. Give the limits of what you know with it.''\par

She ordered the inner warden to summon Shield and Justice, then looked at the recorder. ``That remains in your hand, Outsider. If it opens, you die before the lid clears.''\par

``Understood,'' I said.\par

``Under necessity of Shield, I admit the four of you as far as the sealed floor and no farther. The seam you used is dead from this hour. If your warning is false, I answer for opening it. If it is true, we count every other answer after the Mountain is still standing.''\par

She gave the order. The warden at the seam drove a bronze pin into the five-cup plate. From inside the wall came three descending blows. The pin's head was flattened against the bronze. The warden took his hammer away.\par

Va-Sheva turned back to Kailan. ``I contained you to keep you breathing. You will stay where I can see you until Justice takes you from me.''\par

Kailan bowed his head once. She took the lamp and led us upward. Sarah tried her weight on the bound knee, put the folded papers back inside her sleeve and followed.\par

At each crossing Va-Sheva gave a low word to a warden. One went downward, another ran ahead, a third stayed behind with the field radio and hard case, neither opened. Every question she asked went to Sarah.\par

``How many bores?''\par

``Three active. One deep. Two shallow.''\par

``How long has the deep one worked?''\par

``Not less than six days. Not more than nine.''\par

``Can you stop it from this side?''\par

``Not by meeting the bit. You may collapse a throat ahead of it, if you know the rock and can lose the chambers beyond. You may foul a water bore, but then you lose the water. You may let them enter a false void and close it behind them. I need the survey.''\par

Va-Sheva sent a warden for the keepers of the north-western galleries and another for the oldest survey kept by Stone. She asked whether the deep reserve shared a wall with the lower wards, and how many people a capture hospital of that size could hold. Sarah gave what she could and asked for the width of a passage. When she needed to draw, a warden handed her a black marking stone. She set the two folded pages between her teeth and put her free hand against the wall.\par

Lines appeared beneath the marking stone: ridge, rig, angle, water, Hall. Va-Sheva held the lamp while Sarah changed a line. Sarah took the papers from her mouth and gave the uncertain distance. Va-Sheva looked over the completed marks and wiped them out with her sleeve. Black dust remained in the folds of the grey cloth.\par

``The council must hear all of it,'' she said. ``You. Her. The papers. The machine. Justice will require an ordered account. All five cast you out, Kailan. I am carrying you back to their sealed floor with two Outsiders, while the men outside put charges into our rock.''\par

``Have the papers brought after us,'' Kailan said. ``Sarah has kept one leaf from the case. The other page came from her station book.''\par

Sarah touched her sleeve. Va-Sheva gave the order to the warden at the crossing.\par

Where the stair turned, Sarah and Jean-Charles had gone several steps ahead. Va-Sheva lowered her voice. I was one step behind Kailan with the recorder against my ribs.\par

``Va-Elise wrote that you did not speak for eleven days after containment. Is it true?''\par

``Yes.''\par

``Could you drink?''\par

``When she put the cup in my hands.''\par

``Sleep?''\par

``Not enough.''\par

Va-Sheva raised the lamp to see the next step. ``I would have to give the same order.''\par

Kailan stayed below her. She lowered the lamp a little.\par

``Do not ask me for more tonight,'' she said. ``I need to keep you alive long enough to answer them.''\par

She climbed after the others. Kailan waited until the space of one step had opened between them, then followed.\par

The route crossed an open stair above the common wards. Lamps burned over food stalls. A woman walked twelve children in pairs; the smallest held the back of the child's tunic ahead of him and dragged one sandal. A seller weighed roots in a hanging pan while two men disputed his weights. Someone beat dust from a mat over a rail and earned a shout from the level beneath. Water ran in a channel cut through the centre of the floor.\par

No one looked upward as we came onto the stair. I had spent two years comparing tool marks, copied signs and the mineral residue on objects Sarah put before me. Below us a man mended a pot with wire. He held its broken halves between his knees, passed the wire through a drilled hole and turned the pot to reach the other side. His cup stood outside the circle of fragments at his feet.\par

At a table under three lamps, a baker pressed his thumb into rounds of dough, turned each one and pressed again. The bread reached us before we crossed the centre of the stair: yeast, warm grain and the scorched flour at the edge of an oven. I knew charred cakes from sites nine thousand years old. I knew the pits in which the grain had been stored, the querns on which it had been broken and the changes in human bone after people began to eat it every day. The baker struck flour from his hands and called to a child to carry six loaves home.\par

The recorder knocked against the rail. Several faces lifted. Va-Sheva caught my sleeve before it struck again.\par

``They do not know,'' she said. ``They will not learn it from you on a stair. Walk.''\par

The child with the six loaves had stopped beneath us. One loaf slipped against his chest; he trapped it with his chin and laughed. I drew the machine close under my coat and walked. At the upper turn the stalls disappeared. The smell remained for twelve steps more.\par

\ornament

The first blow took the stair sideways.\par

Stone struck my shoulder. The recorder drove into my ribs beneath my coat. A lamp went out below; another rolled in a yellow circle until a hand caught it. Then the sound arrived through the floor: one flat concussion, a single force driven into the Mountain without the patient interval of the five-stroke sounding. Dust fell from the joints overhead. Far inside the rock, worked stone ground against worked stone.\par

Sarah put both hands on the wall. ``A charge. Down a shallow bore, close enough to break a void or test what breaks around it. North-west, near the surface. What lies there?''\par

Va-Sheva had turned toward the sound. She stood with the lamp held clear of the wall.\par

``What lies there?'' Sarah repeated.\par

``Faith,'' Va-Sheva said. ``A Faith passage. The road to the Hall.''\par

She ran. Jean-Charles went with her and the nearest warden. Sarah called for a lamp, a plan of the north-western arc and the keeper of Stone. The ward children below had begun to cry. The seller's hanging scale knocked against its post with each movement underfoot.\par

Kailan put his palm to the wall. I put mine beside it. A tremor travelled through the stone, stopped, then returned deeper and slower. Grit moved under my fingers. From beyond the turn came a sharper failure and Va-Sheva shouting for the passage to be cleared.\par

I lifted the recorder toward the grinding. Its reels turned. Kailan took the lamp left on the stair. We ran after the others.\par
