\typesetchapter{Chapter 21}{Clock}

The stair went down a long way, and at the bottom of it there was not a library.\par

I had expected a library. Paul had expected a library. We had come down braced for shelves, for the smell of a great many books. What waited at the bottom was a machine, and it was moving.\par

We stood on the last step and did not go down into it at first, the four of us. Shield's lamps found the near part of it and lost the far part in a dark that swallowed light the way deep water does. And there was a sound, very low, under the cold, a sound I had heard my whole life and never once been able to place, and I placed it now with a lurch in the chest: it was the almost-nothing the wards call the breath of God, and it was this. This room. This slow turning. The machine that had been running under every prayer I had ever said, a mile of stone above me, every day of my life.\par

The chamber was vast, and cold with the deep cold, and lit, when Shield brought the lamps, by a light that was not all ours: for far above, through a shaft cut with the patience of my people, a thin true line of the outside came down, dust-filled, careless, and fell across the middle of the floor, and I knew that light. I had stood in it once, at the Oasis, when I was a Candidate and stole a look at the sun. It was the equinox beam, the same shaft that gives the Festival of Equal Light its one honest moment, brought down here to fall on the heart of the machine. And where it fell, it fell on a great wheel of stone, taller than a man, notched around its rim like the edge of a gear, and as we stood there in the cold and watched, the wheel moved. One notch. A single tooth of it climbed past a fixed stone pawl with a sound I felt in my teeth, and settled, and was still. And I understood, because it was my office to know what light is made to do, that I had just watched the Mountain count a year.\par

\ornament

``It is a clock,'' Sarah said, in the flat voice she used when a thing was too large to be said any other way. ``It does not count the hours. It counts the years. Look at the beam.'' She walked into the equinox light and let it lie across her hands. ``That shaft only holds true for a few days around the equinox, when the sun stands where it stands. Every year, on that day, the light comes down straight and touches a mark, and the mark tells the machine to take its step, and corrects it if the step has drifted. A pendulum keeps a day. This keeps a year, and trues itself against the sky so that it can never be wrong. Your Festival of Equal Light is the winding of the oldest clock on Earth. That is what you have been singing at.''\par

Paul had gone to the wheel and was looking at its rim, at the notches, at the fixed pawl, at the long spiral of teeth that climbed away from it into the dark of the chamber and, I saw now, wound around and around the walls, tier on tier, a stone screw of years going up into a height the lamps could not reach. ``Then we can read it,'' he said, very quietly. ``A clock that has taken one step a year, and never missed, and never lied, because the sky would not let it lie. We do not have to believe anyone's scripture about how old this place is. We can count the teeth.''\par

``And the Festival,'' I said, because it had opened under me like a trapdoor. ``Twice in my life I have knelt in that beam with the whole Mountain and been told it was the moment God looks down and finds us balanced. Children are named in it. My mother wept in it.'' I looked at the shaft of dusty light lying across the turning wheel. ``It was never God looking down. It was a man, a hundred thousand years dead, reaching up through a hole he cut in a mountain to lay his finger on his clock and make certain it had not lost a day.'' And Sarah, standing in the beam, said, without any cruelty, ``Yes. And it is the most sophisticated thing I have ever seen a human mind make. He built a religion whose rites are the tending, so the tending would outlive everyone who understood it. Your Mountain has kept this clock perfectly for a hundred thousand years precisely because it forgot it was a clock.''\par

\ornament

It took the better part of two days, and it broke something in every one of us that came down to do it.\par

They counted the teeth. Paul and Sarah and Sa-Tavan of Justice, with tallies and chalk and a discipline I could only watch, went up the spiral of the years on ladders and scaffolds Shield rigged for them, marking every hundredth tooth, then every thousandth, calling the running count down into the cold, and I stood below and did what was mine to do: watch for the places where the machine had been changed, because a change made on purpose leaves a shadow, and I have spent my life seeing shadows.\par

It is a strange labour, counting to a hundred thousand. The mind cannot hold the whole; you become, for two days, only a hand that marks a tooth and a mouth that says a number, and the number stops meaning anything. And Sa-Tavan of Justice climbed with them and set the tally into the wet clay of his office. They worked in shifts and slept in the cold at the foot of the machine rather than climb back up into a Mountain that would ask them what they had found. I carried them food and water and stood my watch below, reading the shadows, and twice in the two days I called a halt because I had seen a seam, and both times I was right, and both times it was worse than the time before.\par

And Sarah checked the count against the world, because a count you cannot check against anything is only a story with numbers in it. She read the wear on the pawl, the exact bright groove eleven hundred centuries of identical small blows had cut into the fixed stone, and said the number of blows agreed with the teeth. She read the dust, the fine slow fall of the deep, layered on the ledges of the machine year on year like the rings in a tree, and counted the layers where they were thickest and said they agreed. She read the drift of the very light, the way the place on the wall the equinox beam touched had crept, over the count, by exactly the amount the slow wobble of the turning world should move it across such a span, and said that agreed too, and that was the one no forger could have faked, because it required knowing a thing about the Earth that no one on Earth but her, perhaps, now knew. Three separate clocks, the teeth, the dust, and the sky, and they told the same time, and the time they told was not eleven thousand years.\par

``Say it,'' I said to her, when I saw her face at the foot of the ladder, because she had reached the count and could not seem to speak it, and Sarah Blackwood being unable to speak a number was a thing I had not seen. ``Say it. I have been told a lie my whole life and I would rather have the truth from you than the lie from my fathers.''\par

``A hundred and forty-five thousand years,'' she said. ``Give or take what such a number gives or takes. Your eleven thousand years are the tail end of it. Your Mountain is older than my whole science believed people capable of building anything. It is older than the oldest thing my world had ever found and called the dawn of us. It has been standing in this rock, counting, keeping that beam true against the sky, since a time my people place ourselves barely learning to bury our dead.'' She looked up the spiral of the years into the dark. ``I chased this for sixty-six years and called myself its discoverer. I am a child who found a footprint and thought she had invented the foot.''\par

``It is worse than being late,'' Paul said from the ladder, meaning to comfort her and doing the opposite. ``Everything we know out there rests on the idea that there was nothing before a certain shallow floor: that minds able to do this'' --- he laid his hand on the machine --- ``could not have existed, that the deep past was empty of us. And the deep past was full. There was a civilisation down here that could aim an earthquake through the core of the Earth before my ancestors had a word for the moon, and it is not in a single one of our books, because it made certain it would not be. We did not fail to find them. We failed to imagine them.''\par

\ornament

I had learned deep time once before, from an Outsider, before I ever met one. In the first days of my captivity, when Paul was still only a voice on a machine to me, I had been shown a man in a red cap who took me down a canyon wall in pictures, layer under layer, and told me that a rock lying low is a rock lying old, and that the floor was older than my whole scripture and older again than that. \emph{These immense periods of time baffle the imagination}, the man had said, and I had thought, in my pride, you don't say, I who sat among the ten who mattered in a world eleven thousand years old. I had taken it as a wonder about the Earth, a thing about rocks and dead beasts, far from me, safely far.\par

Now I stood at the bottom of my own canyon, and every layer of it was a year my people had counted and hidden, and it stood no distance from me at all. It was the stone I was standing on. The immense period of time that baffled the imagination was the length of the lie I had been raised inside. And I understood, at last, what the canyon had tried to teach me: that a thing which calls itself eternal tells you the one lie that is never true, and that everything, the rock and the beast and the Mountain and the god, has a floor, and a first day, and if you go down far enough you will find it, and it will have been made.\par

The Keepers took the number the way people take a death, which is to say each alone, and none of them well. Ka-Leth of Balance, who counted everything, did not count this; he sat down on a stone ledge and said, after a long while, ``A hundred thousand years of stores drawn and replaced. A hundred thousand years of the water held level. And I thought my office ancient at eleven.'' Ka-Dhavar of Shield looked up the spiral into the dark and I watched him understand, before the rest of us, what the seams would mean. And Ka-Raedin of Light, my own Keeper, came and stood beside me in the equinox beam, the two of us of the office of light standing in the one honest light in the whole Mountain, and said, low, so that the others would not hear, ``You knew. Not the number. But you always knew there would be a floor. It is why you pulled the cord. The rest of us needed a hundred thousand years of teeth to be taught the one thing you were simply born unable to un-see.'' It was as near as he ever came to asking me to forgive him, and I gave it the only way that hour allowed, by saying nothing at all, and letting him stand in the light beside me as though he had the right.\par

But the teeth of the clock were not smooth all the way up. Five times, going up the spiral, the count broke.\par

Paul found the first break and would not believe it until Sarah had climbed to it and put her hands on it, and then they called me up, because it was mine to read what had been done on purpose, and I climbed the scaffold into the cold upper dark and saw it. The spiral of the years ran up, tooth on tooth, and then, at a certain height, there was a seam. The teeth above the seam were cut by a different hand, to a hair a different depth, the way the jamb of the Archive door had been cut differently from the Mountain around it. And set into the seam was a mark, a deep deliberate figure, and Sa-Tavan of Justice, who had come up behind me white and silent, said, in a voice I had not heard him use, ``That is the reset mark. I know that mark. It is on the Cataclysm gravure. It is the mark for the Beast.''\par

Five times. Five seams in the count, five reset marks, five places where the clock of the years had been stopped and started again by a hand that had just fired the Beast, and above each seam a new count began, a new age, a new eleven or twelve or twenty thousand years of teeth, running up until the next seam, the next mark, the next ending. The Mountain had not stood in peace for a hundred and forty-five thousand years. It had ended the world five times, and ridden out each ending in the sealed dark, and come out, and let the world rebuild, and waited, and when the rebuilt world found it again and reached for it again, it had ended the world again. Five times before. And each time, I understood, looking at the fresh seam at the very top of the spiral, the one still bright, the one whose count read eleven thousand and some, each time it had wound its own memory back to nothing, and taught the new children that the world was eleven thousand years old and had never ended and never would, so that they would carry the terror pure, and never know they were the sixth to carry it.\par

``The Sixth Cycle Archive,'' Paul said, from below, and I heard the whole book of it close in his voice, every strange phrase we had passed and not understood. ``That is what this is. Not the sixth room of an archive. The archive of the sixth cycle. There have been five before. We are the sixth. It says so on the door and no one down here for a hundred thousand years has been allowed to read it.''\par

We stood with the fact of the five a long time, and it was Sarah who said the thing that made it unbearable, because Sarah had spent her whole life at the far end of exactly this and could see the shape of the whole. ``Do you understand what the five mean,'' she said. ``They do not only mean it has happened before. They mean it works. Five times the world out there has climbed all the way back up from a made winter to a thing that could build a drill and a satellite and a state, and five times you have knocked it down to ash. Everything my kind ever built, every city, every book, every bomb, was the sixth climb. And you have just ended it. The sixth, by the count on that wheel. And unless something changes in this room, in us, tonight, there will be a seventh, that climbs a hundred thousand years out of the ash we have just made, and finds this door, and fires it again, and never once knows that it was the seventh, exactly as you did not know you were the sixth.'' She looked at the bright new seam, our seam, the one still pale. ``That is the machine. Six firings, and the same tooth coming round each time. It is a wheel. We are a spoke. And it means to turn forever.''\par

We stood with the wheel a long time, and then Sarah said one thing more, quieter, that I have come to weigh as heavily as the wheel itself. She had set our five endings, the depths of the five seams, against a record I had never heard of: for the outside keeps a year-count of its own, she told me, in the ice of the far south and the mud of the deep sea, layer on cold layer going back further than even this machine, and she alone in that room could read it. And only a few of our endings, she said, matched a cold century her science had never been able to explain; the rest of the world's winters, and its great dyings, and its scattered drowned ruins, were the world's own, and owed us nothing. \emph{Do not let this become the answer to everything,} she said. \emph{Your Mountain is the hidden hand behind a handful of bad centuries that the rest of us blamed on comets and gods, and no more of human history than that, and it is monstrous enough. The founder's whole sickness was that he could not let a thing be the size it was. Do not you take it up now, standing here with a hundred and forty-five thousand years under you and the whole of the dark inviting you to make yourself the centre of it.}\par

Va-Sheva had come down on the second day, with Jean-Charles, because Shield does not leave four Keepers and two Outsiders alone at the bottom of the world with a machine that ends it, and she had listened to Sarah's wheel and her spoke without a movement of her face, and when Sarah finished she said one thing only, in the flat Shield voice, and it was the most frightened I ever heard her. ``Then Shield has watched the wrong direction for a hundred thousand years. We have watched the desert. We have watched the outside. And what turns the wheel was never out there at all. It is in this room, and in us, and in the plain fact that we will do it again.'' She looked at the seam. ``Shield cannot seal against Shield.'' And Jean-Charles, who had held a stair at the ending of one world and now stood at the foot of the record of six, said nothing whatever, and I believe it was the first thing in his life he had found no words to meet.\par

\ornament

I have said that each office carried its guilt in its own shape. Justice's shape was waiting for him at the top of the spiral of the years, and it was worse than any of ours, and I watched it break him, and I have never been sorry for a man the way I was sorry for Ka-Orun of Justice in that cold high dark.\par

Because Justice is the complete record. That is the office: that Justice remembers everything, edits nothing, keeps the true and unbroken account against which every act is weighed, so that no one in the Mountain can ever be judged by a lie or spared by a forgetting. Ka-Orun had ruled the ending of the world lawful out of the completeness of his record. He had staked five thousand lives and the whole outside world on the certainty that he held the true history and could reason from it. And he stood now in a chamber his office had never been permitted to know existed, in front of five erasures his own predecessors had made, at the top of a count that gave every Justice who had ever lived the lie about the one fact that mattered most, and he understood that his office had been, for a hundred and forty-five thousand years, the proudest instrument of the very erasure it was sworn against. It had passed the deception down, judge to judge, each one certain he held the whole, each one holding a hole.\par

``I ruled it lawful,'' he said. He said it to the seams, not to us. ``I stood over the Hall of Cataclysm and I found the condition met and I let the drop fall, and I did it because I believed I knew everything there was to know, because I am Justice and Justice knows. And I knew nothing. I did not know I was the sixth judge to be handed this exact case and told it was the first. Perhaps every one of them ruled as I ruled. Perhaps the ruling is part of the machine, the same as the drop and the beam, a thing built to come out the same way every time it is fed, and Justice is a part of it. The machine decides. Justice is the office that is made to feel it decided.'' He put his hand on the bright new seam, the sixth mark. ``I have spent my life certain that a thing done by law was done in the light. And I have found the room where they keep the light, and it was locked, and my own fathers locked it, and threw away the key, and taught me that the dark was the whole of the day.''\par

And then, because he was Justice to the last of himself, he did the hardest thing I have watched a man do. He took out the wet clay of his office, there at the top of the spiral of the years, and he began to record the erasures. Every seam. Every reset mark. The count of the teeth. The five hidden endings, and the sixth we had just made. He wrote down, in his own hand, into the permanent record, the proof that his own office had lied for a hundred thousand years, so that no Justice who came after him could ever again inherit the clean version of it. ``If I am only the office that is made to feel it decided,'' he said, pressing the marks into the clay, ``then let me at least decide the one thing the machine did not build me to decide, which is to write down that I was fooled. Let the seventh judge, if a seventh is ever made, inherit at least this much: that he is not the first. It is the only mercy I have left to give a man I will never meet. And it is more than one of my fathers ever gave to me.''\par

No one answered him, because there was no answer, and because Justice does not want comfort; Justice wants the record; and the record, for once, was more than he could bear to keep.\par

It was among the ledgers, on the last of the two days, that I found the man.\par

For there were ledgers, at the base of the machine, in a dry recess the equinox beam never touched, sheet on sheet of a material that was not paper and not stone, in a keeping so perfect that the oldest of them were as sound as the newest. The others took them for the foundation liturgy, the oldest and most sacred and least meaningful of the holy texts, the drone under the doctrine. Sarah and Paul could not read them; the script of the lowest sheets was a tongue no living mouth had spoken, drifted so far from Mountain speech across a hundred thousand years that even Paul, who could hear a language change under his hands, could only say it was the root of everything above it and nothing he could translate. So they set the oldest aside as beyond us, and turned to the newer ledgers, and I was left alone with the base of the machine, and with the drone, and with my one gift, which is the seeing of what has been placed on purpose.\par

And I saw it. Under the liturgy, inside it, carried down through a hundred thousand years of copying by scribes who thought they were preserving the meaningless holy base and were in fact preserving a smuggled thing, there was a change of voice. The liturgy is a chant; it has no I in it; scripture never does, meant to sound as though no one wrote it. And here, threaded through the oldest chant, in a slightly different hand, on a slightly different material, ordered by a hidden rule that only showed itself once you stopped reading it as prayer and started reading it as a thing a person had arranged, there was an I. Someone was speaking. Someone had hidden himself in the sacred drone beneath the god, and had waited a hundred and forty-five thousand years for a reader with an eye trained to see what is put in front of him on purpose, which is the exact eye my people had spent that whole time trying to breed and beat and exile out of existence, and had failed, once, in me, on the one night it was needed.\par

I read the first sentence of the I aloud, and it was not holy, which was the surest sign it was true: it carried the one thing scripture never carries, a person wanting something. \emph{Let it be recorded, by the one who will not be recorded elsewhere, that what follows was chosen and not received; that a mind stood behind it; and that the mind was mine.} Paul made a sound. Sarah made none. Sarah had gone entirely still, the stillness of a hunter who has followed a track for sixty-six years and come, at the end of it, to a glass standing where the animal should have been. ``He is defending himself,'' she said, very quietly. ``Listen to it. The first words in a hundred thousand years to come out from under the god, and they are a defence. He knew. He knew it was wrong, or that it would be named wrong, and he wrote this to argue his case to whoever finally dug him out. He built the Mountain to keep the secret, and then he could not bear to be the secret, so he hid himself where no believer would ever look, and left his confession for the only reader who could reach it: an unbeliever with a trained eye. And he signed it as the first of a line.''\par

``There is a man in here,'' I said, into the cold, and they came, all of them, down off the scaffolds and out of the ledgers, and gathered around me in the equinox light, four Keepers and two Veils and a witness and a woman who had chased this her whole life, and I read them what I had found, the first words of the I under the god, where the man who built the Mountain had signed his work and told the future who he was.\par

He gave himself no title. He gave himself a designation, cold and exact, the way a man names the first of a series he means to be long. And the designation was the two words that Paul's machine had already caught once, weeks before, off a Shield drawer, and that Sarah had chased across a desert, and that the whole book of my life had been walking toward without my knowing it.\par
