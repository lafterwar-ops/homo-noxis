\typesetchapter{Chapter 2}{Mirror}

How a boy raised to worship stone became a man who could take it apart: I'm still wondering where it all began.
It began, if it began anywhere, the morning after they cut my hand --- in a low chamber panelled throughout in mirror and dark speculum-stone, over a shallow bowl of standing water that no one had thought worth the guarding.
Nobody hands you the thing that ruins you. They teach you some smaller, more decorous thing, and dress it with such care, and look away at precisely the wrong moment.\par

\ornament

I came to the practice halls of Light before Second Silence with an empty stomach gone sour on its own acids, a thumb bound in linen that tugged at the cut with every closing of the hand, and the private, almost devout conviction that I would not, a second time, be made to look a fool.\par

The day before, I could have crossed those thresholds only as Kailan --- a boy under instruction, suffered near the outer rooms because his teachers had troubled to vouch for him.
That morning the gate no longer asked for sufferance.
It asked for record.
An attendant in a robe of dead, dustless grey read my new name from a tablet on which the characters had been incised so lately that a fine pale grit still lay unswept in the troughs of every stroke;
and \textit{Sa-Kailan}, in a stranger's mouth, had less the sound of an honour conferred than of a key turning, somewhere out of sight, in locks I had never been shown.\par

The stone changed under my sandals as I crossed from common passage into Light. It became smoother, colder, cut at shallow opposing angles, so that a step did not merely
sound but returned a faint image from below.
Even the darkness was tended here, assigned rather than left to gather where it pleased.
A corridor beyond the gate was crossed by thin bars of light that fell from slits too high to see and vanished into black cuts on the far wall.
Dust moved through them, visible, invisible, visible again, each mote existing only where the beam permitted it.\par

I slowed despite myself. Faith reached its chambers by devotion, Balance by measure, Shield by permission, Justice by record.
Light, I had always believed, worked differently. Light showed. A man did not need to believe a lamp; he had only to open his eyes.\par

Then a thing Va-Raedin had said in the Hall returned to me. \textit{Do not admire the flame. Ask where the air enters.}
I looked again at the shafts crossing the passage.
The obvious thing was their precision.
The better question was where they began, who had narrowed them, why half of them were received by slots and not stone, and what waited in the darkness that had been denied the beam.
I had not answered it by the time I reached the hall.\par

The Hall of Light was not one hall, though it had one name.
From one end it seemed broad and shallow; from the stair it narrowed to a blade of space, mirrors along one side, black apertures cut into the other.
From the candidate entrance, where I stood, it looked almost empty.
Then it began to give itself away.
Mirrors stood everywhere, but none where a child would have set them: tall strips no wider than a hand, small round discs at knee or shoulder,
and a few that were not mirrors at all but dark polished stone that returned shape without obedience.
Arcs and crosses and incomplete circles had been cut into the floor.
Three doorways stood at the far end.
One open and dark, one closed by bronze, one half-open with a line of brightness along its edge that I distrusted at once.\par

Va-Raedin of Light, stood at the centre, hands folded behind his back, pale grey robe, no ornament but the narrow silver thread at the cuff.
He looked rested.
I resented it.
The ceremony had lasted half a day, and he had stood through it without shifting, leaning, or submitting to any of the small disgraces by which bodies confess themselves.\par

``You are early,'' he said.\par
``You said before Second Silence.''\par
``I did.'' He looked at my bandage. ``Does the thumb hurt?''\par
``No.''\par
``It should.''\par
``It does a little.''\par
``Then begin with truth where truth is inexpensive.''\par

Heat moved into my face, and I said nothing.\par

He turned to the three doors. ``Which door opens?''\par

I nearly answered quickly, and disliked myself for the impulse.
The right doorway's brightness was so plainly a lure that the lure might be to distrust it too easily.
I studied the floor instead.
A thin strip of light lay at the base of the left doorway; the right's brightness touched nothing; the bronze was too solid, too central, too theatrical.\par

``The left,'' I said. ``The light at its base belongs to an opening. The right only reflects one. The middle is meant to make the eye feel opposed.''\par
``Open it.''\par

I crossed the hall and pushed.
Nothing.
Pulled.
Nothing.
I searched for latch, seam, hinge, weight.
Smooth stone answered me everywhere, and behind me Va-Raedin stayed quiet, which I hated more than correction.
I pressed the bronze door once, because anger sometimes insists on humiliating itself with the body, and it gave me nothing either.\par

``You asked which door opens,'' I said. ``Not which door appears open.''\par

``No.''\par

I looked again. Doors, mirrors, floor. 
The nearest cut circle was worn on one side only, as though candidates had learned to step into it from the same direction. 
So I stood inside it, and the room changed, not by moving but by being seen from the point for which it had been arranged. 
The mirrors no longer held the three doors. 
They caught a plain stone panel behind me, flush with the wall, unmarked by light. 
I found the seam by touch and pressed, and it opened inward.\par

I tried not to smile. He saw me trying.\par

``You are pleased because you found the fourth door.''\par
``I found the door that opens.''\par
``You found it after failing.''\par
``I corrected the failure.''\par
``That is better than defending it. It is not the same as succeeding.'' He came beside me. ``Name it.''\par
``That light deceives.''\par
``No. That is what boys say when a lamp embarrasses them. 
The lesson is that sight depends on position. 
From the entrance you saw three doors and chose among them. 
From the circle you saw the room that had arranged the choice. 
The hidden door was not hidden because stone wished to be secret. 
It was hidden because you accepted the first place given to your eyes.''\par

I have stood in more rooms since than I can count where that sentence was the whole of what was happening: someone deciding, quietly, which place to give my eyes.\par

``A Candidate learns where light falls,'' Va-Raedin said. 
``A Candidate who follows brightness serves whoever placed the lamp.''\par
``A Veil learns who placed it,'' I said.\par
``Eventually. If he stops being proud of finding doors built to be found.''\par

The correction landed because it was deserved, and I went into the dark chamber before I could be told, partly to escape my own face.\par

\ornament

The second room was narrower and colder, lit through slits so thin they should not have lit anything, so that the chamber glimmered in fragments. 
On a stone table lay a mirror shard, a black measuring rod, a bead of clear glass fixed to a white tile, a bowl of water, and two bronze wedges. 
Va-Raedin closed the hidden door, and the darkness thickened, then organised itself around the slits.\par

``Move the point of light onto the far wall.''\par
The far wall was blank.\par
``May I touch the lamp?''\par
``No.''\par
``The table?''\par

``You may touch what is on the table.''\par

I began with the shard. 
The nearest slit sent a fine blade of light too high to catch, and I angled the shard until the light jumped downward, bright and disobedient, crossed the black rod, and vanished. 
I moved the rod, then understood it was not an obstacle but a measure, its shadow reading the beam's angle more precisely than my eye. 
A bronze wedge held the mirror steady, and I walked the point along the wall until it woke the bead into a smeared glow and vanished again.\par

For minutes I worked in silence, at an exercise simple to understand and humiliating to perform. 
Each time the point steadied, the smallest movement of my wrist sent it running. 
My bandaged thumb made the shard hard to grip; I compensated with the wrong fingers, overcorrected, lost the beam, found it, and heard Va-Raedin breathe once through his nose. 
At last I set the mirror flat and used the water, tipping the bowl until its surface caught the beam and threw it upward, unstable but bright, 
and the point appeared on the wall, alive to every tremor in the bowl. I held my breath until the surface stilled.\par

``Acceptable,'' Va-Raedin said. I felt the word as insult and reward at once. ``Why was the water harder than the glass?''\par
``Because it moves.''\par
``Everything moves. Be precise.''\par

I looked into the bowl. The surface still carried the small ripples of my hand, and the point trembled in sympathy. 
``The mirror keeps its angle,'' I said. ``The water answers after the hand has stopped touching it.''\par

He was silent long enough for the answer to become visible to me as well.\par

``Better.
Water remembers disturbance. 
A careless hand leaves a pattern after the hand is gone, and light thrown off the pattern tells you something happened.''\par

``So what is unseen can be known by what it disturbs,'' I said.\par
``Sometimes. The water tells you a disturbance occurred. It does not always tell you what disturbed it. Young minds love to turn traces into stories.''\par

\ornament

By the time he led me on through more chambers it was past Third Measure, and beyond the walls of Light the Mountain had long since come to its full day: 
carts toward the food galleries, voices near the washing pools, the metal rhythm of a gate being tested by Shield. 
In one of them he crossed to a blackened panel and laid his hand flat upon it. 
``Where is the exit?'' 
The entrance behind us was one answer and therefore probably not the answer; his hand drew my eye to the panel, too large, too flat, no seam, unless soot hid the seam. 
I walked toward it. 
He removed his hand, and the panel became simply black stone, and I stopped.\par

``Why did you come here?''\par
``You drew attention to it.''\par
``Yes. And?''\par

I looked back. 
When he had stood at the panel, his body had blocked a low mirror behind him. With him gone, the reflection crossed the room and struck the left arch, 
showing that the floor beyond it did not continue. 
The false corridor had declared itself only when he stepped away.\par

``You hid the falsehood by standing in front of the light,'' I said.\par
``That is what a Keeper of Light does. I am a Veil.
I prevented you from seeing the condition that made the falsehood visible. That is not the same thing.''\par
``The distinctions are convenient.''\par
``They are survival. 
Light is not truth. 
It is a condition under which truth may become visible, falsehood persuasive, or danger manageable. 
A lamp in the wrong place blinds more thoroughly than darkness.''\par
``Then what is our charge?''\par
``To know the difference before others suffer for it.''\par

I did not ask how anyone could know the difference if Light itself could be arranged into lies. 
He would have approved the silence, which made keeping it mildly intolerable.\par

\ornament

The chamber that followed was lit by a single lamp on the floor, ringed by six mirrors on slender bronze stems, each facing the lamp at a different angle. 
Cut into the far wall, in plain letters:\par

\begin{scripture}
Where light falls is only the first question.
\end{scripture}

``Place them so the lamp finds the door,'' he said. 

I wasted minutes on the brightest patch before I understood it was bait, a reflection of a reflection, brightness doubled and direction corrupted. 
I tried again, tracing the order this time: lamp to mirror, mirror to wall, wall to absence, absence to edge. 
My pride objected to crouching before my sense accepted that height was another form of ignorance. 
From the floor I saw that one mirror reflected not the lamp but the gap between two brighter paths. 
I shifted it toward the gap. 
A pale line sharpened on the wall; another mirror sent it downward; a seam appeared at ankle height. 
Not a door for a man. A panel.\par

By the time the panel opened, my knees ached and the linen on my thumb had darkened with new blood. 
Inside lay a small bronze token, heavier and finer than anything a boy is usually given to keep. 
I took it at once for a sign of Light, and at once could not place it, for it was no lamp such as children draw, and no mark I had been shown in any hall: 
a broad disk at the centre, and round the disk ring upon ring, close-set and perfectly even, spreading to the rim like the rings a dropped stone leaves in a still pool.\par

Only much later would I understand why such a sign was never carved above a door or worked into a robe --- 
that a mark of Light which looked so like water would have set the wrong question moving in the wrong heads --- 
and that it was given only into the hands of those who had opened the rooms, and shown to no one else. 

What I did not understand that morning, did not so much as suspect, was that the rings were not the secret of it. 
At the very centre, where the disk sat solid, there was a thing I saw only later.\par

``Take it,'' he said. It was heavier than it looked. 
``Proof that you opened the room. 
Keep it, do not lose it, and do not show it; it is not easily made, and I will not cut you another.''\par

I turned it once between finger and thumb, made nothing of the rings, and put it away.\par

\begin{room}

--- The token. Go back to it for me. What was cut into it --- exactly?

--- A disk at the centre. Around the disk, rings: many, close-set, even, spreading out to the rim. Like the rings a stone leaves on a still pool, but held in the odd pale colour and never moving.

--- A token of light, looking like ripples of water you say? One moment please, it should not be long.

\centerline{[\,---\,]}

--- Forgive me. That took rather longer than I promised you. I have had a thing fetched up and printed --- here, on this sheet. Never mind for now what it is, or where it is from. Only tell me this: did the token look like that?

--- Yes. Exactly like that. Except for the fact that yours is red, and mine was just pale upon pale.

--- \ldots{}

--- Paul?

--- Well. All right. All right. --- And when he set it in your hand, did you know what it was? What it meant?

--- Not then. I did not. But I would come to learn it, later.

\end{room}

A passage overlooked a lower work hall through narrow slits, and I saw that nothing there belonged to Light alone. 
Below, Faith singers rehearsed near a side arch, and no full song reached me through the stone, only a low pressure in the rail beneath my hand, 
as if the wall had taken the note and returned its body without its words. 
A thin mist rose behind the singers. 
It did not come from the floor. 
It emerged from a line of holes above the arch, so fine I had first taken them for pores in the rock, and a hidden lamp turned the vapour pale at the exact 
moment the singers lifted their faces.\par

Va-Raedin let me look. After a while: ``What do you see?''\par

``Water in the air. Light placed to make it visible. Timing.'' 
Below, a singer raised both hands, and the mist thickened with the movement as though the prayer had called it. 
The stone under my palm answered a breath after the bodies below. 
``Faith is using Light,'' I said. ``And does not know the hand that placed the lamp.''\par

He nodded once. 
And a thing entered me there, small and sharp, that I did not have the courage to say aloud: that a prayer could be sincere and placed with precision at the same time. 
That a blessing could be engineered without becoming false. 
If the people were comforted, was the comfort a lie? 
If God provided through valves, did the valve disprove Him, or serve Him? 
The mist thinned. 
The stone released its low answering hum. 
It had been beautifully done.\par

\ornament

It was almost Fourth Record, and the work had worn my attention down to a bright thin wire, when Va-Raedin stopped me in a passage between two dark chambers and 
did a thing he had not done all day. 
He asked me a question to which he plainly knew I had no good answer, and waited with the patience of a man who has all afternoon to watch another man fall.\par

``You have said a sentence since before you could reason,'' he said. 
``\textit{A Candidate learns where light falls.} Say it to me. Then tell me what it means.''\par

I said it. 
Then I told him what it meant. 
I said that a Candidate learns the Mountain's light as a mason learns its stone: every shaft and slit and lamp and aperture, 
where each beam began and where it was sent and what it touched, so that Light might be kept, and mended, and never fail the halls that leaned upon it. 
I gave a great deal of detail. 
I was rather pleased with the answer. 
It had the clean shape of competence, and it filled the silence, and I mistook filling the silence for having said something.\par

He let me finish.\par

\begin{room}

--- When first you gave me that sentence, I took it for a plain thing. That a Candidate is set to learn every beam in your Mountain --- each lamp, each shaft, each aperture --- for the upkeep of the place. A custodian's education. Was I wrong?

--- You have said very nearly the words I said, at fifteen, standing where you are sitting now. And I shall be no kinder to you than he was to me.

\end{room}

``That,'' Va-Raedin said, when I had run out of competence, ``is what a mirror would tell you, if you could teach a mirror to speak.''\par

He took one of the plain strips of glass from a niche in the passage, a hand's width, no more, and held it up between us, angled so that I saw my own face in it, badly, lit from beneath.\par

``What does this do?''\par
``It returns what falls on it.''\par
``Exactly. It returns what falls on it, and adds nothing, and keeps nothing, and understands nothing. It is perfectly faithful and perfectly empty. 
Three-and-twenty thousand festivals of equal light this Mountain has counted, and turned its mirrors to the sun at every one, and not a mirror among them 
is the wiser for a single festival.'' 
He did not lower the glass. 
``You have said your sentence a thousand mornings and never once thought it. 
You have been a mirror. You return the words exactly. You add nothing. You understand nothing.
Light is not a hundred tricks. The tricks are how a fool keeps a candidate busy for a year and calls it teaching. 
Light is doctrine. 
You will think about the words as hard as you have today thought about the angles, or you will be a very skilled mirror all your life, and never once an eye.'' 
He lowered the mirror at last. 
``Which will you be? A mirror, or an eye?''\par

\begin{room}

--- Festivals of equal light, and you number them in their thousands. Was that the whole of your measure of time? Had your people nothing finer than a feast twice in the year?

--- There was a clock. One, in a room I have not yet brought you to. It was nothing like yours; it kept no hours, and no mornings. It beat once, and once only, at each festival of equal light --- twice in the year, and never between. What cogs and weights turned inside it to keep it so patient, I was not privy to. I was never Light enough to be shown.

\end{room}

``Come,'' he said. ``I will show you where light falls.''\par

\ornament

He took me down by a stair I had not been shown, below the practice halls, to a door that was neither hidden nor barred, and that told me more than any lock could have: 
a thing so wholly kept that it had no need of keeping, because only three Light ranks in the Mountain were ever brought to its threshold, 
and not one of them would speak of it to anyone who had not stood there too. 
This, I would learn, was how Light kept everything it kept: not by guards, not by oaths sworn on pain of some named horror, not by any threat at all. 
It set no watch on its secrets. 
It simply did not bring you to a thing until you had paid its price --- and a man who has bled for a door does not fling it wide for one who has not. 
The cost was the lock; 
the earning was the seal; 
and no other was needed.\par

The room beyond was long and low and nearly dark, and cold with the particular cold of places the day is never let into. 
The wall we entered by was pierced from floor to ceiling with small round holes, hundreds upon hundreds of them, each no wider than a fingertip, 
and through every one came a single thread of light, so that the wall at my back, when I turned to it, was a field of faint stars shining the wrong way --- 
inward, into the room, out of the body of the Mountain.\par

I did not understand, at first, what the far wall was. 
It carried points of light as well, where the threads crossed the dark and came to rest; but they were not set in ranks or any honest order. 
They were scattered across the whole black face of the stone --- some in clusters, some alone, some steady and bright, some so faint I found them only by looking beside them. 
It might have been a night sky copied by a hand that did not believe in constellations.\par

Then Va-Raedin raised a shielded lamp to the wall's lower edge, and I saw the faint lines scored between the points, joining them, and the shapes those lines made, 
and my skin was cold before my mind had caught up, because I knew the shapes. 
I had walked them that same morning. 
The long gallery of Light. 
The stair we had just come down. 
The washing pools, in their two uneven rows. 
The food galleries. 
And there, low and to one side, the great round of the Hall of Ceremony, its ring of five, and at its heart a small blind place where no point shone at all, 
where nothing was permitted to fall.\par

It was the Mountain and its many floors, laid upon a wall in points of light.\par

``We call it the Mirror,'' Va-Raedin said. 
``Every beam in the Mountain ends here, and here is nothing but the ends of beams.''\par

I could not speak. 
I have stood before a great many things since, and been afraid of some and moved by others, but I am not certain I have ever again been struck so 
purely dumb by the plain fact of a made thing. 
It was the most beautiful object I had ever seen. 
I want that set down before anything else is, and set down exactly, because it is the part that will not be believed and the part that matters most: 
that a boy who had spent a single morning learning that light could be bent to a purpose was shown, at the end of it, every purpose at once --- 
the whole known world rendered in obedient fire on one wall --- and could have wept for the beauty of it.\par

``Watch,'' he said.\par

We stood a while, and the points held still --- most of them, most of the time --- and I did not understand why stillness should be worth a man's watching, until, 
near the middle of the wall, at a place I knew to be a passage between the food galleries and the lower cells, a single point stirred: 
dimmed, and steadied, and dimmed again, and went still.\par

``Someone crossed the light there,'' Va-Raedin said. 
``The beam does not care who. It tells you only this: that at that place, at this hour, a body passed between the source and the wall. Not the face. 
Not the errand. Not the burden. Only ever where and when.''\par

Between First Light and Fifth Blade, he told me, the halls move, and the whole wall shivers and flickers like water in wind, and it takes years to learn to read a 
busy thing and know the ordinary flickers from the ones that matter; 
but after Fifth Blade the Mountain holds still by its own long law, and men keep to their cells, for the dark halls cost more than they give and this is a people 
that does not spend a body it can keep --- and in that stillness a single point that stirs is as loud, upon the Mirror, as a shout in an empty hall.\par

``You will learn each point,'' Va-Raedin said. 
``Which beam it is, and which place it stands for, and what its stirring means, and what its stillness means, which is harder. 
There are two hundred and fifty-six of them on this wall. 
No two candidates learn them in the same order, and it takes years, and there is no shorter road. 
That is the whole of what a Candidate does, in the end. 
A Candidate learns where light falls.'' 
He looked at me, not unkindly, and let the thing land. 
``They all fall here.''\par

I stood before the wall that could find any body in the Mountain, and I had a thought I did not say. It cannot find this, I thought: it can tell him the passage I stand in, and the hour, and by which stair I came down; it cannot tell him what is moving behind my face while I look at his beautiful wall. The Mirror held every place I had ever set my foot, and it could not hold the one thing I had, in that moment, quietly decided to keep.\par

They had taught me to see.
It is the last gift a cage should ever give a prisoner, and they gave it to me freely, and called it upkeep. 
That was the whole of the horror, though I did not feel it as horror then, and would not for years: that to learn where light falls is to learn to keep the gate; 
that the wall which watched the Mountain would one day be mine to read, and mine to tend; 
that I would stand in the coldest and most beautiful room of the prison and keep it faithfully, and curse the prison in every breath I drew, and keep it still. 
A boy taught to see is not, by that gift, taught to leave. 
He is taught to become an eye the Mountain can use.\par

\begin{room}

--- Let me be certain I have understood you. A room that can find any man in your Mountain, and tell his masters the passage he stands in and the hour he stood there. And you were fifteen, and they brought you down and showed it to you, and you thought it beautiful.

--- I thought it the most beautiful thing I had ever seen. 

--- It is a good place to leave the morning. And the afternoon runs as we agreed: a few hours with the tutor, and then the physician asks an hour of you for the body. He is not content with your numbers.

--- Instruction, yes; I will sit with your tutor. The physician and his hour, we shall see. Do you know what they will teach me?

--- The shape of it, yes. The exact content or the methodology, no. We are kept apart, one from another; the House calls it compartmentalization. Each account is to be taken clean, free of the others' influence. Come. I will show you where you will eat.

\end{room}
