\textit{In Noxius}

--- The hearing, Kailan. The sealed chamber, the five, the ruling they made. I need it from you --- in your own words, onto the reel.

--- No. Put the thing away and leave me be. It is done. It fell three days since, and it is falling still, out there, on people who never once heard our names. What is an account for now? Who is left above to read how carefully it was decided?

--- Because it has happened. That is the very hour a record is for --- not before, to warn, but after, when nothing can be mended and the last thing left to save is how it truly was. Someone must set it down as it was, or in a hundred years it will be only what they needed it to have been.

--- You were in the room, Paul. Your machine ran the whole while; it holds every word already. Write what you saw. Or have it from the Keepers, from Sarah, from Va-Sheva --- they did the speaking. I stood at the wall with my hands bound and said almost nothing.

--- Va-Sheva will not speak to me. Sarah gives me her back. The Keepers are turning air and counting fish, and have no hour to spare for a man with a machine. And I will not be the whole record through one pair of eyes, my own least of all --- one seat, one angle on a thing that large, and it stops being an account and becomes a story with a witness. Laid beside yours it stands up straight; alone it leans. I need yours.

--- Not now. I have a shift on the vents and nothing in me to give you. Not now, Paul.

\centerline{[\,---\,]}

--- How about now. Please.

--- \ldots{} Really? The very moment I finally sit down? Where do we restart?

--- Where we left off. What has happened since we left the Blackwood Estate?

--- Surely you can do that yourself, and better than I would. I can take it up again once we reach the Mountain. Now let me rest.

\end{room}

So it falls to me. I am an ill-chosen narrator for a journey. My training is in dead things --- the strata, the midden, the worked flint, the bone that has lain in silt for forty thousand years. A living people, a desert, a war: I read all three for the first time that spring, and badly, and at speed. I will set down what I saw in the order I saw it, and where I understood nothing I will say so.\par

It began with a departure already underway, before any sentence could frame it. The last reel had ended on the lawn, in the cold, with the machine turning its blades; I will begin where it stopped. Behind us the house stood lit at every window it had spent two years pretending it did not have. I had come to think the calm of that place a law of nature, and in one night it had turned out to have been a coiled thing all along --- doors I had never seen, opening; men I had never met, in the halls; Va-Elise everywhere at once, moving people the way a foreman moves a floor. Hiding was over: the enemy already knew the Mountain was there, and the one thing left worth keeping from them was time, which no amount of Sarah's money could buy and every amount of it could hurry. So she stopped spending on secrecy and began spending on speed; and I stood in the middle of the change with my machine and a coat, the one useless scholar in a building that had plainly rehearsed exactly this, and understood that I had spent two years a guest in a garrison and called it a home.\par

The helicopter took us off the mountain in a lurch that put my stomach through my boots, and taught me the lawn had been a helipad all along, and I had walked my morning thoughts across it for two years. Sarah --- ninety-three, and serenely unbothered --- spent the flight on a headset, making calls I heard only her half of: clipped, exact, in three languages, each one locking some further piece of a machine already in motion. Somewhere over the noise a flat male voice asked the cabin why we were not yet in the air, as though the honest labour of a helicopter were a personal insult; and I learned that this was simply how the whole cold competent tribe of them spoke, to whom any gap between a decision and its execution was a thing to be shaved to nothing.\par

The aircraft was a private one; I had flown to digs in the cargo netting of transports older than myself and had never in my life been inside such a thing, and I am ashamed how completely it dismantled my principles. There were beds. There was a shower. Food arrived by means I never detected, and I ate it like a condemned man, which is exactly what I was. Kailan touched none of it and sat looking out at the tops of the clouds with the stillness of a man being flown back to his own execution. Sarah, who ate almost nothing and slept upright, opened her eyes once to tell me to take all the rest and comfort I could hold. ``What is coming has no kindness in it,'' she said. ``This is the last of the soft world you will see for a long while, and it may be for good. Enjoy it.'' Then she shut her eyes again.\par

\ornament

We came down onto a strip of old colonial tarmac that someone had swept and lit for us between one of Sarah's telephone calls and the next, and the soft world ended at the foot of the aircraft steps. Three vehicles waited with their engines turning: black, identical, armoured, built for country that has no roads. Around them stood the dozen or so who would run us the rest of the way, and I had never stood among people so quietly alarming, or so good at what they did.\par

They were led, in the way of such people, from the front and the middle at once. Va-Elise took the lead car, the one that would break the road ahead of us. Jean-Charles took ours, the middle one, which I understood to be a compliment I would rather not have been paid. And the third of them --- the one who actually moved us, who set the tempo the rest fell into without being told --- was a woman named Alice.\par

I cannot make you believe Alice, so I will only report her. She was perhaps thirty-five, with cropped fair hair and the flat clear eyes of a person for whom fear is a language never learned; and in a country that kills the unready with sun and cold in a single day she was dressed in walking shorts and boots and a quantity of matte-black equipment slung and taped precisely where a hand would reach for it, and she looked, God help me, like a plate from an adventure I would have been ashamed to be caught reading at fourteen --- and was, past any argument, the most dangerous creature on that airfield. She put us into the car with two fingers and a tilt of her head. She checked a weapon I could not have named against her forearm without once glancing at it. When Sarah leaned to the window and asked her, dry as the ground, whether she still remembered the way to the desert, she answered without a flicker: ``My name is Alice, and I remember everything.'' Then she dropped into the seat beside the driver --- a lean, silent man she touched once on the shoulder, in a way that told the whole of a story none of them would ever tell me --- set her hand on the sill, and said, to no one and to all of us, ``To the hive.'' And the three cars came off the mark as one.\par

What followed was the fastest hour of my life. They ran a single strip of tar laid dead straight across nothing at a speed I had not believed a wheeled thing could hold, the lead car out ahead to open the road, ours in the guarded middle, the third hard on our tail, the drivers steering by the green ghost-world of the sights they wore over their eyes. From the seat behind me a quiet man rode the whole way with a long cased weapon across his knees and never once used it, and was plainly the reason we should never need him to. Once a knot of headlights showed far out across the pan --- a checkpoint, a patrol, somebody's men, I never learned whose --- and Alice looked at it exactly as long as it took to price it, and the weapons in all three cars came up and settled again without a word, and the lights fell away behind. I sat strapped between a woman of ninety-three and a man riding to his own funeral, and understood that whatever else these people were, they were extremely good at their jobs.\par

\ornament

They ran us through the rest of that night and into the light of the next day, off the highway at last and down a track a wheel could barely hold, to the place where the drivable country gave out --- and there, at the very end of what a machine could reach, was one of Sarah's own.\par

She had told me, on the plane, of her stations: small, buried, manned by two or three, the far nerve-ends of a body she had spent sixty years growing; men set to watch a drum and a border and a fuel gauge and to be bored, because boredom was the sound of her work going well.\par

The door of it stood open to the desert, which Sarah said no door of hers had done in sixty years. It was the finest room I had entered since Switzerland: cool dry air off a machine in the wall, water at the turn of a tap, good coffee, a kettle gone cold. She paid comfort into her far stations the way another woman pays wages. And inside that good room the instruments were still at their patient labour --- drums turning, styli drawing --- and on the paper feeding off the nearest drum was the shape I had learned to fear beside a Swiss lake: five even strokes, unhurried, arriving out of the ground beneath our feet while we stood reading them, caring nothing for the three men who had kept the post. There was blood --- on the floor, on the lip of the table, gone black in the heat --- and no bodies, and nothing to bury. A message lay half-written in a hand that had stopped inside a word, the pencil still in the gutter of the page. Someone had burned a box of paper in the corner, badly, in haste, so that the hearts of the sheets survived, curled and brown. I catalogued it as I have been trained to catalogue a dig, one item and then the next, and was ashamed of how the habit steadied me.\par

``They were taken,'' she said at last, standing, her voice scoured flat. ``Not killed. Taken. You do not burn your own papers and leave three dead men behind them; you burn them because you are being made to go and have one moment to deny them the drawer. There are no bodies because a body is of no use to them and a living man is. These were my people; they knew this quarter, my methods, my notation, and whoever holds them now holds all of it, in a room somewhere, being asked questions.'' She looked at the drum still drawing its patient poison. ``I built a nervous system for this Mountain,'' she said, ``and someone has begun, very carefully, to pull it out of the body while the body can still feel it.''\par

She found something else among the half-burned papers, and I saw it go into her coat --- a small, deliberate movement, no different to the eye from the turning of any other sheet, which I caught only because I had lately learned to watch the watchers. She said nothing of it, then or for a long while. I learned later what it was: a duty roster, charred at one edge, recording a resupply that had come up from the south three weeks before the place fell --- by a quarter, and a route, that only a very few hands had ever been trusted to hold.\par

I had none of the culprit that night, only the shape of the wound: her network had been opened from within, with a key, by one of the few for whom she had ever cut one. Sarah folded the charred roster into her coat and said nothing of it, and I did not press her. We were three nights of hard desert from the Mountain, and a suspicion with no face to fasten to is a weight a leader carries alone until it has one. So she carried it, with nowhere yet to set it down.\par

I held the machine low and let it hear the room --- the tick of the current-maker, the wind at the open door, the five strokes drawing themselves out of the earth, Sarah's scoured voice --- because a record is worth exactly as much as the hands off it are kept, and mine were the only hands in that country I could answer for.\par

\ornament

The vehicles could go no further. Past the station the ground broke into the folded rock that no wheel has ever crossed, and there, waiting in it as though he had grown out of it, was a young man of the veiled people with animals and two silent others and his face bound against a light that had yet to rise. Sarah spoke a name to him --- Amastan --- and he answered the silence after it, and not the name: Amastan had been thirty years in the ground. What met us was the name worn now by a son of a son, an office that had outlasted the man who first filled it, as the offices of that whole country have a way of doing. Behind us the cars turned and were gone back the way we had come before the dust had settled --- loud, all of them, as far as the enemy's eye reached, and no further. From here we would go the old way, and quiet.\par

We crossed the folds by night, lying up by day under cloth the colour of the ground, and I learned that a desert crossing is mostly waiting, and thirst paid out by the mouthful, and animals watered before men. And I learned Sarah. She was ninety-three years old and she was, past any contest, the hardest of us. She had crossed worse ground for worse reasons, she told me, and never once given a desert the satisfaction of watching her tire; and she gave it none now, though withholding it took the whole of what she had left. By the third night she could no longer get up onto the animal without the young man's hands beneath her, and she took them each time with a face that dared any of us to name it. Once, in the white blank of a noon, I woke to find her own hand shaking too badly to hold the cup at her mouth, and I held it for her, and we conducted the whole small mortifying business in a silence we agreed, without a word, to understand as never having occurred. She would sooner have died on that animal than complained off it, and I have never so admired a person, nor been so frightened for one. She had reckoned this crossing might be the last thing her body would ever do, and had spent it anyway, to the mouthful, on us. We came down to the seam with the water all but gone, and Sarah still, from the back of a failing animal, giving the orders.\par

Once, lying up through a white noon, Kailan said something of the Mountain's without weighing it --- that this was its own country, its outer wall, the last ring no state had ever crossed. The young man was slow to answer, the way the desert makes men slow. ``The Mountain tells itself that,'' he said at length. ``The sand keeps no walls, and no masters. It lets a man cross, and closes behind him, and forgets his name by morning; and a country that forgets your name is owned by no one.'' Then he went back to the animals. I wrote the sentence on the inside of my wrist that noon, in pencil, and have it still.\par

I will not pretend I rode into that country as a scholar rides into a field season. I had come to the Blackwood house two years before as a doubter --- retained, in plain terms, because I doubted: a disbelieving eye kept on hand to test the account of a hidden people no sane man could be asked to credit. For two years I had more than half believed Sarah a rich woman ridden by an obsession, her buried civilisation a fable her evidence had been bent to serve; and I had wanted, God help me, for it to be true in spite of that. Then, a week before this ride, they had set the man himself in front of me, and given me those few days to weigh whether he was genuine or the most patient fraud I would ever meet. Now the desert was handing me my proof by the hour, and every proof was also a sentence of death read out over five thousand people who had no idea they stood in a dock. Somewhere under a shoulder of rock ahead of us they were breathing, certain past all argument that no one alive knew they were there; and I was riding toward them with a machine in my coat and the whole apparatus of my doubt turning, one crossing at a time, into belief.\par

We came in sight of the camp on the fourth day, brought up before dawn onto high broken ground the young man knew. I lay on cold rock between a woman older than any grandmother I had known and a recording machine, and looked down at the thing that had come for the human past; and the first thing it did to me, before the fear, was a species of vertigo. Kailan said of it, much later and only once, that it was \emph{built like the Mountain}, and left it there. What lay in the pan below in the grey light was two utterly opposed peoples --- one hidden eleven thousand years, one arrived that season with satellites --- who had, under the single pressure of guarding a secret, grown the identical body. I had crossed the world to study convergence in flint and bone, the way unrelated hands arrive at the same blade because the stone permits only so many good answers. I had never looked to see two whole civilisations converge, in the dark, on the same architecture of the soul, and be given half a day to admire it before it killed everyone I had come to meet.\par

Sarah read the camp to us the way she read everything, low and even and without hope. From above, she said, they had eyes we could not see --- the dish on its mount that spoke to something standing off above the weather; the whole camp laid out on the assumption that it was watched from the sky by its own side and must be kept invisible to every other. Twice while we lay there a fleck crossed the high blue with no sound to it and caught the early light, and she said it was theirs, and would come again on a timetable she could have written on the rock. It was, she said, the most expensive kind of secret there is, the kind one state keeps from all the others; and they were spending the whole of it on the Mountain. I had come braced for antiquity and found the most modern thing I have ever seen turned like a burning-glass on the most ancient, and I lay on that rock and was afraid of my own century for the first time in my life.\par

On the ridges and in the throats of the approaches were men dug in and facing inward, and instruments on tripods pointed at no sky. And below, in the flat pan, stood the squat heavy trucks I had seen only as smudges in her stolen photographs, and beside them a drilling rig, and about its foot the beginnings of a hole going down. She read us the particular parts of it quietly, the way a physician reads a shadow on a plate to a family. The truck on the western ridge was set to cover an old drovers' track to the Oasis from the north --- the one the Mountain's own Shield had let fall out of memory because no outsider had ever found it; someone had told these people precisely where to look. The deep borehole under the rig was aimed at a point she named west of the Hall and lower, and she went grey giving the reason: it was aimed at the water, at the great hidden reserve the Mountain kept against its lean years, because a besieger who means to take a place alive must first find what it drinks. And the tents at the centre, ringed twice, with their own generators and vehicles built to carry a man lying flat --- those were a hospital, and the hospital frightened her past all the guns, because a man does not raise a hospital to rob a mine. He raises one when he means to take prisoners he must keep alive, and to spend his own men in the taking, and to stay a very long while.\par

``Above, around, below,'' she said. ``That is how men close on a body, and they have closed it. They mean to know the whole of it --- the skin and the bones and the soft parts --- before they open it. They have no intention of destroying this Mountain. You do not spend this to destroy a thing; you drop one bomb on it, as my husband would have, and go home to dinner. This is the pattern you mount when what is inside the thing is worth more than the thing, and you want it whole, and alive, and yours.''\par

I asked her, in a whisper, how she could be so certain from a mile off in a bad light. She said she was certain because she had stood in a hundred camps built to that same pattern, on the buying side of them, and because a camp is a sentence and she could read the grammar of this one, and because, God forgive her, a good half of it was written in her own hand. And then, to the ground, and to none of us: ``Some part of me wanted it found. The whole time. So that it would be over. So that I should not be the last one left holding it.'' A pause. ``Well. It is found. And this is what found looks like.'' She said nothing more for an hour, and I did not fill it.\par

The camp was divided exactly as the Mountain is divided. The men who drilled did not speak to the men who guarded, nor the men who read the instruments to the men who spoke to the sky; and none went near the ringed tents where the questions were being put to her taken people. Each man held his own corner and was walled from every other, so that to seize any one of them was to seize a corner and never the map. The Mountain had done it out of terror, across eleven thousand years, so that no one hand could ever end the world. These men had done it out of thrift, in a season, so that no one captured hand could ever undo the operation. Opposite fears; opposite centuries; the identical wall. The clever, frightened animal we both are, given a thing worth hiding, builds the same hiding-place every time --- whether the thing it hides is a god or a grudge.\par

\ornament

Jean-Charles had gone down into the dark and come back before the light, and would not say how, and he brought two things up out of the camp with him.\par

Jean-Charles was a Frenchman of a kind I had read about and disbelieved: a man wholly competent at violence and wholly charming about it, who had once carried Kailan across this same desert as a bundle under a cloth and made a joke of it, and who now moved through an armed camp in the dark as though it were a party where he happened to know the host. I am an indoor animal. I could not take my eyes off him, and I trusted him without reservation. Before the light was full, a second page in Sarah's coat would make that trust difficult to carry.\par

The two things he carried up were these. The first was a man --- gagged, bound, furious, and very frightened --- an entirely ordinary man of the outside, a sentry from the camp's edge who had wandered a few yards too far into the dark to relieve himself and met, in place of privacy, the smiling Frenchman. The second was a flat hard case of documents and a black instrument for speaking across distance. Sarah took the case, opened it on the rock, went through it faster than I could follow, came to a single page, and stopped.\par

``There,'' she said, and turned it so the machine could see what the machine could not read. ``That mark, in the top corner. That belongs to a compartment of the French strategic service --- the heir to every arrangement my father and Martinez ever made in this desert --- and it has set its own quiet mark on a page pretending to be about minerals.'' She laid the page down with a care worse than any violence. ``There is my answer, then. The rogue contractor I came out hoping to find does not exist. This is the state. This is the loaf grown from my crumbs, and the men who took my network from me worked out long ago that the yellow rock was never the prize. The prize is the thing under the rock. They know a mechanism is down there. They do not know what it does. And they are drilling toward it, on my grid, with my methods, and they will reach it, because I am the one who taught the world how.''\par

I asked if I might have the case. It was the one thing on that rock I was fit to touch: my whole trade had been whether a paper is what it claims to be, and a week under Kailan's eye had laid a second trade over the first --- the thing his own masters had once ground into him, that light lies loudest where it is brightest, and the truth of a thing hides in the shadow it throws. Sarah had read the bright mark, the service stamp that said \emph{state}, and read it rightly. I went looking for the dim one, into the small dry print a man sets down only because he is certain no enemy will ever hold the page; and three leaves deep, in a logistics annex that nobody bothers to dress because nobody expects it to matter, I found the thing I had been carried across a continent to find and never once told to look for.\par

It was a route. One line among forty: a track into the folds from the north, called by its local name and marked, in a quartermaster's shorthand, \emph{source: internal, reliability high}. I knew the track. An hour before, on the ridge, I had heard Sarah say that the Mountain's own Shield had let that path fall out of living memory, that no outsider had ever found it, that someone had told these people exactly where to look. And I knew, because she had said that too, whose corridor this was: after Martinez was shut out, the northern approaches of Horizon had been entrusted to a single pair of hands, so that one clean channel would remain that Martinez's masters could not read. Here was that one channel, reduced to two dry words on an enemy's supply list. I did not say the name, even in my head; I did not have to, for the shape of it was the shape of a man I had eaten beside and come to love a little, and the newly trained part of me had known it before the fond old part would let the words come. And the case that held it he had carried up out of the dark on his own back --- and might, for all I could tell, not know what its annex said. It was serious. It was not proof. I closed the case, found Sarah's eye, and did the last thing my invented order ever asked of me: I folded the page small, put it in her hand, and told her under my breath to read the annex first, and mark whom the enemy called reliable. She read it. Her face did not move, which was how I knew. She folded it smaller than a page needs folding and put it away inside her coat, and left it there --- I would learn --- for the rest of her life. ``Thank you, Mr Morgan,'' was all she said. ``You have finished your work. I am sorry that it was this work.''\par

The black instrument woke then of its own accord. A light came up on its face and it spoke, small and tinny --- a young man's voice, in French, bored and just beginning to be worried, asking why post nine had missed its check, saying that if Farid had crawled behind a rock to sleep again he could answer to the sergeant himself this time. Jean-Charles laid his hand flat over it until the light died. It bought us minutes, no more. The boy on the radio thought Farid asleep behind a rock, not taken; but the next voice would be a sergeant's, and after that men would come looking. Farid, then. The man bound at our feet had a name, and a post number, and a friend on a cold ridge who had covered for him one call longer than he ought to have, and a sergeant somewhere who would be angry in the ordinary, tired, human way of sergeants; and not one of them had been told that they were camped three hundred metres above the end of the world.\par

The bound man talked, in the end, because Jean-Charles crouched by him and said something low and did him no hurt, and had told us he would: ``a fence-sitter, a man who believes he is guarding a mine; he will give us the little he has been given, and the little will be the trouble.'' And the little he gave was worth more for its gaps than its substance: he knew his own watch and the two beside it and no others, and had been told this was a mineral survey of the highest secrecy. He knew there were people in the ground ahead --- ``primitives,'' he said, ``a lost tribe,'' to be handled \emph{without loss of life} --- and I marked the word: \emph{primitive}, the exact term my science spent a hundred years learning to be ashamed of, and the precise word the Mountain's own scripture keeps for us, aimed the other way down the same barrel. He knew his sappers grumbled over strange orders --- \emph{intact, everything intact, break nothing} --- and he did not know why, and had been kept from the why.\par

It was Kailan who put the three questions, less for their answers than for the walls they would strike. Who told you to take no life? A different officer than the one who set the charges. Who may say the digging is to stop? A cell you have never seen. Who among you may speak to the men on the far ridge? No one; they are a separate command. Three questions, three walls, and behind the walls the same lattice, confirmed now out of the mouth of a man who lived inside it and had never once seen its shape.\par

And then Kailan said the thing that only he, of all of us on that rock, could have seen. He had been turning the pages of the case while the sentry talked. ``There are five,'' he said, and Sarah opened her eyes, because it was his to see and he had seen it. ``Look how the live-capture orders are written --- five priorities, each one coded, each named by a task and never by a man: the one who reads the deep water, the one who keeps the hours, the one who holds the seal, and two beside them. Five \emph{functions} the work cannot go forward without. They do not know what the engine is for. But they have understood that it is worked by five hands that no single hand may ever join, and they mean to seize the whole set living and make it answer to a new one. They want the key with the Keepers still hanging on the ring.'' He was quiet a moment. ``It is the one crime the thing was built to make impossible. And they are three hundred metres from it, and do not even know that is the crime they have come to commit.'' The sentry, when it was put to him, had no notion what the five codes meant; he guarded one corner and had never seen the shape. I set it down as Kailan said it, and could never have reached it myself, never having been raised inside the machine I had come to record.\par

``The road is open,'' Jean-Charles said, and the thing that was always in his face behind the performance came up under the competence for a moment --- a soldier's grief, I took it for, then. ``There is a seam on the far side the young man knows and their sensors do not yet cover. It would have closed within the day; Farid's missed check has shortened that to minutes. We go now, or we do not go at all.''\par

Va-Sheva met us inside the skin of the Mountain. She was standing in a low stone chamber in the plain grey of the office the Mountain calls Shield, two wardens behind her and a lamp set on the floor between us. She did not look at Kailan as people look at one another. She looked at the air about him, at the spaces to either side, weighing what he had brought in before she would let herself weigh him --- reading him, I think, by everything except his face; and I wondered how much of a life one could spend being guarded that closely and mistake it for being known. She was with child. I saw it when the lamp found her, and she made no motion to hide it from any of us. I set the fact down and let its weight lie, for I could not have said whose weight it was --- whose child, whose grief, whose fear --- and it was not mine to assign.\par

``You were sent out to find whether the enemy within was real,'' she said to him. ``Shield's own writ, sealed by the council that could not agree to keep you. You come back to me with two Outsiders the Mountain has never weighed --- the Frenchman is Shield's own, known and vouched; these two are not --- and one of them is the maker of the very net that hunts us --- and a machine that has recorded God knows what, up a seam no Veil is shown, in the middle hour of an attack this Mountain does not yet know it is under. By every law I hold, I should have you killed at this lamp, and them beside you, and the seam sealed behind your three bodies.''\par

``Then do it,'' Kailan said, ``or hear me; but decide now, because the woman beside me can tell you the depth of the hole they are drilling and the day it reaches your Hall, and no one else alive can, and every breath you spend deciding is a breath she is not spending on saving your people.''\par

She looked at Sarah then, and Sarah looked back, two women who had each spent a lifetime holding a wall against the other's kind, and something passed between them with no warmth in it and a great deal of recognition, and was gone. ``Jean-Charles vouches for the breach,'' Va-Sheva said --- to Kailan, never to Sarah.\par

``He does.''\par

``I trust his hands. I have worked beside them.'' It was said flatly, and Kailan went very still at it; and of all the hands in that Mountain, the one she named as trusted was the one the paper in Sarah's coat had marked. ``I do not trust hers,'' she went on, ``and I am about to have to.'' She gave a low word to a warden, and turned back to Kailan, and for the space of one breath the office slipped. ``I contained you to keep you breathing,'' she said. ``I did not do it so that you could bring the breathing back inside the wall. That was cruel of you. We will not speak of it again.'' And I wrote it down, understanding barely the half of it, trusting the meaning stood whole even where I was empty.\par

--- Sit before you fall, Mr Morgan. Queen Anne, or smoked Earl Grey?

--- \ldots{} Smoked Earl Grey, thank you. It seems fitting.

--- You did not climb all this way for my tea. What is it?

--- The sealed hearing. I have almost reached it, and I cannot walk a reader up that road, because although I walked it, I could not read it --- I am a man of letters, not numbers. Kailan's account gives me the first of the Justice rooms and no further. I saw Ka-Leth work the rest, but I did not understand what he was doing. You did. Do you remember their setups --- the problem each posed, the answer that opened it?

--- I remember them. We had the Faith road already, from the boy's account; this was the Justice one --- a matter of procedural control, at a guess. It is only now plain to me what these people have spent their centuries studying, and how deep it runs; it is almost past believing. But the first room: \textit{the balance of scale}. A scale cut into the wall, the block-sizes lying against the weights they carry, the beam engraved leaning the wrong way on purpose --- and one weight to set at the one right notch to level it true, reckoning by the number each block is worth and not the look of it. The number each notch showed, weight or none, was the combination, and the door gave. The second was \textit{the nearest truth}. \textit{1 1 -1; 3 2 1; 7 5 -1; 17 12 1; 41 29 -1}. There Ka-Leth did not so much as pause, so he carried it by heart. A square, and its diagonal --- even you would know that for the root of two. The harder half is the nearest honest ratio to it, and he entered that combination without a breath: two hundred and thirty-nine over a hundred and sixty-nine. What came next?

--- The wolf thing.

--- \textit{The false witness}. One wolf in a thousand sheep; the wall names the true wolf nine times in ten, and brands an innocent three times in a hundred; and it asks the real weight of guilt on the one it has branded. You would not have the trick of it. I worked it again, to be sure. Where did I put it \ldots{}

I set it down: \textit{counted by sevens, six left over; by elevens, eight; by thirteens, three; by seventeens, five. Lower than 17017.} One number under all four, and the door. I could reconstruct it, given the hour and no one drilling overhead --- but that it is not hard for me is beside the point; the point is the room passes no one who cannot, exactly like the others. And the last was cut on the wall with a word I take to mean \textit{the forecast}: this sequence, \textit{two, five, five, eight, nine,} for the first five years, and a question for year seven: \textit{Lay the straight judgment through so that the errors are least}. That one he knew: one, two, six, over ten. You and I both know how he did that one. Now \ldots{} do you want the antechamber, and the golden pillars, as well?

--- No need. I have that from the boy's trial already. Thank you.

--- What I cannot make sit right is why they ever needed to hide at all. A thousand years ahead of us; they could have walked out of that desert and had the whole earth for the asking.

--- With what? There are five thousand of them, Mr Morgan, and every clever thing they know is bent on five narrow subjects, and not one of the five is how to raise an army or a foundry. A people can be a thousand years ahead of you in a lens and a number and still be a handful of monks on a rock, with one weapon under the floor that was no good for taking anything --- only for ending everything. The hiding was not their weakness. It was the whole of their strength.

--- Tell me something, Mr Morgan, and it need not go on the reel. That antechamber, all that vastness --- was it the best day of your life?

--- Professionally, yes. Which is the tragedy of it.

She led us up through the office's own ways, and spent no breath on Kailan and every breath on the thing in hand, throwing questions back over her shoulder that only Sarah could answer --- how deep, how many, how long since the drilling began, whether a borehole could be fouled from within or heard coming in time to empty the galleries beneath it. And Sarah answered every one, flat and fast and without a tremor: the enemy of the place instructing its defender, in a stone corridor, in exactly how she would take the Mountain if she stood in the camp below instead of the passage above.\par

``The council will have to hear the whole of it,'' Va-Sheva said, and did not slow. ``You. Her. The machine. Their Justice will not move a hand's breadth without the whole laid out in order. They cast you out with one voice, Kailan --- all five agreed. And now I am to carry you back into the sealed hall, and set an Outsider at your one side and the woman who built our death at the other, and ask the five to believe the three of you, on the night the walls begin to come down.''\par

Once, where the stair turned and the others had gone on ahead, she said a thing not meant for me, which the machine caught because the machine catches everything. She did not turn her head. ``Is it true, what the containment cost you? Va-Elise wrote to me that you did not speak for eleven days.'' Kailan told her that it was true. ``Good,'' she said, and there was no cruelty in it; it was her office; a woman confirming that a wall had held under load. ``Then it worked. You are breathing. I was right to do it. And I will not forgive myself for having been right, for as long as I am given to live. Do not make me say more than that. I have a Mountain to lose tonight, and I cannot lose you as well and still be of any use to it.'' And she went on up the stair, and I climbed after her with the reel turning, carrying a stranger's grief up a staircase in the dark.\par

We had climbed only a little further when the route crossed an open stair above the common wards, and for the length of that one crossing I was given the thing I had come around the world to see, and would have paid a great deal, by then, never to have seen at all.\par

It was an ordinary evening down there. Lamps at the food-stalls. A line of small children being walked somewhere in pairs by a woman carrying a switch she plainly never used. Two men arguing without heat over the price of something in a basket. I had spent two years and the better part of my belief on the proposition that these people were real, and here they were, being real, at their supper, and the fact of it went through me like a grief. And then the smell of the evening bread came up the stair around us --- warm, ordinary, yeasted, the same loaf, near enough, that has risen at the human hearth since before there were nations or numbers, that Kailan's people had raised in this stone every evening for eleven thousand years and believed they would go on raising for eleven thousand more --- and it undid me in a way the guns had not, because I knew, as a man knows his own trade, exactly how old that smell was and exactly how few evenings it had left. I could have leaned over the rail and shouted the date of their ending down to them, and it would have saved not one of them, because they had been raised from the cradle to be constitutionally unable to hear it. Va-Sheva gave me no leave to stop walking. ``They do not know,'' she said, very low, at my shoulder. ``And they will not learn it from you, tonight, on a stair. Walk.'' So I walked, and the warm smell of the bread came up the stair after us, and thinned as we climbed, and was gone.\par

We had gone not much further when the Mountain took its first blow.\par

It came up through the stone into the soles of my feet and the bones of my jaw --- one deep, flat concussion with nothing in it of the patient five-stroke sounding I had learned to read; a blow, simply, the way a fist is a blow. Dust sifted down from a seam overhead. Off through the rock there was a long grinding, and then a nearer, sharper one: worked stone failing along a line that living hands had cut true a hundred generations before mine was born. I had never stood inside such a failure as it happened, and I hope, with whatever is left to me, never to stand inside one again.\par

Sarah had gone rigid, her head turned, reading it off the air. ``That was no sounding,'' she said. ``That was a charge --- they have put an explosive down one of the boreholes and fired it, close, to break a way in, or to see what breaks. What is there? North-western arc, shallow, near the surface --- what did they just reach?''\par

And Va-Sheva, who had always watched the spaces between things, was already moving toward the sound, her voice gone hard with the first true fear I had heard in it, throwing the answer back over her shoulder as she went.\par

``Faith,'' she said. ``That is a Faith passage. That is the road to the Hall.''\par

And she was gone into the dark toward it, Jean-Charles a pace behind her with a warden, Sarah after them already and calling for a lamp and a plan of the north-western arc; and for one breath Kailan and I were left alone in the emptying chamber. I watched him put his hand flat to the cold stone of the Mountain that had made him and cast him out and would now be broken open by the very century I belonged to, and I saw his face change at what he felt beneath his palm, and I set my own hand to the wall beside his, wanting, I suppose, to know what he knew; and the stone was moving. Not much. A tremor, deep and slow and wrong, the settling of a mass that had had, in eleven thousand years, no cause to settle. Then I did the only thing I have ever been of any use for. I lifted the machine toward the grinding of the wounded rock and let it hear the exact sound the end began with, for whoever might one day need to know; and then Kailan and I ran, after the lamps, and after the noise, and toward five thousand people at their supper who believed, past the reach of any argument, that they could never be reached.\par
