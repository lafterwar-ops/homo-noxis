\typesetchapter{Chapter 21}{Vigil}

The enemy above us took four more days to die, and we knew it the way we knew everything now, at a remove, through instruments and stone.\par

Their charges stopped first. On the second day after the firing the patient thudding that had been the roof of our lives for a season simply was not there any more; Shield's listeners reported nothing where the drilling had been. Then, over the days after, they reported the other sounds --- the ground moving in ways no record Balance kept had ever shown, long shudders rolling through the region and growing. It was not the far blow itself, which had crossed and gone; it was the slow tearing that followed it, the antipodal rupture setting its neighbours failing, and those their neighbours, the wound travelling fault by fault out of the far world and toward ours. We did not see it end. We heard the ground take it. Sarah stood at the Faith stone she had made her own, and on the fourth day said, without any triumph in her, ``The camp is gone. The ground opened where the camp was, and closed. They are under.'' And that was the last the world ever knew of the men who came to own the Mountain: that they were buried by the first small yawn of the thing they had come to steal, four hundred metres from the door they never opened.\par

Jean-Charles I barely saw for the first four days, though he was inside the seal with us; he had come through ahead of the closing gate before the first load fell, and had not stopped since. He was not the man who had bought a grieving woman sweets and made a joke of breakfast. While the Beast fired and the wards shook, and through the broken days after, he worked with Shield without sleep, carrying the wounded and the dead out of the inner Faith passages the firing had cracked, and moving the living from wards gone unsafe. On the fifth day he found me in the deep. He did not smile, did not embrace me, only stood a moment and set his hand on my shoulder the way he had in the desert when I was a bundle under a cloth, and said, ``We are alive, man. Those of us that are. I have decided to be glad of it later, when there is a later. For now there are people to carry.'' And he went to carry them.\par

I had never understood, until I watched it save us, that the piped light, the turned air, the two waters, the deep stolen heat, every miracle I had been taught to call God's mercy, was one machine with one purpose: to keep five thousand people alive inside a sealed stone through the end of a world.\par

\ornament

Ninety days. That was the lock, and no one argued it. The arithmetic was Balance's and Shield's together: the time the air could be turned and cleaned without the outside; the time the stores would feed us; the time the world above needed to do the worst of its convulsing before a person could stand in it and live. Ninety days sealed, and then Shield would crack a single high vent and taste what the world had become, and we would learn whether ninety had been enough or whether we had bought ourselves only a longer grave.\par

I will not pretend the ninety days were one thing. A day is a day even at the end of the world; you wake, and you are hungry, and something must be done. We had been raised on the Beast as the end of endings, the unthinkable, the wrath. And here we were, on the far side of having loosed it, boiling lentils.\par

\ornament

They moved the children first, out of the upper wards where the Faith passage had broken and the cold came through, into the deep districts, and without panic. The Mountain had drilled the moving of children for eleven thousand years and called it a festival game; when the true day came the children thought it was the game again, and went down singing the song that was the evacuation order set to a tune so that no child would ever be too frightened to obey it.\par

The kitchens paid off too. At the Festival of Equal Light five hundred hands had made a game of the loaves --- boards of warm bread run hand to hand from the ovens to the Hall, the prize to the team that spilled none, a tipped board a thing of laughter and its loss written down by a Balance clerk with a smile. Now the same relay ran through the crowded galleries to the relocated deep wards, bread and water and rolls of the ashkeepers' filter cloth passed along a living chain that had rehearsed itself for eleven thousand years as play; and no one laughed, and nothing was let fall.\par

There were water-listeners. I had passed them my whole life and thought them a minor holy order, old men and women who put their ears to the great pipes and murmured. They were the gauges, and listened to one thing only: the water --- the levels and slow movement of the two reserves, which they called up in shifts to Balance, who set them against what the sealed city drank and ate and burned, so the day the stores would fail might be known before it came.\par

The air had its own listeners, and those I had known best of all and never understood. At the Festival of Equal Light the Ventsingers were the loveliest thing in the Mountain: singers set in separate galleries, each sustaining one long note into the stone and waiting for the Mountain to hand it back through a far throat, so that a voice you could not see answered a voice you could, and where two returns met they made chords no one had written, and we called it the Mountain singing with us. It was worship, and it was the Mountain testing its own lungs. A singer who has sent notes into those throats since childhood knows to the breath how each ought to come home, and which voices it should meet on the way. So when, deep in the sealed dark, Shield had closed a broken district and divided the surviving passages a new way, a note came home thin and late and alone, the voices that should have answered it never arriving; and the Ventsingers knew, before any wheel turned, that a throat had blocked and an occupied ward had begun to breathe its own breath back. Their call sent the vent-keepers running to the right wheels, and the ward breathed again; and what I had loved as a boy for being beautiful had been beauty and a set of lungs at once.

There were ashkeepers, whose whole office, I had thought, was to tend the shrine of the last burning. On the fortieth day the tasting stone came in grey. High above the sealed wards the Mountain kept a blind sampling plate, open to the outside sky and to nothing within, so Shield might learn what the air had become without letting one breath of it past the Seal; and when they drew it in it came filmed with ash, fine and grey and from no wind we owned. No outside air had entered, only its dust, on a stone that touched nothing we breathed. The ashkeepers knew it at once. Their useless holy liturgy about the grey that falls when the world is punished turned out to be an instruction manual for filtering volcanic ash out of an air supply against the day Shield would open the vent, written by a hand that had known, eleven thousand years ago, exactly what the sky would do.\par

I watched an old woman I had known as a child, who had once scolded me for running in the Balance halls, sit through the whole ninety days at the thing she had always done, the accounting of the salt fish. So many barrels drawn, so many remaining, carried and checked and carried. She did it through the end of the world with the same pursed exactness as through every ordinary year. The Mountain was full of her.\par

Somewhere in the fifth week Va-Maera of Faith came partway back. Her hearing did not return and never would; but the mind the fallen roof had driven out of reach rose again, slowly and unevenly, until she knew faces once more and could say the rites her whole life had worn into her, even where other parts of her stayed broken.\par

A child was born in the deep wards on the fiftieth day, because bodies keep their own calendar and do not consult the sky. The news of it went through the sealed Mountain faster than any of Sarah's silences, ward to ward, a child, a child born. I watched people who had spent a season marking the end of everything stop, and turn toward the rumour of her, and be, for one afternoon, helplessly glad. Va-Maera came and blessed her, as Faith blesses every child the Mountain is given. ``Be welcome into the count of the people,'' she said over the small new breathing, in the flat over-loud voice of the newly deaf; ``be kept in the dark and found in the light; may the Chains hold their count above you, and the Beast keep to its Hell, all the days that are given you; let every breath be whole'' --- and there she stopped. Her eyes went to Va-Sheva, and were truly the ugliest thing I saw in all the ninety days, and she held it as long as she needed. ``let no one hand hold the whole.'' Then she gave the child back to its mother, and was glad again with the rest of them. They named her for the season, which was the season of the dark. I thought that a grim weight to hang on a new person, until Va-Sheva told me the name meant the ones who are born to carry a light through it. The Mountain had, in every generation any record held, a name ready for a child born into the dark. I did not think, that afternoon, to wonder why a people who believed themselves eternal and safe would keep ready a name for the child born at the end of the world.\par

In the broken galleries where the lamps had died I saw the use at last of the silliest game of Equal Light, in which the attendants of Light had raced to tie the old service knots blindfolded, or behind their backs, or with one hand hidden in the robe. A boy of Light went down a lightless aperture with one arm bound useless since the Faith roof fell, and tied the lamp-line and the repair-cord that made the passage safe for the rescuers wholly by touch, one-handed, the way the game had taught his hands to work when they had neither sight nor a second hand to help.\par

They gave me work, in the end, because a man with idle hands in a sealed crisis is a danger and a grief to himself. They put me on the vents, under an old vent-keeper named Sa-Orin who cared nothing for what I had done in the Hall of Cataclysm and a great deal whether I turned the wheel he pointed at the instant he pointed at it. He worked by the Ventsingers' calls, and I by him; and once, when a cry came down that a chord had gone lame in the wards below us, Sa-Orin set his hand to a wheel and I threw my weight on it where he pointed, and far off through the stone I heard the missing note climb back into the chord and the chord stand whole --- a ward I would never see, breathing again for the exact turn of my two hands. It was the first honest work of my life.\par

\ornament

It was in those days that I found Sela again. She had been of Light with me when we were young, the one who carried the test-glass and the little oil and the cloths and watched me at the beam with the corner of a smile she could never quite put away; and when the office lifted me above her she had lowered her eyes in the corridors and put the smile away for good. Now there was no office left to stand between us. She found me in the crowded galleries, afraid the way the whole Mountain was afraid, and stopped when she saw me --- the erased boy come back a stranger, broader and harder than the beam-walker she had known, the man who had gone outside and returned to break the world open --- and the smile came back. I was more alone in those ninety days than I have ever been, before or since, and she was frightened, and neither of us had an office left to hide inside. I will not dress it up. We needed each other, and we stopped pretending we did not.\par

\ornament

The outside failed slowly, and we read it like a sickness in a body we could not see.\par

We had no window and wanted none, but the Mountain has senses finer than eyes, and the world sent us its dying through them in pieces we had to assemble. The seismic returns first, the ground telling Sarah and Faith's stone that the far catastrophe had run on past the blow into a cascade, one region of the broken crust setting off the next around the ring of fire, for days, for weeks. Then the ash, on the fortieth day, which meant eruption somewhere upwind on a scale that could darken a sky. Then the cold, which the deep heat was far too slow to feel for a long while yet, but which Shield read off the temperature rods in the sealed upper galleries, the outer stone going colder by the week than any winter the record held, because the world the Mountain borrowed its warmth against had gone frigid above. And then the silences, which were the worst, because they were where knowledge should have been. Sarah had left, in the estate and the network and a dozen dead drops, ways of hearing the outside that had nothing to do with the Mountain, and Va-Elise had carried a slender thread of them in. In the early days a very little still came, faint and delayed, a coded nothing that meant only \emph{someone is still there}. And one by one those went out. They stopped arriving, on their days, at their hours, the way letters stop from a friend who has died before anyone thought to write you. By the sixtieth day there were no more. The outside had stopped sending, because there was less and less of an outside left to send.\par

Her own threads gave only that coded pulse. The open frequencies gave more, in the early weeks --- for a wire can hear the outside without the Mountain drawing a breath of it --- and Sarah listened to all of it and read the worst of it to me, because, she said, I had aimed at these people and owed them the knowing of what I had aimed at. There was the states' traffic, loudest and least human, cyphered and clipped, one government asking another across a dying continent whether the thing in the southern desert had been theirs, and disbelieving the no. There was the soldiers' band, the ridge-men going quiet channel by channel as the ground took them. And under both, on a civil frequency anyone with a wire could catch, a woman's voice somewhere north of the sea read over and again, in three languages, the same short list of names and a place where the children carried out of a flooded town could be found by anyone still coming to look. Her voice held the first hour. It broke in the third. And then that thread too, on its day, at its hour, stopped, and Sarah marked it down with the others, and we did not speak of the woman again.\par

``That was Geneva,'' Sarah said once, of a silence, flatly. ``That thread came through Geneva. If it is gone, Geneva is gone, or dark, or drowned.'' She did not weep. I never saw her weep. But she began, around the sixtieth day, to sit for long stretches with her hand on the Faith stone and listen to nothing, and I think that was her weeping.\par

Once, of a particular silence, she said a name we had all of us heard once already, down a wire in the sealed hall, on the last night: Roger Martinez, the man who had carried the French half of her arrangement her whole life, and had, at the end of it, carried it against her. ``That will have been Roger,'' she said, without heat. ``All spring I believed I still had a hand on that channel --- that I watched it, and fed it what I chose, and that Roger was the same tired go-between fetching my grids down it he had always been. I even pitied him, for a tool kept past its use. He was no one's errand-boy by then. They had made him the master of it, because he was the one man alive who understood what the Mountain was; and ran the whole of it, arguing its case to me with more grace than I could find to refuse. He spent sixty years holding a door open for whichever master came first. At the last he stopped holding it and walked through, and it opened onto this --- and the wave he loosed came round the world and killed him, I should think, in whatever quiet room in Paris he sat to be told he had won. I said to him once that a tool outlives its use and is discarded. A good enough tool, it turns out, is promoted; and it turns in the hand; and it takes the arm off at the shoulder.'' She took her hand off the stone. ``Do not mistake that for grief. I have none to spare for Roger. I only dislike being proved wrong about a man on the same day the whole world proves me right.''\par

And once she said the largest thing, and the coldest, listening to a whole night of the world go silent on her drums. ``Your fathers could have owned the surface of this earth, boy, with what they knew, and taught it, and shaped a hundred centuries of it. It would have cost them the one thing they could not bear to spend: being the only ones who knew. Open the hand, and you stop being special the same morning; but you inherit a world. They kept the secret, and the privilege, and hid in a hole. And the world they would not stoop to inherit is the one turning to ash over our heads tonight, and no soul down here will ever have it now.'' She said it with no grief in it at all, which was the worst way I ever heard it said.\par

I have tried since to grieve the whole of it, and I cannot, and the failing is the most frightening thing I carried out of the sealed dark. One voice I could grieve: the woman reading the children's names until her own gave out; Geneva, because Sarah had made it a word and a place; one thread, one silence with a face behind it. These I could hold, and they broke me. But behind each thread was a city, and behind the city a country, and behind that the whole round of the warm world; and when I set my grief against that number it slid off, the way a hand slides off wet stone. I wept for a stranger's voice and felt nothing I could name for the billion behind her, and the billion was the true weight, and I could not lift it. I had been the eye that fired it, and I could not so much as mourn it to its size. That was the day I stopped trusting my own horror to prove me any different from anyone who could do such a thing.\par

Ka-Cheryn of Balance kept a different account beside her stores, and would show it to no one; I saw it once, over her shoulder: a list, in her careful hand, of the things the Mountain used that could never be made again inside it, and beside each one a number, the years or the decades it would last. Steel. Certain salts. The fine wire that Light needs. The medicines. And at the bottom, in a hand gone smaller, one line with no number beside it that I did not, then, understand: \emph{the seed comes up a little wronger every reset, and the blood, and no register I keep will buy that back.} She was reckoning past the ninety days to the ninety years, and past even those to a debt that fell due in no lifetime she would answer for. She never once spoke of it.\par

The guilt did not come in a storm, at once. It came the way the ash came, slowly, from nowhere you could point to, settling on everything.\par

Mine was the plainest, and I said it to Paul's machine early, so it would be on the record before I lost the courage: that I had helped to fire it. The surface of it: I had come home and been the eye they needed. The deeper thing: the counterfeit knocking that drew the enemy in might never have reached this pitch had I never let a scholar walk me down into a mountain. I had spent the book of my life insisting I was a victim of my people's cruelty. And I had come back and been the hand that turned the key.\par

Hers was larger and she never once tried to make it smaller. Sarah had built, over sixty-six years, the very apparatus that provoked the attack; her grids and methods and network, leaked and inherited, had drawn a state to this door. The sounding that woke the Mountain was played on an instrument she had, in a sense, invented. And now she had stood in the Hall of Cataclysm and been the ear that timed the strokes that ended the world she came from. ``Do not comfort me,'' she said once, when I began to. ``I have earned every gram of this and would be insulted to be talked out of it. I spent my life reaching for the largest thing there was, and I closed my hand on it, and the closing burned down everything else. That is the thing I am, and I will not have it called a tragedy that happened to me. I ask one mercy of you, boy, the same I asked of the five: that when you decide what is to be done with what we now hold, you do not let me be the only one who holds it. I have learned, too late, that the whole in one hand is the whole crime, even when the hand is sorry.''\par

The Keepers carried theirs each in the shape of his office. Ka-Dhavar of Shield, who had raised the Seal and given the order that closed the world, grew very quiet and gentle. He spent his spare hours among the wounded and frightened, as though to pay down what he had done one comforted person at a time. Ka-Raedin of Light, my own Keeper, could no longer bear to look at the deep machinery, and gave more and more of his office to me, his heretic, his eye, hating that the one man who could stand to see the whole of it was me. And Ka-Leth of Justice went hunting, night after night, through his own records for the place where the law had failed, some sentence he might have read otherwise to let a lawful Mountain do a smaller thing. He never found it, because there was none, and the not-finding was breaking him. The Mountain had spent eleven thousand years teaching its children that they were the ones the world would one day come to destroy. It had never taught us what to be on the morning we learned it was the other way round.\par

And Paul kept recording. I asked him once how he could bear it, the careful witnessing of an atrocity that had already succeeded, the obscene tidiness of getting it all down. He was quiet a while. ``Because the alternative is what was done to your people,'' he said. ``Someone stood where I am standing, eleven thousand years ago, and decided that the truth of a terrible thing should be hidden, dressed up, made into scripture, so that the people who lived inside it would never know what had been done to them or in their name. The Beast is the crime we committed; I will not pretend the lie is worse than the killing. But the lie is the older crime, the one that let it be done again --- that whoever inherits the weapon inherits it blind, so the whole horror stays sacred, and repeatable, and never learned from. I am the opposite of that man. I am making certain that whatever else happens, this one will not be a secret --- that the people who come after, if any come after, will not have to be lied to. It is the only clean thing I know how to do. I am going to do it until the reel runs out.''\par

Va-Sheva's reckoning I was not shown, because she showed it to no one, and to me least of all. She ran Shield's half of the survival through the whole ninety days, the districts and the vents and the moving of the frightened, and she did it without a wasted motion. If she woke in the dark with the count of the dead in her mouth, she did it where no one could see. Once, deep in the season, I found her alone at a closed vent with her hand flat on the cold iron of it, in the posture I had watched her use to count a living man. I thought she was listening for the outside, and she said, without turning, ``I am not listening for anything. There is nothing left out there to listen for. I am standing here because it is the one place in this Mountain where no one will come to ask me a question, and I needed, for the length of one turned glass, to not be asked one.'' I left her the turned glass.\par

Shield does not only defend a Mountain; it reckons, afterward, how the Mountain came to need defending. Va-Sheva ran that reckoning through the ninety days alongside the survival, because her office required her to walk toward the thing the rest of us walked away from: the breach, and by what road the outside had come to the one door it was never meant to find. The road, followed patiently in the sealed dark with the rigour her whole life had made of her, came home to a hand she had cut a key for herself.\par

I already carried the fact of it. Sarah had given it to me weeks before, in the dark, unable to go on holding it wholly alone --- that the hand which turned the key had been Jean-Charles's. What I did not carry, and could not have guessed, was what Va-Sheva would do with the same knowledge on the day her own office finally laid it in her palm. She told me after, in as few words as she ever gave anything, that she had spent three nights certain she had made an error in the tracing, because a wall does not often fail at the exact place its keeper has promised herself it cannot. And a fourth night certain she had not. He had come to her years before because a Veil of Shield at the Horizon threshold was the finest door into the Mountain the outside had ever been offered; he had opened it the way he opened everything, and his service had taken the routes that brought a state to the folds. She held the proof of it in her own hand, in her own office, in the season after she had laid that same hand flat on his chest and named him Mountain.\par

And here is the thing she did, which the whole eleven thousand years of the Mountain had been built out of the refusal to make. She did not open him. She had the means --- Shield is very good at opening people --- and she had the grief and the right, if anyone ever had them. She did not use them. She did not go to him and demand to know how much he had chosen and how much he had been ordered, when the feeling in him had turned real, whether at the last he had tried to give his masters less than they asked. She let all of it stand unmeasured. Because to take the one question --- \emph{did you love me, or was I only a door} --- and wring an answer from him would be to decide, forever, that he had been only a door; and she found, at the end of her audit, that she would sooner carry the not-knowing than manufacture the certainty. She kept him Mountain. She never once asked him the question, and never once let it go, and carried the two of them, unreconciled.\par

I thought it weakness, the first time I saw the shape of it, as I had once thought the salt-fish woman limited. I know now that I had mistaken refusal for incapacity again.\par

But before the vigil had run its ninety days, on the sixtieth, the Mountain opened a door that was on no map, and it was the Beast that opened it, not any hand.\par

We were in the deep, Sarah and Paul and I, with Ka-Leth of Justice. Justice had taken to keeping the two Outsiders near him, having decided that the safest place for a danger you cannot destroy is under your own eye. And the ground moved, once, the way it had been moving less and less as the far cascade wore itself out, a long low return rolling home through the crust from the antipode of the world. But this return did something the others had not. Deep in the rock below us there was a sound of stone releasing stone, deliberate, mechanical. A counterweight was falling. We would learn how it had been hung: the finishing of the two hundred and eleventh load had armed it, through a linkage buried in the count of the machine, so that no drill and no half-firing could ever set it. But arming was not opening. While the Earth still rang above a certain height the counterweight held fast; only when the long ringing had decayed, week upon week, down past that mark --- as the makers had known it would, near the sixtieth day --- did the next true return, come home below the line, let the stone go. It could open once, and only for a Mountain that had fired the Beast whole and then outlived the worst of the shaking. The great returns of the firing itself were far too loud to spring it. It was made to wait until the Cataclysm was complete, the Mountain still standing, and someone left alive to inherit what it hid.\par

Ka-Leth went white. I had seen the man rule the world's ending without his voice shaking, and a door opening turned him to a frightened old man, because the door was worse to him than the Cataclysm. The Cataclysm was in his law. This was not. ``There is nothing there,'' he said. ``I hold the--- I hold the whole record of this Mountain. Every door. Every seal. Every chamber that has ever--- there is nothing in that quarter of the deep but rock. I have the record. I have the record.'' And Sarah, who knew better than any of us what it is to learn that your record has a hole in it exactly where you were most certain it was whole, said to him, not unkindly, ``Then your record was edited. Someone who came before you took that door out of it. Come and see what your office was not allowed to know.''\par

The door stood open in the stone, a black seam where an hour before there had been a wall. Cold came out of it, and a smell of great age and dry paper and something mineral beneath. Beyond it a stair went down into a dark our lamps would not reach the bottom of. Ka-Leth of Justice, keeper of the complete and unbroken record of eleven thousand years, stood at the top of a stair his office had never known existed, and could not make himself take the first step.\par

Sarah did not come down. She stayed at the threshold with her instrument against the jamb, reading whether the stone would move again, and said she would send up for Shield to look at the mechanism before anyone went deeper. But Paul was already moving; he could not stand at the mouth of a record no one had ever heard and wait for a procedure. It was Paul who went first, with the little machine already turning, before any Shield hand had climbed down. I went after him, because the door had opened for the Mountain that fired the Beast and I was the eye that had fired it; if anyone alive had the right or the curse to read what waited at the bottom of that stair, it was me. And behind us, after a long moment, we heard Justice begin, slowly, to come down into the record he had never been permitted to keep.\par

I set my hand on the cold jamb of the door as I passed through it, the way I had set it against the Mountain's skin the night the first blow fell. The stone of the jamb was not like the stone of the Mountain. It had been cut differently, by different hands, to a different standard, older and finer and more certain of itself. We were about to walk out of the Mountain my people built and into the Mountain that another hand had built, and they were not the same Mountain at all. Everything I had been raised to call eternal and God-given had once been a set of drawings on a table in front of a living man, who had left his name, and his reasons, and his defence of himself, at the foot of a stair that only the ending of a world could open.\par
