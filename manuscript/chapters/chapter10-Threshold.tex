\typesetchapter{Chapter 10}{Threshold}

\begin{room}

--- The second of April; the ninth of our interviews. Before we return to your account, \textit{Rome} can wait, did you read the translation of \textit{Genesis} I gave you?

--- You promised me the first chapter and gave me four; thank you, Paul, though I do not yet know whether it was a kindness.

--- The first murder was the earliest sensible place to stop. Keep one thing in mind: a shared shape does not tell you which came first, nor that either is true.

--- Yes, I understand that now. Your Sagan, Attenborough and Darwin, taken together, were more convincing about our origins than any scripture I have ever read. Nevertheless, your murderer resembles ours. You call him Cain, son of Adam; ours is his grandson. The envy, the secret killing, the blood in the ground, the exile, the gate shut behind him --- all of it kept, and the Mountain lost. You descend from the murderer's line by your own scripture, not mine. What I have not quite understood is Eve. Your God forbids her the fruit and swears it will kill her; the serpent tells her the boundary may guard God, not her. She looks, she crosses, and her eyes open. My Mountain would call that an ascension. She is Light, or perhaps the serpent is. Either way, you call it a fall, and you call the one who opened her eyes evil. Why?

--- Listen to what you asked. To decide whether the serpent's gift was good or evil, I must already possess the knowledge of good and evil --- and that knowledge is the fruit.

--- Paul, is that a Faith trick you are using on me?

--- It is part of the story's circularity. One can judge the serpent, or one can judge the motive of the author who placed him there. What the story says and what the story is intended or used for are two separate questions.

--- In yours the woman crosses by her own hand and is blamed; in ours she is carried across as spoil and kept blameless because she never chooses. Then your God sentences her to multiplied sorrow in conceiving and bearing children, and says that her husband shall rule over her. Is that the price of having chosen once: to be forbidden to choose ever after?

--- Perhaps. Though I notice neither author troubled to ask her, and both were writing for men who would rather not have been told.

--- This time you can ask the one who crossed. Hear me before you name it ascension, illumination, revelation or fall. No hand carried me over. The choice was mine.

\end{room}

Ka-Raedin left me on the north side of the Hall of Ceremony, before the forbidden circle where the great Pendulum swung, and told me to give it the whole of my mind until I found what moved. After a year in which every problem had been mine to choose, I disliked being given one. By the twentieth day I would have traded it for any three of my own. By the fortieth I slept beside the Hall between rounds, woke with chalk on my fingers and resented any foot that crossed my marks. The Pendulum did nothing but pass before me, a dark blade crossing its mark, while my explanations died one after another beneath the swing.\par

One grey morning I set the angle of the still northern star upon Justice's engraved circle. The wall-shadow of its unit stick was the fraction I had lacked. I divided each day's measured drift by it, then seven days together, then all forty. The results agreed: the Mountain completed one turn while the fixed stars returned, and at our angle the floor carried the Pendulum through a full circle once in two and three-quarter days. If the Pendulum kept its line, the Mountain turned beneath it.\par

I went down to Ka-Raedin with the stick, the angles, the forty days of marks and my division. He made me begin again whenever I used a number where a word should have stood, then took the slate from me and worked the final line in his own hand. When he reached my answer, he set his palm flat upon the slate and looked through the wall toward the Pendulum, as if the stone had become briefly transparent.\par

``You found it,'' he said. He kept his palm on my figures. ``A year in the cold, and you come down holding the eye, the number and the stone in one hand. Count the people in this Mountain who could follow what you have just shown me. You will not need the second hand. You are younger than the next by thirty years.''\par

Then he told me what the finding had earned. Faith had kept a record of the turning since before the Mountain numbered its own years. Justice preserved the number as an inheritance sealed against interpretation. Va-Maera alone still read the changing direction aloud in the Hall of Prayer and called it the course of God. I was to carry her my reckoning and bring her observations back to Ka-Raedin.\par

I had never entered the inner Hall of Prayer. I expected height, processions, the white fire of ceremony. I found Va-Maera alone at a low table with her sleeves tied back, blackening the edge of a brass sight with soot. Beside her lay columns of angles written in three hands, a cord worn shiny by use, and a bowl of lamp oil in which she was cooling a pin.\par

She did not ask why Light had sent their Veil into her inner ground. She pointed to the free side of the table. ``Put down what you believe you have proved.''\par

I laid out the swing, the star-angle and the turning ground. Before Va-Maera objected, I told her the mark described the ground moving beneath the Pendulum and not the course of God. She listened without defending either name. When I had finished, she took the oldest of her tables from its wrapping and asked where the mark should have stood on six nights chosen from years before my birth.\par

I worked until my confidence became sweat. Five of the six agreed with my rule as closely as the old instrument allowed. The last did not. Va-Maera turned the tablet over and showed me, on the reverse, a note that the south bearing had been repaired between those observations. I corrected for the changed bearing and the sixth mark entered the width allowed by the cord. ``I chose that night to see whether you would erase the inconvenient mark,'' she said.\par

``Then you accept it,'' I said.\par

``I accept that your number predicts a mark which repeats.''\par

``And will you stop calling it the course of God?''\par

She took her eyes from the slate. ``Your ground turns about what?''\par

``An axis. Its northern end points into the empty place beside the still star.''\par

``A direction in Heaven occupied by no light, around which every light appears to turn. And that is not the direction of God?'' She placed one finger on the old heading. ``You have corrected our motion. Have you disproved our direction?''\par

``I have disproved what the words say the Pendulum is doing.''\par

She placed my finger on the oldest entry, then on the corrected angle two hands below it. Farther down, the same mistaken digit entered every copy at once. ``That is when the first table entered Faith's keeping,'' she said. ``Do you imagine I chose every word I inherited? Light received the Pendulum before it understood the swing. Faith received the mouths that stand beneath it. My office preserves what was received. If I erase every error after I recognise it, the next person inherits only my certainty.'' She pushed the tablet towards me. ``Write your correction beneath ours. Do not erase what you have not disproved.''\par

I wrote my rule beneath the old heading. Va-Maera laid out six dates chosen across one year and covered three entries on each: First Light, the line of true noon set against Third Measure, and Fifth Blade. ``The Pendulum gives you its next round for nothing,'' she said. ``The day does not. Tell me where these marks stood.''\par

I returned with the year of solar heights I had taken through smoked glass. I used the angle of the northern star, the sun's changing height and the small margin it lost each day against the fixed stars. My first three placed Fifth Blade before First Light: I had carried one of Justice's shadows in the wrong direction. I began again. When I had set all eighteen positions, Va-Maera uncovered Faith's table. Each entered the width allowed by the cord.\par

She turned over a clean slip. ``Two days from now. First Light. True noon against Third Measure. Fifth Blade.''\par

I gave her all three positions. She made me write them, fold the slip and close it beneath my seal. ``If the sun finds your marks, we continue. If it does not, you return before deciding which of us has failed.'' She turned back to the brass sight and did not raise her eyes again.\par

Two days later the first edge of the sun crossed Faith's blackened sight within the width of my First Light line. At the day's height, the sight's shadow stopped and reversed within my noon line, short of Third Measure by the distance I had given. At dusk the sun's last edge left the sight within the width of Fifth Blade. Va-Maera opened my slip only after she had entered all three observations beside it. She offered no praise and changed no prayer. She wrapped my proof together with the table that called the turning divine. Thereafter I carried sealed predictions and observations between Light and Faith.\par

Twice each week I crossed the old service spine above the cedar descent, passed the disused oil shelves and followed the southern vents until their cold had softened into Mountain air. I began recording every draught, lamp and angle along the way.\par

On my fourth crossing, with the lamps behind me trimmed low, a point of light appeared through one of the southern vents on the new road to Faith. It was no larger than a grain of salt and seemed to come from beyond the stone. I saw it for the length of one step. When I stopped I had passed the line and it was gone; one step back brought it again.\par

The year beneath the lenses had taught me the difference between light made and light returned. I lowered the nearest lamp and the point sharpened. I shielded the flame and it remained. When I raised it again, glare thinned the point but did not move it. It held only with my head against one seam, on the line where outside air entered before it became ours.\par

The vent opened south-east. The pale point lay where the second ridge would have stood beyond the Mountain. Va-Sheva's warning returned to me: \textit{Do not point.} I had obeyed her before understanding.\par

A reflected point might be no more than a mineral face catching stray light. A cold thread through a sealed vent might be an old repair. Dust worn thin beside a stair no one used might be the work of rats or careless feet. I had three small facts. Irregularities could be nothing or something; until followed, they were neither. Beams were Light's to follow. If this one came from outside the Mountain, the question belonged to Shield as well. I reported none of them. Shield would close the road before I knew whether it defended the Mountain or exposed it.\par

For twelve nights I did nothing that could yet be named trespass. I watched from the permitted passage, covered and uncovered the nearest lamp and memorised the positions at which the point appeared. The lamp changed only the glare around it. The point failed when blown dust veiled the hidden opening and sharpened after the vent keepers cleared the upper throat. One narrow position brought eye, opening and second ridge into line. By the twelfth night I had learnt which stars to seek to follow the bearing in darkness.\par

By then inquiry had become a task I imposed on myself. I knew leaving the Mountain was forbidden. But all my years of corridors, sight-lines, counts and concealed marks had trained me to pass without notice; here was a way to test whether I could. If I found the source and returned unseen, I would bring the Mountain a discovery instead of a suspicion. I repeated that purpose until trespass sounded like service.\par

On ordinary nights, after Fifth Blade, every unappointed body returned to its sleeping chamber. The passages emptied, the Mirror stilled and Shield could give a name to almost any movement. Mourning alone kept the Mountain awake: kin, Faith and attendants crossed its lower ways until dawn, and Shield drew wardens inward to hold the Hall and its approaches. The outer watch was not abandoned, but it was thinned.\par

A child's death gave me such a night. The fever took the child before its naming, and so I have no name to place here, only the silence I used. The vent keepers had completed their last round before the mourning and would not return until First Light. I used the space their grief had made.\par

Since the Equal Light that closed my year beneath the sky, the nights had lengthened. I waited until the Pendulum had passed Fifth Blade and entered the long unspoken reach before the next First Light. My year of solar measures told me how wide that winter reach would be; it had scarcely begun. I wrapped a spark-stone against the damp and carried no lamp.\par

The lower stone yielded where its edge had been shaved thin. Beyond it, handholds had been cut into the passage without any office sign. Some were deep and polished; some had been widened later; lower down, a second line of holds suited a shorter reach, and one broken lip had been repaired with a pin whose metal had gone black. The passage had been altered for more than one reach and repaired after use.\par

Cold moved through the cracks ahead with the irregular breath of weather. At the last slab I put both hands to the hidden grip and waited through twenty breaths. No foot answered. I pressed. The slab gave, and the night entered around its edge. I opened it wide enough to pass, stepped outside and drew it shut behind me.\par

The night made scale uncertain. By day the desert had shown me a floor, a horizon and the Mountain standing between them; now stone, sand and distance shared one black surface beneath the stars. The still northern star restored one direction. I set the south-eastern bearing from the three fixed points I had used in the observatory. My remembered view from the Oasis and the line through the vent placed the ridge within a broad count. I had also chosen the long low dune that crossed the direct ground. I walked until its spine stood between the Mountain and me, then kept below it. My feet counted in silence.\par

The cold came horizontally. Wind pressed my robe against one side of me and filled the other, pulling warmth from my lips through the cloth. In a hollow the dune covered the lower stars. Its spine divided, and for a while each black rise offered the same bearing. I chose one, counted past the distance I had allowed and began to believe I had walked beside the ridge without seeing it. My lifted hand struck stone before my eyes found the rock.\par

I lowered myself and turned back toward the Mountain. Two Shield wardens crossed its outer shoulder, their lamps hooded between each patch of ground. Neither light stopped, widened or returned along my line. I waited until both disappeared and took their going on as confirmation that they had not seen me.\par

I searched the face turned toward the Mountain first, moving both hands over the rock. The polished plate lay behind a lip no wider than my hand, cold and smooth against the stone. It had given me the flash and the pale point. I found no seam around it. Any door there would show itself to the Mountain each time it opened. The plate marked the bearing; the entrance had to lie where the ridge itself hid its use.\par

I went around the rock with one hand upon it, keeping its whole mass between the Mountain and me. On the far side I searched from the ground upward, lost the line twice and began again. At last my fingers entered a shallow engraving: a horizontal line broken twice, with a small notch turning inward. Shield. Above it, hollows rose at the intervals of hands and feet. Their lower lips had been rounded and their dust rubbed away. People had climbed there.\par

I followed the worn holds into a fold above the ground. Behind it my hand found a straight seam and, beneath the seam, a grip protected from sand. I could still return with the plate, the mark and the climb as my discovery. Returning would also let Keeper address Keeper and close the place before I learned whether the road defended the Mountain or deceived it. I pressed.\par

The mechanism resisted, then moved with the thick quiet of something old and tended. As it gave, a metal tongue struck once somewhere beyond the stone. I took the sound for the end of the lock. Dry air breathed out. I slipped inside and pulled the stone fully home behind me. The ridge, the stars and the Mountain vanished together. I felt along the wall until my hand entered a niche and found a lamp standing in oil. Only then did I unwrap the spark-stone and make light.\par

The passage descended sideways before opening into a corridor cut without ornament. Dust lay thin across the floor. In places it held the edges of footprints; in others a sleeve or carried burden had brushed the wall clean. One heel remained sharp and the next had collapsed into powder. They might have been days old or years.\par

The entrance lamp found one shelf. As the flame widened, shelves rose tier above tier and receded farther than the light could follow, all of them occupied: broken stone, painted wood, bowls, blades, corroded instruments, pieces whose uses were no longer known.\par

Every object bore Shield's broken threshold. The tags named source, condition, handling and danger; none invited study by another office. Down several tiers and across hands separated by centuries, one instruction returned: \textit{harmless unless taught.}\par

The clearer footprint crossed two rows, vanished beneath a fallen wrapping and began again beside a painted coffin long as a man. I moved the wrapping to find the heel and uncovered the upper half of the lid instead: across it stood a falcon-headed man crowned by a red sun-disc. A small black serpent rose from the disc's lower edge and held its head against the red. I could not have named him in the archive. The lesson on Egypt has since given me his name: Ra.\par

The oldest tag called him \textit{sun-lord; corruption of eye-of-Heaven memory; pigments fugitive; keep covered.} Someone later had scored through \textit{corruption} and written beneath it: \textit{precedence unestablished.} A third keeper had sealed both statements in place.\par

The cut through \textit{corruption} had bitten deep enough to score the wood beneath the tag. The correction sat above it; the third seal held both. I rubbed a thumb across condemnation, correction and seal, then set the tag down with all three hands visible.\par

The displaced wrapping had protected a painted board. An old man stood over a bound boy laid on a stone, a knife raised in one hand. Behind him a ram had caught its horns in thorns. From a break in the painted sky, a hand reached down and closed around the man's wrist. The order was plain: God above, father over son, the blade between them, and the animal spared.\par

My shoulder caught another panel propped behind me. I stopped it before it fell and brought its clouded glass through the lamp-light. A man had been fixed through hands and feet to crossed beams; his head hung forward, his ribs showed, and blood ran from a wound in his side. Gold remained around his head. Several figures knelt or reached toward him. One covered a face; another held the hanging body below the wound.\par

I repeated the words of the \textit{Book of the Beast} silently:\par

\begin{scripture}
And God looked upon the Outsiders, and saw the works of their hands; and behold, their cruelty was greater than the curse that had been laid upon them. For they tortured for pleasure, and conquered for pride, and lied with holy words, and slew their children for the hunger of their fathers.
\end{scripture}

Here a father raised the blade over his son, and worship knelt before a tortured body opened on crossed wood. I took both as the curse made visible.\par

I set the panel down too quickly and heard wood touch stone. The sound travelled farther than I wanted. For a while I moved without raising the lamp, passing shelves at the edge of sight: a gold mouth, a child's clay foot, black feathers sealed between glass, half a sentence in red. I did not stop again until the footprints crossed my path.\par

The prints ended at a shallow tray of optical pieces. Among them lay a lens whose edge carried the shallow service cuts of Light. I knew the sequence: first grinding, correction, approval. A foreign element had been mounted behind it in a ring too fine for any Mountain workshop, its curvature unlike ours. I raised the assembly toward the distant tags. Lines that had been brown blurs snapped apart. Cracks in ink, fibres in paper and grains of dust appeared with a clarity that made my own best work feel performed with water.\par

Ka-Raedin had counted on one hand every mind in the Mountain able to follow my proof. The corrective ring bore no maker's name. I could not tell whether it was the work of one of the Outsiders' greatest minds or an exercise given to their apprentices.\par

The Shield label was brief: \textit{Mountain manufacture. Removed through Threshold. Returned altered. Demonstration and circulation prohibited.} Beneath it, in a later hand: \textit{corrective element stable.} I wanted to carry the assembly straight to Ka-Raedin. Then I read \textit{circulation prohibited} again and kept the lens in my hand.\par

Through the altered lens, the high labels on the newer cabinets separated into legible strokes. They named peoples I had never heard of: families on islands, hunters among ice, villages under trees so tall that a whole Mountain might have stood beneath them. Each file carried a different place and the same black tree impressed into its cover.\par

The Shield summaries repeated one outside formula: \textit{autonomy claimed; contact refused; representation asserted by the Blackwood Foundation.} Maps, petitions and photographs filled the files. Some held no more than a rumour and a boundary. Others had names, genealogies, inventories of weapons, diseases and men authorised to speak. Court seals and official replies accumulated behind the petitions, sometimes until the argument about a people was thicker than the record of the people themselves. \par

One thin file began with an outsider request to cut a road through a red-stone valley. The black tree answered it. The request returned under another authority; the tree answered again. Three changes of seal and two generations of names passed across the pages while the refusal remained the same, until the proposed road bent north and left the valley blank. No member of the hidden people appeared anywhere in the dispute. Every official reply was addressed to Blackwood.\par

I passed the file twice because nothing upon its cover named the Mountain. It stood among all the others, neither thicker nor more guarded, and wore the same Blackwood tree. Only when I returned to the first cabinet and read every cover in order did I stop at it. On a festival pillar, Light hid the working mark among a hundred marks equally bright. Here, every cover bore the tree.\par

I returned the altered lens to its exact hollow after opening the file. As I trimmed the wick, a faint metal answer travelled from the Mountain end of the chamber. I thought of the tongue that had struck when the outer door opened. I listened, heard nothing further, and opened another file.\par

The first photograph inside showed a fair-haired woman under a hard flat light. Beside her stood a smiling man in desert clothes, his weight divided equally over both feet and his hands free of his robe. I knew that stillness from Shield. Behind them rose a fold of rock I knew in my body before I knew it with my eye: the Mountain seen from forbidden ground. Two names and a date had been copied beneath the outsider's marks. Sarah Blackwood. Amastan. 1974.\par

The next image was all distance. A white flower towered from the horizon, too large to be fire and too ordered to be weather; the surrounding sky had been emptied of colour. The border carried one word and a number in the outsider hand, repeated below in Shield script: Reggane, 1960. In the drawer beneath lay a blistered piece of green-black desert, grains melted together around bubbles, tagged with the same place and number. Whatever had flowered in the photograph had changed sand into glass.\par

A map occupied the cabinet below. It showed the Mountain's outer folds, dry channels, wells and routes. Where the Oasis should have been, the paper held no name or mark. Tracks curved around the blank centre. Names stopped before reaching it and began again beyond it. Every useful line bent around the blank. Without the photograph, I would have read it as empty ground.\par

One route broke the pattern. It ran from the outcrop inward, divided beneath the rock and touched service ways near the Hall of Ceremony. Along it, small boxed words repeated in several hands: \textit{Breach. Containment. Exchange. Loss. Sealed.} A newer entry carried a phrase I had never encountered in any Shield rule: \textit{Threshold Exception.}\par

At the back of the bundle, a loose copy had split along its fold. Most of the entry remained with the missing half, but the torn strip preserved three names in Mountain hand --- Sarah Blackwood, Amastan, Ka-Xhian --- and below them only \textit{Threshold Exception}. It did not tell me what had been permitted, who had requested it or what price had been paid. Ka-Xhian's name stood beside the two outsiders' names and above an exception to Shield's own law.\par

I folded the strip once. Taking it would be breach, and the missing strip might expose the theft. Leaving it meant allowing the chamber to become impossible again as soon as I stepped out. I slid it into the inner seam of my robe. Then I returned to the first photograph. It lay loose beneath four paper corners. I lifted them one by one and put the photograph beside the strip. The map kept its torn edge; the empty corners kept the rectangle of the image. I had left Shield two absences to find.\par

Against the far wall lay the divided body of one machine. Two long side panels each held a door; the rear closure had been made in an upper and lower half; a broad front panel, painted black against the lighter body, rested above a metal engine with eight equal throats. Upright wheels, seats, lamps, springs, a steering wheel, a protective cage and a second fuel vessel occupied separate bays. None of it could have passed whole through the Threshold. Someone had dismantled it outside, carried every piece in and accounted for the machine as an anatomist accounts for a body.\par

One side panel bore a white square painted with the number 195. Near it, beneath scratches and sun-bleaching, remained the black tree stamped on the files. Desert sand filled the lower seam; a dry grass seed had survived behind the window channel. Shield's inventory dated it to the turn into 1980 and ended: \textit{second contact; field cover concluded; body divided at Threshold; no part released.}\par

I was crouched beside the frame when I noticed a second lamp in the wall niche. Its wick was dark, but the brass above it was warm beneath my fingers. Beside the niche, a narrow tongue of metal stood proud of the wall by the width of a nail. I had heard its twin strike when I opened the outer door.\par

Stone moved somewhere behind me.\par

I pinched out the entrance lamp and entered a darkness so complete that the remembered shelves continued to stand in my sight. The dismantled machine offered spaces without safety. I lowered myself behind two leaning panels, one hand holding them apart to keep their edges from touching, the other pressed against the paper inside my robe.\par

A narrow line opened at the Mountain end of the chamber. A Shield warden came through sideways and closed the stone behind her. She was a little shorter than I. Exterior cloth widened her shoulders and covered her mouth and hair; dust whitened her boots to the ankle. A sealed field packet was strapped beneath one arm. She went first to the metal tongue, pressed it flush with her thumb and waited until something answered inside the wall. Then she struck a spark into the warm lamp I had touched moments before and lifted it. The panel against my shoulder settled. One grain of trapped sand ran down its hollow with a sound so slight I felt it more than heard it.\par

The warden stopped. Her lamp rose. Light entered the seams around me, found a rim, a spring, the pale underside of the steering wheel. I could see the stitching at her wrist and, below it, Shield's broken threshold worked black on black. The beam rested on my hiding place long enough for the pulse in my raised arm to move the skin.\par

Then the lamp moved on. She crossed the archive, operated the outer mechanism and went into the desert. The stone closed. Wind ceased. I remained behind the panels until the arm supporting them shook hard enough to risk what fear had not.\par

I relit the entrance lamp. The flame rose smaller than before. I expected the warden to find no one outside, study the disturbed dust and return. I could go after her into ground I believed favoured her eyes, or take the passage from which she had emerged while it was empty and learn where Threshold entered the Mountain. I turned inward.\par

Beyond the hidden stone, a service tunnel climbed beneath the Mountain. Its walls carried fresh scrapes at shoulder height and old oil in the hinge recesses. Far ahead, a door bore three Shield seals, one cut over another, the oldest so deep that the newer hands had worked around it. I did not approach it. The side opening before it carried two office marks on the same lintel: Faith and Shield. A smell came from it before I could decide whether to approach the sealed door.\par

The archive had smelled of dry mineral and paper. From the side opening came older air gone wrong: piss, sweat and, beneath both, the flat iron of blood. The chamber beyond had no visible walls. Long wedges faced with pressed wool and hair projected inward in ordered ranks, floor to ceiling; more descended from above, hundreds of blunt points aimed into the room. At the edges, an open metal lattice took the place of the floor. Through it I saw another field of wedges rising from the darkness below. The room had grown teeth upon every surface and turned all of them inward. At the centre, a square island of bare stone carried one fixed seat, rings at the wrists and ankles, and a narrow gutter running from between the feet to a covered drain. Black staining had entered the island's seams and remained after washing. \par

It was the most complete silence I had known. My ears tightened as on a fast descent. I swallowed; the sound filled my skull, and behind it came the pulse in my neck. The floor shifted under me until I put a hand against the entrance frame. At the foot of the seat, dried blood held one of Va-Sheva's ribbons to the stone. The colour and the tight thread along one edge made it hers. Had she asked the questions there, or answered them? We had all heard stories of Shield's private ordeals, what it might make a warden endure before judging her worthy of being Veil. I lifted it. Dried blood broke from the stone. I folded the ribbon once into the inner seam of my robe and went back through the archive.\par

\ornament

At the outer end I returned the entrance lamp to its niche, pinched it out and waited until the dark gave me back the wall. When I opened the stone on the ridge's hidden face, I could see neither the outcrop, the Mountain nor my own hands. The warden might have been ten steps away or halfway to the horizon. I listened until the effort made sounds of my pulse, and heard no foot on stone. A hooded light opened beyond a low shoulder to my left, closed, then opened again nearer the outcrop. At each opening it lit one patch of ground; between them she moved in darkness. At its third opening the light passed across the stone above my head. I lay against the rock until it closed. Above me, the moving light was gone. The still star remained.\par

Between two openings of the warden's lamp, I reached the Mountain-facing side of the ridge and sighted along the polished plate toward the shoulder of the Mountain. I fixed that line against the still star. I still had the outward count. Whenever the bearing wandered, I corrected it toward north. I left when the hood closed and walked the count back. The first opening fell short. The second crossed the ground where I had been and paused at the beginning of my prints. Whether she had seen them I could not tell. Wind had left their centres clear. I wanted to run. I kept the pace by which I had counted outward and put each foot on stone where stone offered itself. When one black shoulder resembled the entrance more closely than the one ahead, I kept the bearing.\par

At the final count my hands met ordinary cold stone. I moved along the rock by the width the vent required, found the shaved edge under my fingers and pressed before fear could make me search elsewhere. Warmth leaked through the seam. I opened the slab, entered and drew it shut before the searching glow reached the opening. I reset the inner stone, brushed the disturbed dust into the older wear and cleaned the fresh soot from my fingers.\par

From far below came the last answer of the mourning, thinned by stone until the words were gone and only the cadence remained.\par

I did not go to Ka-Raedin. I believed he would summon Shield before I possessed a second witness; the strip would vanish into the chamber from which I had taken it, and my word would stand against its record of my breach. I did not confront Va-Sheva while I could not tell whether the ribbon accused her or asked me to save her. Shield knew Threshold had opened. The missing ribbon would tell Shield how far the intruder had reached; it did not yet know who had opened Threshold, from which side, or how much had been seen. I used that uncertainty and kept mine.\par

At the first lamps, the torn strip crackled beside Va-Sheva's folded ribbon beneath my robe. The photograph lay flat against both. I pressed all three to my ribs and entered the waking passage with Sarah Blackwood's face touching my skin.\par

\begin{room}

--- You interrupted me a single time today.

--- I wanted the account clean and true as much as possible. And we have come to a moment Sarah told me to expect. Tomorrow is Good Friday, and Sarah is available at last; she means to tell you herself how it began, and how that photograph was taken.

--- Why do you call a Friday good?

--- It has to do with the man you saw on the cross. It is long, and hard to explain. Where have you reached in Rome?

--- Augustus. Twenty-seven years before the birth from which you count.

--- Then the answer comes this afternoon.

\centerline{[\,---\,]}

--- There is one more thing, and it is not just the photograph she means to explain. Three days ago I went behind the glass at the hour Martinez changes the memory. I watched him open the second camera that points to the instruction room, take out the card and seal it in a paper sleeve. This time I followed him. In the town he put the sleeve beneath a folded newspaper on a bench and walked on without a backward look. Six minutes later a man I have never seen came for it.

--- Then the information exchanged was the eye behind the glass. The contraband mirror I suspected; the one you watched and left in place.

--- Yes. Whatever that camera had seen and heard since Martinez last changed its memory, this man carried away. I did not touch the packet, and I did not follow him. I had already spent my luck following Martinez. I took one photograph instead. Here.

--- He stands like Shield when Shield wishes to look idle. Past sixty. Hair cut close and grey. The shoulders have kept an old instruction after the uniform has gone. He did not come straight to the bench.

--- He passed it once, read the square in a shop window, returned against the people walking and lifted the paper without looking down. A civilian coat, a soldier's economy. I caught his face only when he turned. When Sarah is in this room, and not one hour before, we will lay it before her. If she knows neither the man nor the second camera, then Roger has been carrying an account of your instruction out of her house by hand. Until then it stays between the two of us.

\end{room}
