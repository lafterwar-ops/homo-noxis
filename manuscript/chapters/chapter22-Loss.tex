\typesetchapter{Chapter 22}{Loss}

We read him together, in the Archive, in the equinox light, taking the sheets in turn: Paul, who could hear a voice in a text the way I could see a hand in a stone; Sarah, who had hunted this man across sixty-six years without knowing his name; and I, who was about to learn that I had been reading my own possible life all along and calling it another man's confession.\par

I will set his account down as he made it, because the making is half of what it says. He did not write a life. He built one. It is cut in measures, and numbered, and it rhymes; and it rhymes in the cadence of the scripture my people have sung every morning for eleven thousand years, because the man who wrote that scripture could not, at the last, say even this in any other voice. He had a god's habit and he kept it to the end. Where I break in, I will say so. The rest is his.\par

What follows is Zero's.\par

\medskip

\medskip

They walked downhill, and they were glad,\\

Sarah laid the sheet down here. ``He is describing demographic collapse by assimilation,'' she said, ``and he is describing it correctly, and it is the one ending no wall and no weapon has ever stopped. My whole century argued it and never said it as cleanly as a man a hundred thousand years dead.'' Paul said, ``Mark where he is standing, though. He is not raging yet. He is a man watching his people fade and refusing, so far, to reach for the blade. Keep that. We are going to need it at the end.'' And I said nothing, because I had begun, without permission, to be afraid of this man, and I did not yet know why.\par

Sarah put the sheet down at that and sat with it, and I saw her go somewhere far off, into a cellar, among a dead father's papers. ``There it is,'' she said. ``The unasked question. My father died with a question in his mouth he would not ask me, and let me have the money instead. And I have declined my own questions, more than once, in front of men whose answers would have cost me the thing I had decided to do.'' She did not pick the sheet back up at once. ``He is describing my family, boy, and not only his. He is describing everyone's.''\par

Paul had been very still. ``This is the most dangerous document I have ever held,'' he said, ``and I once held a plan to end the world. It is dangerous because it is nearly true. He had a real enemy. His father was really murdered. A jilted boy who invented a grievance would be safe; we could dismiss it. He suffered a genuine, specific, evil act --- and took it, and made it permanent, and refused to check the one fact that might have kept it small.'' And Sarah, who knew more than any of us about what it is to convert a private certainty into an institution, said only, ``Yes. I wrote the shorter version. Keep reading. I want to know whether he ever asks it. I never did.''\par

\ornament

I did not, in the room, say what I was thinking, which was that I had begun to write this same document myself, not long before, in the desert, the night my own council cast me out. I had stood with my whole life taken from me and started, already, to compose the doctrine of my grievance. I had been stopped by nothing grander than a fact-checker with a machine, who made me say the small true things one at a time until the large false one had no room left to grow. I had been one refused question away from the man on the sheet in Paul's hands.\par

And I was afraid, in the equinox light, because I had my own Ithe, and I had her in the room. Va-Sheva had chosen a man of the outside who bought sweets for no reason --- and I knew now, we all knew now, that the sweets and the charm and the warmth had begun as an operation, that Jean-Charles had been sent into a proud house exactly as Ithe had been sent into mine. The mirror went past resemblance. It was the same event, a hundred thousand years apart. And I felt the low door open in me, the one Zero had walked through, the one that says: \emph{there, you see, it is their nature; they reach us through our loves; she is Ithe and he is the enemy's instrument and it is proven now, build the wall.}\par

Paul set down the last sheet of that day very gently, the way you set down a sleeping child, and no one spoke, and the equinox beam had moved off the machine and onto the wall while we read, the way it moves, a year at a time, patient, indifferent. And I found that they were all looking at me, Paul and Sarah and Va-Sheva, not unkindly, and waiting, and I understood that they had all three arrived, by their different roads, at the thing I had been refusing since the first sheet, and were waiting to see whether I would say it before they had to.\par

So I said it. ``He sounds like me,'' I said. ``He stood where I am standing, with the same wound and the same low door, and he walked through it, and built this. I am one refused question away from him.'' It cost me more than anything else in this whole account to say it, and Va-Sheva, who was the woman in my own version of his story, watched me say it and did not spare me and did not comfort me, which was the truest thing she could have done, and only said: ``Yes. He does. Now read the rest, and find out whether that is a warning to you or a permission. Because you have not decided which, and I can see that you have not, and it is the only thing left in this Mountain that still frightens me.''\par

I did not answer her, because she was right, and because the honest answer would have been to say that I did not know; that I had read a hundred thousand years down into the root of my own kind and found a man built to exactly my shape, holding exactly my wound, tempted by exactly my door; and that the thing I felt looking at him was horror, and under the horror a terrible companionship, the relief of the badly alone at learning that someone, once, had felt the precise thing and been unable to bear it either. I had come down that stair believing I would read the confession of a monster and be set free by the distance between us. There was no distance. And everything that came after --- the Mountain, the drill, the children, the god --- I read across that one closed gap, the way a man reads the history of a country while standing in the middle of it.\par
