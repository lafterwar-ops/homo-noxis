\typesetchapter{Chapter 22}{Loss}

We read him together, in the Archive, in the equinox light, taking the sheets in turn: Paul, who could hear a voice in a text the way I could see a hand in a stone; Sarah, who had hunted this man across forty years without knowing his name; and I, who was about to learn that I had been reading my own possible life all along and calling it another man's confession.\par

I will set his account down as he wrote it, in his own first person, because that is how it must be met, the way I made Paul meet mine. Where I break in, I will say so. The rest is his. I ask you to watch one thing as you read him, because it is the whole of him and he never once saw it: that he had a true enemy, and a true grief, and a true murder to grieve; and that he took the one operation that wounded him and made of it the eternal nature of a whole kind of men. Watch him turn an accusation he could not prove into a certainty he could build upon. That turn is the Mountain.\par

What follows is Zero's.\par

\ornament

Let it be recorded, by the one who will not be recorded elsewhere, that what follows was chosen and not received; that a mind stood behind it; and that the mind was mine.\par

I was born into the last generation of my people that still believed itself the first of the world, and I will not pretend to you, whoever you are, whatever you are, that we were a small people, or a rude one, or a people you would have pitied had you passed us on a road. We were the accomplishment of the world. We had held the cold lands and the warm for longer than any count then kept; we had made tools that other tools could not improve upon for an age; we knew the movements of the lights in the sky and had names for them and songs that fixed the names; we buried our dead with intention and marked the places, and we did not think ourselves clever for any of it, because a people at the summit of the world does not think itself clever any more than a mountain thinks itself tall. We simply were the shape the world had been climbing toward. Everyone knew it. It was the one thing that did not need to be said, which is always the most dangerous thing a people can possess, because a thing that does not need to be said is a thing no one has examined in a hundred generations.\par

I will give you one image of us, so that you do not do what the scripture I later built did, and picture us as brutes with clubs, because the ones who win always draw the ones they replaced as beasts; it makes the replacing easier to live with. Picture, then, a hall of my people deep in the warm rock at the height of a winter, lit by lamps that burned a rendered fat we had spent an age learning to make burn clean and tall; and in that hall, old men reciting the descent of a line for eleven generations without a single error, because to lose a generation of the line was to lose a piece of the world, and the reciting was checked, name against name, by others who had learned it apart, so that no one failing memory could corrupt the whole; and the young sitting to learn it, not because they were made to, but because there was nowhere in that world they would rather be than at the centre of the thing that knew itself completely. We did not write, or we wrote little and buried it. We kept the world in trained heads, cross-checked, redundant, a library of persons. It was the most beautiful engine for keeping a truth that has ever existed, and it had one flaw, and the flaw is what killed us: a library of persons is only as loyal as the persons, and a person can walk away. A written thing cannot come to prefer the neighbours. A person can. We had built our whole immortality out of the one material in the world that is able to choose to leave, and we had never once had to notice, because for an age no one wished to.\par

And we were losing, and I was among the first of us to say the word, because I was among the first positioned to see the whole of it and cursed with the kind of mind that cannot leave a whole unlooked-at.\par

We were not losing a war. That is the first thing, and the thing hardest to hold, because everything I built afterward was made to make you forget it. What was killing us could not be met with a blade, because it came as a preference. Our daughters went down to the new people's fires in the evenings, at first to trade, then to listen, then for no reason they could give when we asked them, which was itself the sign, because a person of our people could always give a reason; we were made of reasons. They said only that it was warmer there, and they did not mean the fires. One beloved daughter at a time, walking downhill toward the warmth, married into the easy mess of the new people, carrying children who would hold a quarter of us and none of our knowing. Not fled. Not taken. Gone, and glad. That is how a people at the summit of the world dies: not in a battle it could have trained a generation to fight, but in the slow glad drowning of its own blood into the blood of people who were, to our children, simply more pleasant to be near.\par

\ornament

Sarah laid the sheet down here. ``He is describing demographic collapse by assimilation,'' she said, ``and he is describing it correctly, and it is the one ending no wall and no weapon has ever stopped, because you cannot fortify against your own children preferring the neighbours. My whole century argued it and never said it as cleanly as a man a hundred thousand years dead.'' Paul said, ``Mark where he is standing, though. He is not raging yet. He is a man watching his people fade and refusing, so far, to reach for the blade. Whatever he becomes, he begins as the one arguing against catastrophe. Keep that. We are going to need it at the end.'' And I said nothing, because I had begun, without permission, to be afraid of this man, and I did not yet know why.\par

\ornament

My father saw the ending before I did, and my father's answer was war.\par

He held more of our world than any man of our people then living: the labour, the metal, the knowledge, the massed armed strength of the greatest of the Noxius houses. And he had looked at the slow drowning of our blood and concluded, in the flat way of his generation, that a people does not survive a preference by being preferred; it survives by making the alternative impossible. Strike the new people now, he said, while we still hold the advantage of the body and the tool and the disciplined line; break them before they are many; a hard age bought now against a soft extinction bought later. He was not a cruel man. He was a frightened one, and his fear had had longer than mine to become certainty.\par

And I refused him. I want that on the record above everything else, because the scripture I later built erased it, and because it is the true beginning: \emph{I was the one who said no.} I believed coexistence was possible. I thought his fear the fear of an old man who cannot bear to watch his world come to prefer another. I told him that a people who answered a preference with a slaughter would earn the ending it was trying to prevent; that we would become, in the winning of such a war, a thing not worth the saving. We argued it for years, across every table we shared, and I was winning the argument, because I was right; and being right, as it always was with us, was no defence at all against what was coming.\par

\ornament

Her name was Ithe, and I will say it here, against my own long rule, because a record that hides the one true thing is the same lie I built the Mountain to tell.\par

She was of my own people and of my own rank, raised as I was to a certain completeness: the songs, the star-names, the long descent of the line, the wide unhurried way of thinking that only the secure can afford. She was the most finished person I have ever known, and for a time she was mine, and then she was not. I will not dress the estrangement as her fault, because it was not; it was mine, and it was the same flaw that would later cut a whole civilisation. With me she had always known exactly who she was, and it had been, she told me once, like living inside a beautiful and perfectly made room that had no door. I required her to be one finished thing, forever, because her completeness was the mirror of my own and I could not bear a crack in it. So she left the room. She did not, then, go to any enemy. She simply went where she could be more than one thing, and I let her go with the cold grace of a man who is certain he has been wronged and intends to be admired for how well he bears it.\par

That is the door the new people found. I have said we could not be met with a blade. What I had not understood, what my father in his cruder fear understood better than I did, was that a proud house cannot be bought or frightened or flattered, but it can be entered through the one door pride always leaves standing open, which is love. They reached her in her unhappiness --- estranged, reachable, and of the innermost rank of the house they most wished to open. Whether they bought her, or frightened her, or persuaded her that she was serving a peace, or simply told her a story kind enough to believe, I do not know, and the not-knowing is the whole of what I am about to confess. They reached her. And they sent her back to me wearing reconciliation.\par

And I, who could not be bought or frightened or flattered, opened every door in myself to her the moment she came back, because she had come back, and a man winning again the one thing he thought he had lost does not audit the gift. I told her the small things a man tells the woman he is winning: the shape of my father's days, the passes he trusted, the season he would be in the open and lightly held. I did not think of them as secrets. That is the genius of the method; you never do. You think you are being loved.\par

\ornament

They killed my father with what she carried.\par

In a pass he had used his whole life, lightly guarded because I had told her it would be, by men of the new people who could not otherwise have come within a day of him. The one man whose war I had spent my life refusing was murdered by the outside, through the mouth of the woman I loved, in the exact manner he had spent his life warning me the outside would use. \par

I stood over him, and I said the two words that founded everything you are standing inside. I said: \emph{You were right.}\par

And here is the thing no scripture can hold and only I can tell you, because only I was there inside it: that under the grief, in the same breath as the grief, there was \emph{relief.} The argument I had been too tired to keep winning was over. I had not lost it; it had been taken from me, by a blade in a pass, and there is no ease on this earth like the ease of being handed by an enemy the permission to stop resisting the thing you were exhausted from resisting. For years I had held the harder, lonelier, better position, and defended it against a father I loved, and been right, and been slowly worn down by being right in a world that was ending anyway. And in one morning the enemy reached into my house and proved my father's case for him, and I was free. I could stop arguing. I could hate. Do you understand what a rest it is, to be finally permitted to hate? I have built a great deal on the strength of that one morning's rest.\par

\ornament

I could have learned the truth of her. I want the record exact about this, because it is the precise place where I became the founder of the Mountain and stopped being a man with a grief.\par

My father's people were very good at learning things from people; I had inherited them along with everything else, that same morning. I had the means to take Ithe and open her the way the new people had opened me, and learn whether she had known what her small carried things would buy, or whether she had been used as they used everything, without being told the price; whether she had tried to warn me and been too late; whether she had wept, choosing it, or wept, discovering it. I had the means, and I did not use them. I never asked her. I never had her asked. I let her vanish into the story I needed, and I did it on purpose, and here is the reason, and it is the whole engine of the world I built.\par

Because if I asked, and the answer was that she had not known --- that they had used her love as a road and never shown her its end --- then my father's murder was an accident of the enemy's cruelty and my own loose tongue, and not the settled nature of a kind of men. And then coexistence might still be possible. And then I would have to go back to the argument, the long grinding lonely argument, and keep being right in a dying world, with no father left to argue against and no permission left to hate. I could not go back to the argument. So I did the thing my father had done to me across every year of it, and the thing I would later build into five offices and an eternity of scripture: I looked away from the one question whose answer might have stopped me. I let her be guilty without proving her guilty, because a guilty Ithe was a reason, and an innocent Ithe was only a grief, and a reason can be built into a mountain and a grief can only be carried.\par

\ornament

Sarah put the sheet down at that and sat with it, and I saw her go somewhere far off, into a cellar, among a dead father's papers. ``There it is,'' she said. ``The unasked question. He had the one instrument that could have made his certainty smaller, and he declined to use it, because he had already decided what he needed it to say. I know that exact refusal. My father died with a question in his mouth he would not ask me, and let me have the money instead. And I have declined my own questions, more than once, in front of men whose answers would have cost me the thing I had decided to do. That is the whole engine of every terrible thing that was ever well funded: not a lie, but a question left unasked on purpose, by someone who already knows.'' She did not pick the sheet back up at once. ``He is not describing his family, boy. He is describing mine. He is describing everyone's.''\par

\ornament

And then I built the law before I built the Mountain, and the law was this: that what one operation had done was what all of them would always do; that one murder proved coexistence a child's dream; that one betrayal, of a depth I had refused to measure, was the fixed and eternal nature of an entire kind of men. I took a real wound, particular and dated and done by specific hands in a specific pass, and I grew it until it was the size of the species and the length of all time to come. My father had wanted a war. A war I might have forgiven myself, because a war ends. I gave him something worse. I gave him a machine that would fight his war for him forever, against every new man who would ever be born, most of whom would do me no wrong and know nothing of me, on the strength of a thing one frightened woman may not even have understood she was doing.\par

I never learned the truth of her. I need you to feel the size of that, because it is the hinge the whole Mountain turns on. I built the ending of the world upon an accusation I made certain I would never test. She may have loved me to the last and been spent like a coin. She may have hated me and chosen every step of it. She is, to me, exactly as unknown as she was the morning my father died, because I arranged her to stay that way. A man who wishes to keep his certainty does not go looking for the woman who could take it from him.\par

\ornament

Paul had been very still. ``This is the most dangerous document I have ever held,'' he said, ``and I once held a plan to end the world. It is dangerous because it is nearly true. He had a real enemy. His father was really murdered. The operation against him really happened. He is not a jilted boy who invented a grievance; that would be safe, we could dismiss it. He is a man who suffered a genuine, specific, evil act --- and took it, and made it permanent, and universal, and refused to check the one fact that might have kept it small. That is not the story of a broken heart. That is the story of every state that ever turned a real attack into a forever-war and called the forever part justice.'' And Sarah, who knew more than any of us about what it is to convert a private certainty into an institution, said only, ``Yes. I wrote the shorter version. And I too declined to learn the thing that would have made me smaller. Keep reading. I want to know whether he ever asks it. I never did.''\par

\ornament

I did not, in the room, say what I was thinking, which was that I had begun to write this same document myself, not long before, in the desert, the night my own council cast me out. I had stood with my whole life taken from me and started, already, to compose the doctrine of my grievance: the account in which I was the wronged whole and my people the shrugging betrayers who had earned whatever came to them. I had been stopped by nothing grander than a fact-checker with a machine, who made me say the small true things one at a time until the large false one had no room left to grow. I had been one refused question away from the man on the sheet in Paul's hands.\par

And I was afraid, in the equinox light, because I had my own Ithe, and I had her in the room. Va-Sheva had chosen a man of the outside who bought sweets for no reason --- and I knew now, we all knew now, that the sweets and the charm and the warmth had begun as an operation, that Jean-Charles had been sent into a proud house through the one door pride leaves open, exactly as Ithe had been sent into mine. The mirror was not a resemblance. It was the same event, a hundred thousand years apart. And I felt the low door open in me, the one Zero had walked through, the one that says: \emph{there, you see, it is their nature; they reach us through our loves; she is Ithe and he is the enemy's instrument and it is proven now, build the wall.} I understood, sitting in that cold, exactly how a good and reasonable man walks through that door, because I had my foot in it.\par

\ornament

My father was dead, and everything he had held came to me: the labour, the metal, the knowledge, the massed strength of the greatest of our houses. And I, who had refused his war, now held his entire world with which to wage a worse one. I did not raise an army. An army was his answer, and armies lose, and I do not lose. I gathered instead the sharpest minds of the age --- the ones who knew stone, and the ones who knew water, and the ones who knew the sky, and the ones who knew the making and working of metal, and, because a thing this valuable must be guarded, soldiers, for a plan born of a wound always makes a place in itself for men with weapons long before it can say aloud what they are for.\par

I took also, and I set it down here because it is the seed of the cruellest thing I did, the young. Not only engineers and soldiers but children: some of ours, some bought, some gathered from the failing edges of our people, because a place that must outlive its own understanding has to be peopled by those still young enough to be made to believe it was always so. I told myself they were being saved. They were being saved. And they were also being made into the first generation of a people that would be taught, from the cradle, a past that never happened, so that they would tend a machine they must never comprehend and kneel to a god I was about to invent. I looked at them asleep in the carts among the metal, and I did not turn back, because by then the certainty had finished its work in me and I was no longer a man who could turn back. I was a law that had learned to walk.\par

The road south took the better part of a year, and I lost people on it, to the heat and the distance and the slow leaking-away that is how a great column dies in a dead country, and each loss I entered in a reckoning and did not mourn, because a man who has decided to save the whole learns very quickly to spend the parts. We passed the last of the green, and the last of the water that anyone but my water-men could find, and the last of the marks of any people at all, and went on into a country that had never wanted anyone and said so every hour of the day. My engineers began to tell me, carefully, in the way men speak to a lord they have begun to fear, that there might be no place able to hold what I meant to hold; that I hunted a thing which did not exist. I did not turn back. I had stopped, somewhere on that road, being able to imagine an outcome in which I turned back, and I called that strength at the time. It was not strength. It was the certainty I had chosen over the truth of a woman, grown so large by then that it had eaten the horizon, so that there was no longer any direction anywhere that led to anything but forward, into the stone.\par

I went into the desert. I was young. I believed I was saving the world, and I was not lying to myself, or not only; my people truly were ending, and someone truly had murdered my father, and both of those were real, and that is precisely why I could not see what I was doing, because the wound had learned to speak only true sentences, and a grief that speaks only true sentences you will follow to the end of the world.\par

\ornament

Paul set down the last sheet of that day very gently, the way you set down a sleeping child, and no one spoke, and the equinox beam had moved off the machine and onto the wall while we read, the way it moves, a year at a time, patient, indifferent. And I found that they were all looking at me, Paul and Sarah and Va-Sheva, not unkindly, and waiting, and I understood that they had all three arrived, by their different roads, at the thing I had been refusing since the first sheet, and were waiting to see whether I would say it before they had to.\par

So I said it. ``He sounds like me,'' I said. ``He had a real enemy and a real grief and a real murder, and he made them mean everything, and he refused to learn the one thing that might have made them mean less. His Ithe and my Va-Sheva. His enemy's instrument and mine. He stood where I am standing, with the same wound and the same low door open in front of him, and he walked through it, and built this. I am one refused question away from him.'' It cost me more than anything else in this whole account to say it, and Va-Sheva, who was the woman in my own version of his story, watched me say it and did not spare me and did not comfort me, which was the truest thing she could have done, and only said: ``Yes. He does. Now read the rest, and find out whether that is a warning to you or a permission. Because you have not decided which, and I can see that you have not, and it is the only thing left in this Mountain that still frightens me.''\par

I did not answer her, because she was right, and because the honest answer would have been to say that I did not know; that I had read a hundred thousand years down into the root of my own kind and found a man built to exactly my shape, holding exactly my wound, tempted by exactly my door; and that the thing I felt looking at him was not only horror but a terrible companionship, the relief of the badly alone at learning that someone, once, had felt the precise thing and been unable to bear it either. I had come down that stair believing I would read the confession of a monster and be set free by the distance between us. There was no distance. That was the whole of what the Archive had to teach me, and it taught it on the first day, and everything that came after --- the Mountain, the drill, the children, the god --- I read across that one closed gap, the way a man reads the history of a country while standing in the middle of it.\par
