\typesetchapter{Chapter 4}{Mountain}

\begin{room}

--- The twenty-first of November; half past nine; the third of our interviews. The instruments reached you at last, I am told, and you did not waste them: the swimming, the diving, the hunt after your DNA. And yesterday, the heavens: \textit{Cosmos, a Personal Voyage}. I should be glad to hear of both, before we take up your account again.

--- The swimming first, Paul, since you were good enough to win me the instruments, and it was no small task; I will not pretend I took to it like your seals. But I went in, and I went under. In the end I did not need the bottle of air: a held breath and a little nerve carried me down far enough. And with the glasses pressed to my eyes, so the water could not blur them, I put my hand into the weed and closed it and opened it; and there they were, green threads and drifting motes and small living things too faint to name, caught in my own palm, the very things your \textit{Attenborough} had shown me only on his lit glass. I checked them against his pictures. They held. I am not disappointed. It was worth every ache of it, if only for that: I have held a piece of the living world in my own hand, and found the picture did not lie. And under the microscope, the very DNA the voice had named to me: that was there too, exactly where he had said it would be. But the heavens I cannot take in my hand, and that is where my trouble begins. He showed me the whole of it first, Paul, in one turning year: the making of the world, the first fire, the long dark, the wandering rocks; and my people arriving in the last breath of the last night of that year, a little before its midnight. Each second of his year, five hundred of mine. That I could hold. It was the one kindness in the whole day: he gave me a measure I could stand inside.

--- The Cosmic Calendar.

--- And then he would not let me rest in it. He showed me a man who measured the round earth with a stick and its shadow and the noon sun, and asked no god's leave to do it. Eratosthenes. He would have been Light, Paul. He did some of Light's work, in the open, with no office to grant him the shadow. I was asked whether I should like to repeat the man's experiment, and I found that I did not. I knew it to be true. That frightened me more than any doubt: that I could look at a stick and a shadow and simply think: that stands.

--- When last we sat, I asked whether your people knew the earth for a sphere; and you told me you did, and did not, that it was complicated. Was the stick your answer?

--- Part of it. The rest keeps for its place. And it was beautiful and cruel in the same breath, delightful and terrifying, the way your kind is both at once. A man named Kepler gave his life to prove that five perfect shapes ruled the spacing of the worlds. He said a thing I wrote down to keep:

\begin{scripture}
Geometry existed before the creation. \\
It is co-eternal with the mind of God. \\
Geometry provided God with a model for the creation. \\
Geometry is God himself.
\end{scripture}

But he was wrong about the shapes; he was himself the one who proved it wrong; and he went on loving the splendour of the thing to his death regardless. I should like to study these five perfect shapes of \textit{God Himself}. I do not know how a man abandons an idea so beautiful, and so pure; in truth, I do not believe that he wholly did. And that is a part of my unease: some things in that room I trusted at once, and some I could not. For there is this also. In that moving light, sometimes the thing was real, and sometimes it was men dressed and lit to play at what had once happened; and sometimes I could not tell the one from the other. I had a hundred questions yesterday. I have a thousand this morning. And I have understood that I cannot walk out and settle each of them with my own eyes, the way I settled your pond. A man could spend three lives on the questions of one afternoon. So I must do the thing I was never taught to do: decide which of them to trust without checking. And I do not know how. Where I was raised, I never had to choose. Scripture chose; the office chose. A warden told me, \textit{you agree, understanding is not required}; and I agreed, and the not-understanding was a kind of rest. Out here there is no scripture to lean on, and you name that freedom, and you say the word as though it were a gift. But if I am free, then I must choose whom to believe with nothing standing behind the choice but myself. How do you bear it, Paul? If not God, then what? How does a free man decide where to put his trust?

--- Carefully, and never all at once. I trust nothing because it comforts me, nor because a man in a robe has said it, nor because I should like it to be so. I trust what has been made to stand against a test and did not fall: a claim that could be checked, and was, and could have failed and did not. That earns a little of my weight, and only the weight the test will bear, and I take it back the hour a better test throws it down. It is a slow way to live, and a lonely one, and I have found nothing that beats it.

--- Then you and I trust the same thing, and I did not know it until this morning. \textit{What survives the test}; remember that you said it. I may hold you to it.

--- You are meant to be handing me a mountain, not a philosophy of doubt. When last we sat, two days gone, you had gone out to the Oasis, and there we left the day. But the beam was already yours by then: Va-Raedin had set you one thread of light of two hundred and fifty-six, running toward the south-west, and had not let you learn it before they carried you outside. Did you come back to it, after the visit? Give it to me as it came.

\end{room}

Light did not let a boy keep a revelation overnight and call it his own. Va-Raedin had given me the beam the morning after the Mirror, before Second Silence had well filled the practice halls: one point off the wall, mine to learn. But the outside claimed me before I could walk it; the whole long ceremony of the Oasis and the road back stood between me and the beam. And so it had waited on me, and now, the outside behind me, I came back to walk it.\par

Not the wall. One point on it, low and toward the south-western side, steadier than most and given to a slow stir near Second Silence. ``That one is yours until you can walk it in the dark,'' he had said. ``Source to fall, fall to source; every mirror it touches, every throat it passes, and whatever stands near its road that ought not to stand there. When you can draw me the whole of it with your eyes shut, you will be given a second. Do not hurry. Hurry is how a candidate loses a beam and lights a wrong room looking for it.''\par

I was not sent alone. Two servants of Light were set to walk the beam with me --- not to guard it, for Light did not believe a beam wanted guarding, and not to teach me, for I had been one of them until two days before, and there was nothing of theirs left to teach. From the age of twelve I had carried the oil and the cloths down these same corridors; I had cleaned the very glass I was now sent to read, and learned every habit of the work from the wrong end of the order. Ascension had not given me the hands --- I had those already --- but the doctrine, and the robe, and the right to say the word instead of only hearing it. So the two who walked at my shoulder walked there as servants walk beside an office: to carry what I need not stoop to carry, and to be told. Oren was twenty and Sela fourteen; they were servants, and would be servants still when their hands failed them; and I, newly fifteen, stood among the first thirty souls of a Mountain of some three thousand, and all three of us knew it without a word said.\par

I could have worn it the way it had always been worn over me. Light speaks downward in riddles: the Veil hands you the order and keeps the reason, and lets you feel the keeping. I had hated it the whole of my life. So I did not do it. I told Oren and Sela plainly what I wanted of them, and corrected them plainly where they wanted correcting, and asked no more of them than the work required. It was the first thing I had ever been left free to choose; it cost me nothing; and I have wondered since whether they even marked it.\par

Oren was tall, and stooped for it: the corridors of Light were not everywhere built for a tall man, and twenty years of learning where to duck had not cured him of forgetting. He carried the flat patience of a man who has cleaned the same glass long enough to stop resenting it. He knew where every lamp in the quarter sat, and not one reason it had been set there. The younger, Sela, bore the cloths and the little oil and said almost nothing. She watched me the way the young watch a thing they are working to understand --- a boy her own size, in the grey of an office, giving in a level voice the sort of order he had lately been taking himself --- and for the most part she kept it off her face.\par

The Mountain had not yet finished paying for my Ascension. A ceremony spends order and does not replace it; for days afterward the whole body of the place labours to earn it back. Those who had stood too long in the Hall now had to carry, cook, count, wash, teach, bless, guard and record, out of turn and short of hands. Faith had new instruction to send out under Ka-Elun. Justice had the transfer to reconcile against old tablets. And somewhere, because Light had taken a servant off some other task to walk a beam with a new Candidate, Balance was adjusting a ledger. Balance always adjusted the ledger. That word is a soft thing where I sit now, a near-compliment; in the Mountain \textit{noticing} was an office, and it wore a green robe, and it did not forgive.\par

We began at the fall, in the low room where my point died, before the ordinary hours let anyone near it. The doctrine was Va-Raedin's, set in me two days before and gone over in the dark until I held it whole: you do not read a beam from where it dies, for any fool can stand where a beam ends; you walk it back up to where it was born, keeping hold of it at every turn so that it stays your own beam under your hand and not its neighbour wearing its coat, and marking, as you go, whatever stands along its road that has no business standing there. That last was the whole of the craft. The rest was patience. A beam is the most honest thing in the Mountain, and it will show you every liar it passes, so long as you keep hold of it and are not flattered away by a brighter one. Sela had the bronze test-glass out of her satchel before I asked, which told me she had walked a Candidate before and knew the word that came next. Oren went to steady the shard against the wall with his thumb flat across its face. ``Not the face,'' I said. ``The cloth. Your thumb leaves an oil that reads as light within the hour, and the next man swears to a thing that is not there.'' He took his thumb off it without a word. He had known it once, I think, and had been let to stop minding it, as a servant is let to stop minding a thing; and I was not, any more. That, I was beginning to see, was the whole of the office: to go on minding the small thing long after everyone below you has earned the right to be tired of it.\par

I had thought the Mirror was the lesson. Following my one thread of light out of that room, I understood that the Mirror had only been the promise of it. The lesson was this: a beam is patient and a man is not, and every place they disagree, the man is wrong.\par

We did not get far before Shield stopped us. A warden raised one hand at the first turn and held back three workers, two children, and the three of us together. ``South service way is closed,'' he said. A woman with folded cloth on her hip frowned. ``Since when?'' ``Since it was closed.'' ``I have cloth due in the herb rooms.'' ``Then it will arrive by another passage.'' ``That is half an Hour added, and my back already full.'' The warden looked at the bundles, not the woman. ``Then the cloth will be tired.''\par

The children laughed until their father quieted them. The woman did not laugh; she shifted the cloth and turned to go the long way, irritated and unsurprised. No one asked why the way was shut. In the Mountain a closure did not owe anyone a reason. It owed everyone an adjustment. \par

But I was Light, and Light looks. In the moment the warden turned to wave the cloth-woman down the long way, the shut door stood a hand's breadth open behind him, and I saw down the closed passage the thing the shrug was built to keep ordinary. Two Shield wardens. Between them a Faith attendant, carrying a small covered dish, bone-pinned at the rim, and folded over her other arm a grey shawl, an old woman's. I knew the dish. I had watched it sealed the day before, out at the markers, over a single pale strand lifted from the dust beside a stone that had been turned to face the wrong way. Yesterday had not ended when the Oasis went quiet. It had been carried inside, and filed, and it was still walking the Mountain this morning under Shield's hand. The old woman who had gone out to the south-west markers, and the light I had seen thrown from the ridge, and the turned stone --- three nothings, I had been told, that together make a shape --- were somewhere behind that door.\par

And as the warden stepped back, a woman in Balance green passed straight through the closed way with a sealed slate under her arm and no one to stop her. I watched her go. Permission, I understood then, was not a wall. It was a sorting mechanism: the same door that turned back cloth, and hid an old woman, let green through without a word. I was proud of the thought. \par

The beam did not keep to Light; few of them did, which was half the reason a Candidate was ever made to walk one. Mine climbed a service throat, crossed above a washing gallery through a slot no wider than a wrist, and came down again into the trafficked stone where Balance kept the water. The first queue had already formed along a shallow channel, watched by two clerks in green and one Faith attendant. Each household bore a marked vessel; a copper rod lay across the channel, and the clerks walked it from notch to notch, reading allowance by household mark, by work, by illness, by the unborn, by yesterday's draw.\par

``Half measure,'' a clerk said, to a woman who had already opened her mouth. ``Yesterday was half.'' ``Yesterday you drew at the Measure; this is First Light, and no house draws twice before the Measure. You came early, after the heat of the Ascension, and early is half.'' The woman's jaw set, then let go, and she took the half-filled jar. I watched the water settle in it. The surface caught a high lamp and bent along the curve of the clay, so the jar showed fuller from one side than the other. I knew it for half measure only because Balance had said half measure. Without the mark, the eye would have argued. \par

Beyond the water the kitchens opened in a run of low, heated chambers, and the air thickened with steam and yeast and boiled grain and the sour-sweet breath of things left to ferment. The workers were mostly Balance, but Faith was in all their timing --- short prayers to seal a jar, longer ones to open stored grain, work-chants to keep hands moving together when the heat made speech unpleasant.\par

\begin{scripture}
Let every fruit be gathered. Let every vessel be sealed. Let no waste enter the Oasis.
\end{scripture}

They did not sing it beautifully. Some did not sing it at all. One woman sang with plain devotion; another used the lines to count her kneading; a boy mouthed them only when an elder's eye came round. Faith, I saw, did not require the same belief from every throat. Here, it required the rhythm to hold. \par

I slowed to watch them, longer than the beam allowed. A kitchen supervisor caught me at it. ``You want to knead, Light? We can always find a back.'' A few workers laughed. ``No,'' I said. ``Then keep moving. Even Light casts shadows.'' I moved.\par

It was in the wash passage below the kitchens that Sela showed me a thing, though she never knew she had, and it was not the beam. The beam had gone into a tangle of brackets and I had lost the thread of it. I did not ask her for help --- a Candidate does not ask a servant-child to find his own light for him. And as I drew it clean and it stood up plain under my hand, I caught her watching: not the beam, but me. When she saw that I had seen her, the colour came up all at once, quick and helpless, a glint in her eye and her face opening before she could stop it; and she bent to the cloths in her hands as though they had that instant come to need her whole attention. It was nothing. It was a girl of fourteen caught looking at a boy who had just done a deft thing with the light. But it was a whole and open and undefended thing, there and gone before she could fold it away, and I had never in my life been looked at so.\par

I have no gentler way to set it down. I had known a girl my whole life. When we were small she had pressed a chip of blue glass into my hand behind the cistern and sworn it was a jewel off the Outside, and made me promise we would run one day and find the place such things came from; I kept the glass four years, and she had forgotten it inside a season. That was the shape of the two of us, and I see it plainly now: I hoarded what she spent. And in all our years she had never once looked at me the way this stranger of fourteen just had, for half a breath, before she hid it in the cloths. I did not understand, that morning, why so small a thing should lie in my chest like a stone. \par

\ornament

Near the tool wash a man was waiting where no water ran, and he was waiting for me. Sa-Tavan of Justice, named to his office the same morning I was named to mine, a slate under one arm and the new black cord bright at his wrist, and worked small and exact in dark thread on the breast of his robe the sign of his office: a rectangle laid on its side, and within it a square hung from one corner, and a circle pressed into that square where the two crossed, the whole of it held inside the rectangle's line. He stood the way a man stands who has been practising it. ``Sa-Kailan,'' he said, and got the prefix exactly right, which almost no one had troubled to do in two days. ``I was told you would come through here, at this Hour. I have been waiting.'' He did not say by whom. He did not need to: only one man set my road, and it was the same man who would have set his.\par

``Then you have wasted an Hour,'' I said, which was the sort of thing I had heard Candidates say to servants my whole life and had never once been let to say myself. ``I have not,'' he answered, and did not rise to it. ``Justice may wait and still be working. Shield has given me the counts and the containment; what the tablet wants now is the order --- the sequence, in the eyes of one who stood in it --- and you were on the third marker when it began. Tell me what you saw. And tell me the order you saw it in.''\par

I had been on the third marker. I had seen the light before any of them, and I knew, better than his counts could tell him, exactly which thing had come before which. And I did not want to lay my seeing on his tablet raw, where he might turn it any way he chose. So I gave it to him already folded inside Shield's own authority, where he could not open it without opening Shield. ``The Threshold is Shield's ground,'' I said. ``Shield is charged to stand before it and let nothing enter it unnamed, and Va-Sheva of Shield stood there yesterday and did just that: she named what came, and held it. A brightness off the ridge, first, that Shield did not take for a signal. A marker found turned, second, by the wind or a careless hand. And an old woman missed from the prayer only after that, gone out to the south-west markers for a sprig. In that order, and with clear ground between the light and the woman. She never stood where the brightness was.''\par

It was true, every word of it. It was also arranged. I had set the light first and the woman last, and a stretch of empty ground between them, so that no sequence he could draw would bend the one into a cause of the other. That much I could do for her from where I stood, and it cost me nothing but the truth's own order, which I have never held sacred.\par

``A mirage,'' he said, and gave the brightness the word the record keeps for lights that come to nothing. I let him have it. I had seen it, and it had not been nothing; but a mirage asks no further questions, and I wanted none asked --- not of the woman, and not of me.\par

``And the woman?'' I asked, for I needed to know what my arranging had bought her. He wrote before he answered; Justice writes first. ``If the light was Shield's to name, and she stood apart from it, then she is no breach,'' he said. ``She is a strayed old woman, and strayed is an old and gentle category --- and here is the whole of what Justice does with her, which is the whole of what it does with any of us. I carry her case to the tablets, and I find every straying they hold, for the old have always wandered and the record is long; and against each I read what followed it: which came to nothing, which to a theft, which to blood. Then I lay the conditions of her day beside the conditions of theirs --- her years, the Hour, the heat still on the Mountain from an Ascension, the marker turned, the light Shield would not name --- and I set aside every case whose conditions do not answer to hers, and keep the one that answers nearest and truest. What that case became, hers is the likeliest to become; and it is upon the likeliest that I judge, and never upon my liking. Only then do I choose: the gentlest ruling the likeliest will bear without loosing the Mountain's hold on her. Where the record has room --- and for this it has room --- we are merciful; there is more mercy in us than the Halls ever let you see. It is only where the two cannot both be kept that the Mountain comes first.'' He said it like a creed he had just been trusted with and meant to be worthy of. ``She will be marked, watched a season, and let be.'' Then he capped his ink and looked at me as long as I had been looking at him. ``You gave me a very clean order, Sa-Kailan. Cleaner than a boy in his first fright on a marker has much right to give. I have written it as you gave it.'' He did not say it as an accusation, nor quite as a thing let go. His eye dropped to my bound thumb. ``Have that witnessed, so no one later confuses a ritual wound with an instruction wound.'' And he was gone before I could decide what he had seen.\par

I passed the child halls, cut into warmer stone near the family districts. Children sat on woven mats while a Faith teacher moved among them with a cord of five knots.\par

\begin{scripture}
The Mountain is refuge. \\
The Oasis is water. \\
The forest was plenty. \\
Beyond the forest thou shalt not wander.
\end{scripture}

The teacher touched the fifth knot. ``And if the forest is gone?'' The youngest faltered; the older answered.\par

\begin{scripture}
The Covenant remains.
\end{scripture}

Further on, an older class stood for a harder verse, and the teacher took them through it a line at a time, in the flat voice you keep for a thing children must carry whole into a life that will one day test it against the world.\par

\begin{scripture}
And when the wrath had passed, the armies of the Outsiders were no more; but the forest was wounded, and the forest was broken, and the open waters were spoiled, and the land around the Mountain became desert.
\end{scripture}

The forest had not withered in that verse. It had been killed, in the space scripture gave it, which was a single day: the day of the wrath. That was the teaching, and I had it as they had it, long before I could weigh it. Not that the world dried slowly, as a thing left too long in the sun; that it was struck dry, once, by something that had a shape, and that the shape had a name we did not say in front of children.\par

\begin{room}

--- That second verse, the older ones had it. Say it back to me. The forest wounded, the waters spoiled, the land made desert. Your people set that in a child before he can add. And they teach him it happened in a day.

--- In a day. After the wrath. That is the scripture, and I was raised on it, and I have given it to you whole and unshaved.

--- A forest does not die in a day. Not by any weather I have read in the ground. Drought is a slow murder: ten years, a hundred, a thousand. What comes up between one dawn and one dark, and leaves a desert where a forest stood?

--- You have asked me this before, in nearly these words, and I told you then to be patient. There is a scripture for it, a book of its own, the Book of the Beast, and I will recite it to you entire when we reach the place it belongs to, and not one morning sooner. For now you have exactly what the children have: a wrath, a forest broken, a desert made. Do not fish for the beast, Paul. I am keeping it for you, on purpose, and it is worth the keeping.

\end{room}

The beam ran on toward the south-west, out of Light's own halls and down into the salt-house, where Balance takes the trace out of water without spending a drop of it. No one throws water on an open fire here; in the desert you do not let water leave as breath. They seal it instead in fired vessels over a low, steady heat, and lead the steam off along a cooled clay arm, and catch it again at the cold end, where it beads and runs back pure into a clean jar --- the same water given back, safe of every trace it went in carrying. What stays behind in the vessel, the pale ring of salt and whatever else the spring had brought down with it, is scraped and dried and weighed on scales finer than any I had seen, and set in a ledger against the weight from the day before, and the day before that, back further than any hand there could have told you why. A boy no older than Sela tended a row of the sealed vessels, watching the arms run clear, scraping and weighing the rings, calling each figure to a clerk who wrote it without looking up. He did it perfectly, and did not know what a single one of his numbers meant. It was Balance's version of the thing I had watched all morning: the hand is given the doing, and the reason kept a floor above the hand. The weight of the trace was a small number and a large meaning --- whether the spring was sweetening or turning, whether the Mountain would thirst in a year or in a hundred --- and the boy who read it most exactly was told least of what he read.\par

The beam ran at last to the south-western edge of everything I knew: a Balance door I had never seen opened, low, guarded, Justice's mark cut beside Balance's on the lintel, and my one thread of light running straight in under it. I was reaching for the doctrine --- a beam is Light's to follow --- when a voice took it out of my mouth. ``Sa-Kailan.'' Ka-Leth, Keeper of Balance, broad and grave, her hands stained faintly green. She had been waiting too; the whole Mountain seemed to have had my road before I walked it. ``It runs to my door,'' she said, ``and you would like to know where it goes. You may not come in. But you are Light, and a beam is yours to account for, so I will give you this much, from where you stand.''\par

She had them crack the door a hand's width, no more. ``Look. Is any beam but yours entering this room, from any side of it?'' I looked a long time, and for once the doctrine was easy, because I knew my own beam and I knew there was no window there, no second shaft, no lamp within that could feed a thread to that wall. ``None,'' I said. ``Only mine.'' ``Good. Now go round by the outer stair to the far side, where it comes in from the source.''\par

They walked me round to the far side, the side that lay toward the source. And there was the proof of it, had I wanted proof. On that wall my beam came into the room a whole and full thing --- twice the light I had carried all the morning --- and on the near side, the side I had followed through the better part of the Mountain, it went on its way carrying half of that and no more. No second beam fed the room; I had stood at the near door and made sure of it with my own eyes. So the light that entered whole and left halved had not been shared with another thread, nor spilled, nor thrown to any lamp or throat I could name. Half of it had simply stayed inside, in the dark, behind Balance's door and under Justice's mark, and the whole Mountain below was lit by the half that was let go, and called it full. \textit{We divide the sun. We lose none of it.} I had said it that very morning. And here was light lost --- no, not lost: kept back, taken, which is a far worse word. Ka-Leth watched me reach it. ``You may follow it from here, where it leaves,'' she said.\par

I followed the enriched beam to its source, dismissed my two servants, and went on towards the candidate quarters. It was almost Fourth Record.\par

\begin{scripture}
And God said, Keep thou the Mountain, and keep thou the Oasis, and keep thou the Temple.
Keep the secrets apart, and let no one hand hold the whole.
\end{scripture}

I stopped just past the alcove. I had spoken those words myself, in the Hall, two days before. Now they followed me differently. \textit{The Temple.} I had said it a thousand times and never once asked where it stood. And \textit{keep the secrets apart} --- not as poetry now, but as design, as law, as threat.\par

I came back at last to the candidate quarters, half empty, and sat on the edge of my mat, and for the first time since it was hung at my belt I had the quiet to truly look at the Light token. It was old --- that was the first thing the quiet gave me: not old as I was old, nor as Oren was, but old as the corridors were old, older, I thought, than the office it marked, worn to a soft shine by a long file of hands before mine, each given it in his turn and each handing it on to a stranger. And it was not bronze, nor any metal our shops kept: pale as a winter moon and colder than silver, yet heavier in the hand than a coin of gold the same size, and it had taken not a breath of tarnish in all its uncountable years, and no file we owned, I knew without trying, could have marked it. Someone had \textit{cut} it --- there is no humbler word for what had been done to it --- with a care and a trueness that had no business belonging to a thing so old: at the centre a small smooth disc, pierced clean through by a hole no wider than a hair; and around the disc, ring upon fine ring, close and even and unwavering, spreading to the very rim like the rings a dropped stone leaves on a still pool, and held there, frozen, in that cold pale metal, and never moving. I set the pierced centre to my eye and looked through it and saw only my own dim wall, and no reason on earth why the hole should be there, nor why a maker so patient had ringed it round so exactly for a use I could not find. I did not understand the token, and I want to be honest that I did not; I only felt, turning it, that I held a question older than any answer my people had, and made by a hand steadier than my people owned.\par

I held it to the lamp then, and as always it did not show me more; it showed me less, with greater force. The law had held everywhere I walked that day: each office kept one portion, and the whole existed only where the portions were forced to meet. If no single hand held the whole, then every soul in the Mountain lived inside arrangements it could not see; and the thing I had spent the day learning to see had a record open on me too, and had been keeping it, patiently, the whole time I imagined myself the one doing the looking. \par

I was still turning it over when it slipped my fingers and dropped into the cup of water at my knee, and there it did the one thing that whole day I had not asked of it: it lied to me. It lay a clear finger's width to the side of where my hand had put it, and nearer the surface than the bottom it rested on, and larger than itself. I moved my head and it swam. I knew that this was not the token moving but the water bending what came off it to my eye --- the same trick, I would later have a word for, that had made a stranger's jar of half-water show full from the one side. It was a true thing, and a large one, and I had stumbled on it by accident, groping after the token's secret; and the joke of it, which I had not the years yet to laugh at, was that the token had nothing whatever to do with it. I had asked the metal what it was for, and the water had answered a wholly different question, and answered it true.\par

And that night, eyes shut, I drew the whole of the beam's road as I had been taught to draw it --- every turn and throat and surface of it --- and set the drinking-room upon it in its place: one dark bead strung on a line of light. That a room could take light, that Balance could keep the taking, that my whole office could go on reciting \textit{we lose none of it} and believe it entire --- that was the first thing I ever knew for certain my own people had wrong. And Light, which held a word for every part of every thing, held none for this. So I went round to the far side of the law and borrowed one, a smuggler's word for a smuggled good, and it has served me every year since. Contraband light: light off the ledger, taxed at no gate, fallen in no hall, kept back in the dark from a house that believed with its whole heart that nothing given it was ever lost.\par

--- You have set that sentence down carefully, many times now: keep the secrets apart, let no one hand hold the whole. Why do you keep it?

--- Because it is well made. Your people built a good sentence, and I collect good sentences. A thing that cannot be surrendered whole, because no one of you holds it whole: the traitor, the captive, the fool with the loose tongue each carry only a fragment, and the thing outlives the weakest man who keeps it. It is sound engineering in the dress of scripture, and sound engineering is true whether or not a syllable of the account around it is.

--- You say that as a man who would very much like to hold the whole himself.

--- Every man in my trade would. That is exactly why your people were wise to see to it that none of us can.

--- Enough of the Mountain for one morning. I want to give you a thing, and to ask you a thing, and I would rather do neither in this chair. For three days you have tried to get me out of doors for my health, and I have refused you like a sulking child, the pond excepted. Take me out now. I should like to stand under the open sky and show you that I can, I who could not lift my eyes to it at fifteen. Count it your victory. Tell the physician he may have it.

--- Gladly. It is a better use of a cold late morning than a beam you have walked me all the way to its source. Come. There is a lawn on the south side, past the glass doors, that no one crosses in this weather.

\centerline{[\,---\,]}

--- There. You see? I am standing under the whole enormous nothing of it, and I have not fallen down. Now walk with me. Away from the doors, to the middle, where the grass is longest. Good.

--- You did not bring me out for the sky.

--- No. I brought you out for the one thing this ground has that your room does not: no walls. A wall can be given an ear; grass cannot. Out here there is nothing that hears me but you, and that machine on your shoulder, and I have decided that I trust the machine, because it is yours, and because it does not lie about what it has heard. So. Will you trust me for a moment, and play a small game with me?

--- That depends upon the game.

--- It is a scientist's game. One you should enjoy. You told me this morning that you trust what survives a test. I have a test. This afternoon, and the whole of tomorrow, when you go to your work: you told me you spend your afternoons alone in your room, turning the reel. Do not, this once. Go instead and stand behind the glass of the room where you set me down each day. And look. And when we next meet, tell me whether anyone was there.

--- Behind the glass.

--- You told me no one watched me; that only the tutor sat with me. I believe that you believe it. But a man kept apart, as you are kept apart, does not know what stands on the far side of his own wall. So go and see. If the place is empty, you will have proved me a frightened fool, and I will play your football, and never trouble you with it again. And if it is not empty, then I was right; and a man ought to stand by the one who was right. And there is more, for I have not finished frightening you. I have come to suspect there is a Mirror in this house. Not a person set at a keyhole --- I should have caught a person by now --- but a thing of glass and angle that watches without a watcher, the way Light watches in the Mountain. Three years I stood candidate under the finest eyes my world ever made, and I know the signs of a watcher better than I know my own name: men who hold things they were never told, who answer the question you did not ask aloud. I have felt that in this house. I recognise the shape of it. And you know by now what I say of a thing that has a shape: that it may be found. For whom, I cannot tell you. Maybe for you. Maybe for me. Maybe for Sarah. Maybe for some purpose none of the three of us would guess. And understand me clearly: I do not say it against Sarah. I have never met her. You say she is good, and busy, and set high above us both, and I believe you; I ask it for her. Are we even certain it is Sarah's own people watching Sarah's house, and not some other eye set upon Sarah herself? A woman who heads what she heads --- would there truly be no one in all the world with cause to watch her? And this above all the rest: if you do find it --- the watcher, or the beam, or the Mirror --- be clever, Paul. Say nothing. Let nothing cross your face. It is a rare and precious thing, to know you are watched and have the watcher not know you know it, and I would not have you spend it on the first cold afternoon.

--- And that is the whole of it? A Mirror in the walls, an unnamed eye upon Sarah, and me to go feeling for all of it in the dark and keep my face straight while I do. She runs a great deal, I grant you --- ships, metals, men who matter; it is not impossible she is watched. But that is a very great deal of shadow to hang upon a room you have sat in just a few times.

--- That is the whole of it. And if I am proven right, if there are men behind that glass, or a beam, or something else whose shape I do not yet know, then you are mine, Paul. Not the house's. Mine. Your loyalty comes to me.

--- That is a large thing to swear on a lawn.

--- It is the smallest thing in the world, if I am wrong; and I am so often wrong. It costs you nothing but an afternoon and a promise you are certain can never fall due. Go behind the glass, Paul. For her, if not for me. And swear me the small thing: that survives its test, or does not.

--- Very well. If there are men behind that glass, which there will not be, then I am your man. There: it costs me nothing to promise what cannot come to pass. Now put your face straight before we go in. You look like a boy who has got away with something.


--- A boy who has got away with something is the safest man in any house, Paul; no one troubles to watch the pleased ones. Come in out of the cold. You are kept in your cell as I am kept in mine --- only yours opens from the inside, and this afternoon I am asking you to do no more than walk through it. I have a physician to satisfy.

--- So. The water.

--- I told you this morning what it cost me, and I have decided I want more of it regardless. Tell the physician he has not beaten me but recruited me: I will go back, and go under further than I went, and work at last towards swimming without the armbands. Two days, then. Go and do your looking, Paul. And whatever you find behind that glass --- keep your face.
