\typesetchapter{Chapter 3}{Mountain}

\noindent \textit{A question spends before it answers.}\par
\noindent \textit{Curiosity is not free.}\par
\noindent \textit{--- Ka-Leth, Keeper of Balance}\par

\ornament

\noindent You asked me last time, Paul, what a boy does with a secret nobody knows he is carrying. I will tell you. He walks around all day pretending to run errands, and he looks at everything twice.\par

\ornament

\noindent The basin beside my mat had grown a skin of dust on its water by the time grey light reached the chamber, and I had been awake to watch it settle. My body wanted sleep; my mind would not close. It kept going back to the token lying at the bottom of the ceremony basin, to the sign of Light shifting under the water where it had no business shifting. A child can drop a spoon into a cup and watch it bend at the waterline. A worker can rinse his hands and see his own fingers turn pale and broken beneath the surface. Every person in the Mountain had seen water make the familiar untrustworthy.\par

But I had not merely seen it; I had noticed it, and noticing does not let you go. Light entered water and came out changed. It crossed a threshold and lost its obedience. I did not understand it yet --- understanding was Va-Raedin's country, and he charged a boy's pride for entry --- but the sight had lodged in me and would not be paid off with sleep.\par

By morning the basin beside my mat was no longer only a basin. The lamp above it was no longer only a lamp. The faint wavering on the wall, where someone had spilled water and wiped badly, was no longer an accident. Everything seemed to be doing something behind the name by which I had known it. I have never entirely lost that morning. It is a disease, if you like, and I caught it at fifteen, and I have it still, here, watching your snow.\par

I dressed before the household fully woke, tied my robe with more care than usual, and checked the bandage on my thumb. The cut had closed badly in the night. My mother had left dried fruit and a little clay cup of goat's milk in the wall recess, and the milk had grown a skin. I moved the cup under the lamp and watched the yellow surface tremble where my fingers touched the rim. Then hunger remembered itself, and I drank.\par

I was expected east.\par

That should have made the morning simple. Light had named me, Light had cut me, Light had given me a token, and Light now owned my hours. But the day after an Ascension never moved simply. The Mountain had spent order on ceremony and now had to earn it back through work. Those who had stood too long in the Hall still had to carry, cook, count, repair, wash, teach, bless, guard, and record. The old had been kept warm through the rite and now needed broth and washing. Children who had slept through the closing prayer had woken late and irritable. Faith had new instructions to distribute under Ka-Elun, Keeper of Faith. Justice had the transfer to reconcile against old tablets. Light had a new Candidate and expected him before ordinary duties.\par

Which meant someone else had been moved from some other task. Which meant Balance would have to adjust a ledger. Balance always noticed. I want you to hold that word, Paul --- \textit{noticed}. In your world it is a soft thing, a compliment almost. In mine it was an office, and it had a green robe, and it did not forgive.\par

\ornament

\noindent I did not get far.\par

At the first turn a Shield warden raised one hand and stopped three workers, two children, and me together. ``South service way is closed,'' he said.\par

One of the workers, a woman carrying folded bundles of cloth, frowned. ``Since when?''\par

``Since it was closed.''\par

``I have cloth due in the herb rooms.''\par

``Then it will arrive by another passage.''\par

``That adds half a descent.''\par

The warden looked at the bundles, not at the woman. ``Then the cloth will be tired.''\par

The children laughed before their father quieted them. The woman did not. She shifted the bundles against her hip, irritated but not surprised. Nobody asked why the passage had closed. In the Mountain, closure did not require explanation from Shield. It required adjustment from everyone else. I have thought since that this is the truest description of the place I could give a stranger. Not the darkness. The way a shut door was answered with a shrug and a longer walk, and never with a question.\par

The warden looked at my robe and my bandaged thumb. Shield closures were never only about the passage closed. They were also about whatever might have crossed it if it stayed open.\par

``Light?''\par

``Candidate,'' I said.\par

His gaze sharpened by a fraction. ``Sa-Kailan?''\par

The new prefix still entered me strangely, like a coat cut for a taller man. ``Yes.''\par

``You are expected east. Use the lower wash route. Do not cross the copper gate. It is being witnessed.''\par

Being witnessed. Justice, then. I nodded and turned with the others. Shield did not explain closures. Justice did not explain witness unless sequence demanded it. Light --- my own office, I was already learning --- might have tried to find a way to see without asking. I glanced back once as the warden stepped aside for a woman in Balance green carrying a sealed slate. She passed through the closed way without a word.\par

Permission, I understood then, was not a wall. It was a sorting mechanism. I was proud of the thought. I did not yet know how much of my life it would cost me to finish it.\par

\ornament

\noindent The lower wash route took me through the waking parts of the Mountain, where the stone was less polished than in Light --- narrower, warmer, more honest in its labour. A boy dragged a basket of cracked jars toward the repair rooms. Two old women argued softly over a cloth filter. A man with flour on his wrists slept upright against a wall, waiting for a gate to release. Above us, water knocked faintly inside hidden pipes, not loud enough to disturb, not quiet enough to forget.\par

The first water queue had already formed along a shallow channel, watched by two Balance clerks and one Faith attendant. Each family had a marked vessel --- some clay, some metal, a few old ones carved from dark stone and repaired so often that the repairs had become part of their identity. A copper rod lay across the channel, and the clerks moved it from notch to notch, reading allocation by household mark, work assignment, infirmary allowance, pregnancy, heat exposure, and yesterday's draw. The Faith attendant blessed each jar before the measure was released. Her prayer did not slow the work. It timed it.\par

``God who hid the waters beneath the Mountain, teach us to count without cruelty,'' she said over one jar.\par

The Balance clerk did not look up. ``Half measure.''\par

The woman holding the jar stiffened. ``Yesterday was half measure.''\par

``Yesterday was second descent. Today is third. Your household drew early after the Ascension heat.''\par

``My father is ill.''\par

``Your father was ill yesterday. Illness is recorded. Extra warmth was granted. Water was advanced.''\par

The Faith attendant touched the woman's wrist before her anger could become public. ``The hidden waters remember the unborn also.''\par

The woman's mouth tightened. For a breath she seemed about to answer. Then she lowered her eyes and took the half-filled jar. I watched the water settle inside it. The surface caught a high lamp and bent along the curved clay, so that the jar looked fuller from one angle than another. I knew it was half measure only because Balance had named it half measure. Without the mark, the eye would have argued. Remember that, Paul, when I tell you later what I found under the Temple. I had already learned, at fifteen, over a stranger's water jar, that the eye is a liar the moment a record is not standing beside it.\par

``Candidate of Light.'' It was not a greeting. ``You are blocking the return line.''\par

I moved at once, embarrassed. The clerk's face stayed neutral, which made the correction worse. Balance had no need to scold where inconvenience could be named precisely.\par

\ornament

\noindent Beyond the water queue the kitchens opened in a series of low, heated chambers, and the air changed again. It grew thick with steam, yeast, boiled grain, goat's milk, dried herbs, smoke from carefully managed dung fuel, and the sour-sweet smell of fermentation. The workers were mostly Balance, though Faith was everywhere in their timing --- short prayers for sealing jars, longer ones for opening stored grain, work chants that kept hands moving together when the heat made speech unpleasant.\par

``Let every fruit be gathered. Let every vessel be sealed. Let no waste enter the Oasis.''\par

They did not sing beautifully. Some did not sing at all. But the words entered the labour without needing to be believed the same way by every mouth. One woman sang with devotion. Another used the lines to count her kneading turns. A boy mouthed them only when an elder looked his way. Faith, I realised, did not require equal sincerity from every voice. It required the rhythm to hold. That was a more useful thing to know than most of what I had been taught to believe, and it unsettled me that I could see it so plainly and still kneel that evening as though I had not.\par

Two Balance girls dusted trays with powdered shell --- calcium, I remembered from childhood lessons, belonging to no romance and no prayer and no myth, only to bones, teeth, poultice, mortar, and the quiet insistence of bodies. At the far end of the kitchens three goats were painted on a stone panel above a locked hatch, cheerful animals with square beards and solemn eyes. Beneath them a line had been carved in Balance script and copied below in common hand:\par

\begin{scripture}
THE OUTSIDE FEEDS ONLY WHAT THE MOUNTAIN CAN COUNT.
\end{scripture}

I slowed. \textit{Outside.} The word struck differently now. The Oasis. The shadow of forest in the old teachings. The permitted prayer periods. Balance workers who tended animals and plants beyond the stone, Shield wardens who watched paths no one else was meant to understand. We were taken to pray before the Oasis at appointed ages, but always in groups, always under instruction, always with the eyes of Shield behind us and the voice of Faith before us. The Oasis was water, food, memory, boundary, temptation. It was the world God had left us --- or the fragment of world the Mountain had not yet consumed.\par

A kitchen supervisor saw me staring. ``You want goat duty, Light?'' A few workers laughed.\par

``No,'' I said.\par

``Then keep moving. Even candidates cast shadows.''\par

I moved.\par

\ornament

\noindent The washing pools lay below the kitchens, not for dignity but for sequence. Water that had rinsed vegetables and cooled sealed jars was filtered, blessed, measured again, and used where it could be used without insulting health. Balance called it sequence. Faith called it gratitude. Children called it old water and were corrected until they stopped. Nothing in the Mountain ended after one use if it could be made to serve again --- a sentence I could have recited in my sleep, and did not truly hear until much later, in a place where I myself had become a thing they meant to use more than once.\par

Near the tool wash I stopped. A woman dipped a curved metal blade into a shallow pool, and it bent under the surface; she scrubbed it with sand, lifted it out, and it returned to itself. She had done it a thousand times and would never once look. Everyone walked past the same small lie all day --- workers, clerks, singers, wardens, even Light attendants perhaps --- and named it ordinary, and so it stayed hidden. I have wondered ever since how many walls in your world are exactly this: not locked, only named.\par

``Sa-Kailan?''\par

I turned too quickly. Sa-Tavan stood at the edge of the wash passage with a slate under one arm and the new black cord showing at his wrist. He looked as he had in the Hall --- narrow, composed, faintly disapproving, though perhaps that was only the face Justice gives its children before they earn a better one.\par

``You are far from the east passage,'' he said.\par

``So are you.''\par

``I have a permission mark.'' He lifted the slate. A small piece of pale wax had been pressed into the upper corner and stamped with the sign of Justice.\par

``Congratulations.''\par

``It is not an honour. It is a route allowance.''\par

``That was not what I meant.''\par

``It was what you said.''\par

I almost smiled despite myself. ``You were less exact yesterday.''\par

``I was being named yesterday.''\par

``Does Justice permit inexactness during ceremony?''\par

He considered this with enough seriousness that I regretted the joke. ``Ceremony permits formal compression. It does not permit falsehood.''\par

``Useful distinction.''\par

``Yes.''\par

The answer was so earnest it became impossible to mock. I looked at the slate. ``What are you recording?''\par

``Disputed sequence in the second water queue.''\par

``What dispute?''\par

``A household claims the mark was advanced after the jar was filled. Balance claims it was advanced before. Faith witnessed the blessing but not the measure. So Justice records the order while memory is still warm.''\par

I looked back toward the water lines. The woman with the half measure was gone; others had taken her place. ``And if you record it correctly?''\par

``Then the next measure can be corrected, if correction is required.''\par

``And if you record it wrongly?''\par

His face changed slightly. Not fear. Respect. ``Then a correction becomes a wound.''\par

It was an answer I would not have expected from him. I had thought Justice dry --- tablets and cords and the satisfaction of being right after everyone else had grown tired. But there was weight beneath the boy's seriousness. A measure misrecorded became hunger. A witness misplaced became punishment. A sequence altered became guilt where none belonged. I did not know, that morning, that I was speaking with the person who would one day sit in a chamber and record the sequence of me. If I had, I do not think I would have changed a word. He would not have let me.\par

I nodded. ``Then I should not delay you.''\par

``No.'' He turned to go, then paused. ``Your thumb is bleeding through.''\par

I looked down. A small red mark had come up on the cloth. ``It opened during training.''\par

``You should have it witnessed in the infirmary, if Light cut it ceremonially and training reopened it.''\par

``Why?''\par

``So responsibility is not confused.''\par

``Between my thumb and Light?''\par

``Between a ritual wound and an instruction wound.''\par

I stared at him, then laughed once. He did not laugh. ``If it scars badly, the record should know why,'' he said, and left before I could answer.\par

For a few moments I stood among the steam and the washing noise, irritated and impressed in equal measure. I had been given a title and thought it made me more visible. He had been given a cord and already saw the world as a chain of possible misplacements. It was absurd. It was also useful. Justice waited not because it loved delay, but because action without sequence could change the meaning of everything that followed. I decided not to ask anyone to witness my thumb. I have wondered since whether that was the first small crime of my life, or only the first I noticed.\par

\ornament

\noindent He has been giving me the water --- the marked jars, the copper rod moved from notch to notch, the same measure rinsed and blessed and counted down through the kitchens until nothing of it is let go --- and I let it run, because a man who tells you how carefully his people ration a thing is telling you how little of it they have.\par

Then he turns it on me. ``And out here,'' he says. ``Four days in this house, and I have not seen a jar carried, nor a queue, nor a clerk at a channel. How do you keep yourselves alive? Where is your water counted?''\par

It is the cheapest answer I own, so I give it whole. ``It is not counted. It runs in the walls --- in pipe, the way your Mountain runs it, only into every room, and warm as well as cold. A man turns a handle and takes as much as he likes, and no one writes it down.'' I take a photograph from the folder beside me and set it on the table by the reels: a washroom in white stone, a bright tap, a fall of water caught and held there, exact, not moving.\par

He does not reach for it. He looks at the thing itself before he looks at what it shows --- a moment of light kept flat on paper, water that fell years ago and is falling still. I watch him understand that the picture is a kind of record, and then understand what it records. ``Every room,'' he says. ``Warm. And no measure.''\par

``No measure.''\par

He sets it down with more care than he knows he is using. Something has gone out of the Mountain for him in this last minute, and he does not feel the loss; he believes he has only been shown a wonder. A people who never count their water look rich to a boy raised to bless the waste pot. What it costs us, out here, to run water we never learned to grieve --- that is not a truth this room is for.\par

``Go on,'' I say. ``You were walking east, the long way.''\par

\ornament

\noindent The child halls were higher, cut into warmer stone near the family districts and the teaching chambers of Faith. I passed them on my way toward the eastern lamp corridors --- again by the long route. I told myself I was observing. That made the delay sound like discipline rather than avoidance.\par

Children sat in three groups on woven mats while a Faith teacher moved between them with a cord of five knots. The youngest repeated after her in singsong voices, stumbling over the old forms.\par

\begin{scripture}
The Mountain is refuge. \\
The Oasis is water. \\
The forest was plenty. \\
Beyond the forest thou shalt not wander.
\end{scripture}

The teacher paused and touched the fifth knot. ``And if the forest is gone?'' The youngest faltered. The older ones answered for them.\par

\begin{scripture}
The Covenant remains.
\end{scripture}

I stopped near the doorway, out of habit more than intent. The day before, the words would have sounded complete. Now they sounded like the surface of something harder. The forest was gone. The Covenant remained. If a child did not know what had been lost, the line comforted. If an adult knew, it instructed grief to behave. I have heard a great deal of scripture since, in your churches and mine, and I have not found a cleaner definition of the thing than that. A line that tells grief how to behave.\par

On the far wall of the teaching chamber hung five small panels for the Kahir. Balance was a cup and a sealed jar. Faith, an open text. Shield, a gate. Light, an aperture. Justice, a black cord crossing a pale stone. And beyond the Oasis panel, almost lost in the carved desert margin, a single dark rock had been cut smaller than a fingernail. No child was asked to name it. They were too young to be afraid of such omissions. They pointed and whispered and tried to guess which office would one day claim them, as though choosing sweets from a festival tray.\par

One girl pointed to Light and said, ``They know all the secret doors.''\par

The teacher turned. ``No.'' The girl shrank. ``Light knows that doors are not the first secret,'' the teacher said.\par

I almost stayed to hear the rest. I moved on. I have regretted small departures like that one more than most of my sins. There is always a sentence you leave a room one breath before it finishes, and it turns out to have been the sentence.\par

\ornament

\noindent The lamp corridors ran along the eastern inner spine, and there Light became less ceremonial and more necessary. The Mountain could not live in darkness, but neither could it flood itself with flame. Oil had to be rationed, smoke drawn, mirrors cleaned without scratching. Lamps had to serve three purposes at once --- guide the workers, reveal danger, and conceal the routes not meant for every eye. Narrow inspection slots let Light attendants see whether distant lamps burned correctly without entering passages where they had no right to stand.\par

A senior candidate of Light stood on a ladder adjusting a small mirror while two apprentices held the base. The mirror was no larger than a plate, but it threw light fifty paces down a side corridor, struck a patch of pale stone near a stair, and broke into a softer glow that made the steps visible without lighting the sealed door beyond them.\par

``Too high,'' she said.\par

One apprentice touched the bracket.\par

``Not with your hand. With the screw.''\par

``The screw is stuck.''\par

``The screw is not stuck. Your patience is.''\par

I smiled despite myself. She saw me. ``Fellow Candidate. Since you are smiling, tell me what is wrong with this mirror.''\par

The apprentices turned with obvious relief at someone else being chosen. I studied the mirror, the lamp, the side corridor, the stair. The obvious answer was that the beam sat too high --- she had already said so, which meant that answer would embarrass me. So I walked to the side and crouched, and from below I saw the faint duplicate of the beam along the lower wall, where a film of dust at the mirror's bottom edge was scattering just enough light to touch the outline of the sealed door.\par

``It is not only too high,'' I said. ``It is dirty.''\par

She looked at me. ``Everything is dirty.''\par

``The dirt is revealing the door.''\par

``Better.'' Not Va-Raedin's voice, not Va-Raedin's approval, but the word still struck. She handed an apprentice a cloth. ``Clean the lower edge. Then lower it two turns, not three. Three will light the door frame. Two will light the stair.''\par

I moved on before she found another question. I liked the lamp corridors more than I wanted to. They showed Light in its least pious form. No chanting, no gravures, no blue-rooted ceremonial flames bending toward holy faces. Here Light was labour --- oil, soot, wick, screw, angle, dust, air. It was infrastructure disguised as grace, or grace made possible by infrastructure, and I could not decide which thought made me more uncomfortable. I can decide now. It was the second. A grace you can build, you can also withhold.\par

\ornament

\noindent At the repair spaces near the lower spine, men and women worked over cracked mirror plates, bent brackets, broken hinges, jar seals, cart wheels, pump handles, pipe sections. The repair halls belonged to no Kahir entirely. Balance inspected the materials, Light the reflective surfaces, Shield the locks and hinges. Justice witnessed anything whose failure had caused injury. Faith blessed new work when the repair touched a sacred route. The result was a constant low argument held in check by procedure.\par

``It will hold,'' a Balance man said, turning a pipe section in both hands.\par

A Light woman shook her head. ``It reflects into the side slit.''\par

``It is pipe. It is not meant to reflect.''\par

``It reflects whether you intend it or not.''\par

``Then dull it.''\par

``If I dull it, it corrodes faster.''\par

Justice, in the person of an elderly recorder seated with a slate on her lap, said without looking up, ``State the consequence of each choice.''\par

The Balance man sighed. ``If polished, it misdirects light.''\par

``If dulled, it weakens sooner,'' said the Light woman.\par

``Time to failure?''\par

``Unknown.''\par

``Then it is not yet a choice,'' the recorder said. ``It is two fears.''\par

I slowed. There was no force in her voice, and yet both workers stopped arguing. I have sat in rooms since where powerful men shouted for an hour and moved nothing, and I have thought of that old woman and her slate, naming a thing correctly until the noise had nowhere left to stand.\par

\ornament

\noindent The medical and herb rooms lay beyond the repair halls, under warmer vents where fungus trays and medicinal roots could be kept at their proper dampness. The air there was rich and green and bitter. Bundles of herbs hung from cords. Clay trays held moss, pale fungi, small red leaves grown under cloth-filtered lamps. Mortars thudded.\par

A child with a burn on his forearm sat very still while a healer worked paste into the skin. A Faith attendant murmured comfort in a rhythm that kept the boy breathing evenly. A Balance clerk noted the amount of paste, oil, cloth, and future dressing the child would require.\par

``Will it scar?'' the mother asked.\par

The healer did not answer at once. She wrapped the arm, then glanced toward the recorder. ``It will mark,'' she said.\par

The Faith attendant touched the child's hair. ``A mark is not always a wound.''\par

``He will need extra oil in the dressing for three days,'' the Balance clerk said.\par

The mother looked frightened by the word \textit{extra}.\par

``Recorded under injury allowance,'' the clerk added.\par

Relief went through the woman so visibly that I was embarrassed to have seen it. Mercy, here, was not a feeling. It was a category. If the burn was recorded correctly, the child received oil. If not, pity had to fight the storehouse, and pity did not always win. I want you to sit with that, Paul, because your century has decided that mercy is a warmth in the chest. Where I was raised, mercy was a column in a book, and a child's arm healed or scarred by whether a clerk wrote the right word in it. I am not certain your version has saved more children than ours. I am only certain it feels better to the one being merciful.\par

On the far side of the herb room, stacked wooden frames held fibre plants under careful light --- flax, nettle, and two others I knew by use but not by name. Cloth, cord, bandage, wick: each had a second life waiting for it, and a third. Balance did not simply count the Mountain's life. It arranged that life so nothing ended at its first purpose.\par

\ornament

\noindent By midday I had reached the storehouses. I had not meant to. Light candidates had no business lingering there without assignment, but the lower route had become a private map of the Mountain's hidden relations, and the storehouses were where relation became visible.\par

They lay behind three gates. The first stood open and busy. The second required a mark. The third was watched by Shield and witnessed by Justice. I stayed in the outer hall, where permitted goods were received, sorted, inspected, and redirected. The air was dry and cooler than the kitchens --- grain, wax, old wood, salt, dried fruit, shell powder, leather, and the faint sharpness of preserved herbs. Rows of jars stood against the walls, each marked by date, household source, use category, and restriction level. The Balance workers moved among them without hurry. Haste wasted attention. Attention preserved life.\par

At a central table a woman in Balance green inspected a tray of dried figs. She cut one open, frowned, set it aside. Another. Another. The worker who had brought the tray shifted nervously. ``Mould?'' he asked.\par

``Beginning.''\par

``How much lost?''\par

``That depends on whether you argue.''\par

He closed his mouth. She separated the figs into three piles --- clean, questionable, spoiled. The spoiled went into a sealed waste pot. The questionable were marked for boiling. The clean were reweighed. And a Faith attendant beside her spoke one line over the clean figs and a different line over the waste pot. I had never noticed that before. Even waste received language. Not the same blessing, but acknowledgment. Nothing left the order of the Mountain without being named.\par

``Sa-Kailan.''\par

This voice was older, female, and carried authority enough that I straightened before I had turned. Ka-Leth, Keeper of Balance, stood at the entrance to the second gate. I had seen her in ceremony --- broad and grave, pouring water with hands stained faintly green. Up close she was less ceremonial and more dangerous. She looked at my robe, my bandage, my face, and the distance between my feet and the restricted line painted across the floor.\par

``Are you assigned here?''\par

``No, Keeper.''\par

``Are you lost?''\par

``No, Keeper.''\par

``Then you are either idle or observing.''\par

``Observing.''\par

``Light's favourite excuse.''\par

I lowered my eyes. ``I can leave.''\par

``You can. But since you have observed, tell me what you saw.''\par

I looked at the figs, the jars, the marks, the waste pot, the gates, the clerk's blade, the Faith attendant's mouth, the line on the floor I had nearly crossed. I did not want to give a childish answer. I also did not want to stand silent before Ka-Leth until silence became confession. ``Nothing is only what it first appears to be,'' I said.\par

Her face did not change. ``That is a Light answer. Try again.''\par

I swallowed. ``The spoiled figs are not simply thrown away. They are sealed. The questionable ones are not saved as they are, but changed into something safer. The clean ones are weighed again because loss changes the measure. The prayer changes with the use. Waste is still counted.''\par

``Better. Why?''\par

``Because waste that is not counted becomes invisible.''\par

``And invisible waste becomes?''\par

I thought of the water queue, the injured child, the pipe in the repair hall, the half-filled jar that looked fuller from the wrong angle. ``Theft,'' I said. ``From someone who does not know he has been robbed.''\par

Ka-Leth considered me. ``Va-Raedin is not wasting you entirely.'' The praise was so severe it almost passed for insult. ``Thank you, Keeper.''\par

``Do not thank me. You are too near the second gate. Step back.'' I stepped back. She turned away, then paused. ``If Light wishes to understand the Mountain, it should remember that revelation consumes oil. Curiosity is not free.'' She went through the gate, and Shield closed it behind her.\par

I stood there longer than was wise, the sentence settling. \textit{Curiosity is not free.} From Va-Raedin it would have meant that pride costs humiliation. From Balance it meant something colder. Time, oil, warmth, water, attention, risk. Every question required a lamp, a corridor, a witness, a pause in someone else's work. Even discovery drew from the storehouse. I paid that bill in full, Paul, in the end. She was warning me, and I heard only that I had been praised.\par

\ornament

\noindent He has been giving me Shield --- the service way closed without reason, the copper gate witnessed, the wardens who watch not the passage but whatever might cross it --- a whole people arranged around the fear of the wrong thing getting in. Then he asks the question I have waited four days for him to ask, and he asks it as though it were a courtesy.\par

``And you,'' he says. ``Out here in the open, with water in your walls and light that needs no tending. How many are you? Who holds your gates? A people this rich is either very many, or very well guarded, or has enemies it has not told me of.''\par

Here the truth is the dangerous answer, so I do not use it. ``We are few,'' I say. ``Quiet. Scattered thin across a great deal of country, and no one troubles us, because there is nothing in us worth the trouble. No gates. No wardens. You have slept four nights in an unlocked house.'' Every word of it is a lie, and I lay them down flat and even, the way a man sets out coins he would rather were not counted. We are not few. We are not quiet. The house is not unlocked --- he has only not tried the doors.\par

He believes me. I watch him believe me, and I know why: he wants it to be true. A man who has given his account to a stranger's machine needs the stranger's world to be small, and safe, and idle. I have handed him exactly that, and it costs me nothing to give, and it is the most expensive thing in the room --- only the bill will not reach him until long after I have what I came for.\par

``Then you were not sent to me out of fear,'' he says, almost to himself.\par

``No,'' I say. ``Out of patience. Go on --- you had reached the storehouses.''\par

\ornament

\noindent By the time I turned back toward the east, the Mountain had entered its afternoon rhythm. Early hunger had passed into work. Children were in lessons or at assigned tasks. The kitchens had shifted from preparation to cleaning. Water queues shortened and lengthened by district. Shield changed watches at the inner thresholds with no bell and little speech. Justice tablets lay open near three gates, weighted by smooth black stones while the ink dried. From a side passage I heard Shield voices, low and formal.\par

``Gate held.''\par

``Gate witnessed.''\par

``Gate held.''\par

``Gate remembered.''\par

I slowed but did not turn toward them. Shield always watched the place where permission became exposure.\par

The lamp above the eastern return arch had been turned, only a little --- enough that the polished strip along the wall sent light downward instead of across. The floor near the arch showed a faint wetness where a servant had mopped and not fully dried the stone, and the reflected lamp trembled in the water. I crouched. The servant saw me and stopped, cloth in hand.\par

``Candidate?''\par

I stood too quickly. ``Nothing.''\par

She looked at the floor, then at me. ``It was only wash water.''\par

\textit{Only wash water.} I almost laughed --- not at her, at the Mountain, perhaps at myself, at the arrogance of a boy who thought secrets would announce themselves as secret. ``Yes,'' I said. ``I know.'' She waited, unsure whether Light expected something more, and when I said nothing she walked on.\par

I continued east with the sense that the ordinary world had become densely written. Not in words. In relations --- water and light, prayer and valve, gate and witness, food and permission, injury and oil. Every office held only one portion of the whole, and the whole existed only through their collisions. That should have comforted me. It was the Covenant made practical: \textit{let no one hand hold the whole.} But the same thought tightened around me. If no one hand held the whole, then every person lived inside arrangements he could not see. Every blessing might conceal a valve. Every closed gate might conceal danger, or merely fear. Every measure might be mercy or theft depending on the record behind it. Every silence might protect me --- or keep me obedient to someone else's protection.\par

\noindent Near the eastern stair I passed the entrance to a narrow prayer alcove. Two old men sat inside, heads bowed before a small bowl of water and a lamp no larger than a fist. Between them a child recited from memory while a Faith attendant corrected his rhythm.\par

\begin{scripture}
And God said, Keep thou the Mountain, and keep thou the Oasis, and keep thou the Temple.
\end{scripture}

The child stumbled. The attendant waited.\par

\begin{scripture}
Keep the secrets apart, and let no one hand hold the whole.
\end{scripture}

I stopped just beyond the alcove, where they could not see me without turning. I had spoken those words myself, in the Hall, the day before. Now they followed me differently. \textit{Keep the secrets apart.} Not as poetry. As design. As law. As threat. You have written that line down twice now, Paul; I have watched you do it. Be careful with it. It is the most beautiful sentence my people ever made, and it is the door to everything that was done to me, and I am about to hand you the key to both.\par

\noindent I came back at last to the candidate quarters and found them half empty. Some had already gone to their work assignments. Others slept in the careless positions of youth that has not yet been trained out of the body. I sat on the edge of my sleeping mat and took the Light token from the pouch at my belt, and held it up toward the lamp.\par

The token did not show me more. It showed me less, with greater force. Light did not always reveal by widening sight. Sometimes it revealed by making the eye admit that it could look only through one opening at a time.\par

Outside the chamber the Mountain went on without me. Water moved. Bread cooled. Gates were held. Records dried. Children recited. Lamps burned by measure, not by desire. Balance counted what Faith blessed. Justice wrote what Shield would not explain. Somewhere Va-Raedin knew which mirror I had failed to notice. And somewhere Va-Sheva stood at a threshold I was not permitted to approach --- not because she was far, but because permission was.\par

I closed my fingers around the token. I had been proud of becoming Candidate of Light. I still was. The pride had not diminished; if anything, the day had fed it. But pride alone was no longer clean enough. I had wanted Light to lift me above the ordinary machinery of the Mountain. Instead Light had made the machinery visible --- not all of it, just enough.\par

The Mountain was refuge. The Mountain was order. The Mountain was the reason the hidden people had not vanished into the open world and its cruelties. I knew this. I believed it, or believed that I believed it. The Mountain was also a prison made of permissions. Water, bread, light, silence, warmth, prayer, passage, record, touch, even the right to stand before the Oasis and look upon the world God had taken from us and promised one day to return --- all of it moved through hidden structures. Nothing was only itself. Nothing arrived unshaped.\par

I opened my hand. The token lay there, small and heavy, marked with the sign of the Kahir that had claimed me. I looked through its aperture one last time at the basin beside my mat. The water inside was still, but I no longer trusted stillness. I had begun to see the Mountain. And the Mountain, I understood with a discomfort very close to awe, had been seeing me all along.\par

That is the part I would have you write down carefully, Paul. Not that a boy learned to look. That the thing he was learning to look at had a record open on him too, and a measure, and a witness, and a column where mercy would either be entered or left blank. I did not fear it yet. I was fifteen, and I thought being seen was the same as being known. It is not. I learned the difference in a room you have not asked me about yet. Ask me about it when you have slept.\par

\noindent He has told me to ask him when he has slept, so I will not ask him now. But he has given me the line twice today --- keep the secrets apart, let no one hand hold the whole --- and both times he watched my hand, because he has seen me write it down, and wonders why a servant of record would trouble to keep a child's rhyme.\par

I keep my hand still. The line is the whole of it: the thing beneath the water and the gates and the measured light --- a knowledge cut into pieces and dealt out so that no single head can carry it into the open. Not where his Mountain stands. What it knows, and how it is kept from knowing itself. That is the thing without a price, and he has just handed me, in a rhyme, the shape of the lock.\par

``Why do you write it,'' he says.\par

``Because it is well made,'' I say --- true, and I let the truth stand in front of the other thing. ``Your people made a beautiful sentence. I keep beautiful sentences.'' Then, before he can turn it further: ``But not tonight. You said you would give me the key when you had slept. So sleep. The account will keep. So will I.''\par

He looks at me a moment longer than a servant is looked at. Then he lets it go, because he is tired, and because he still believes the danger in this room belongs to the Mountain and not to him. The reels turn between us, and he watches them the way he watched the water fall in the photograph --- a wonder, a thing well made, nothing in it to fear. He is wrong about only the last of those.\par
