\typesetchapter{Chapter 3}{Mountain}

\begin{room}

--- The eighteenth of November, nineteen ninety-eight; nine o'clock; the second of our interviews. Before we take up the account, there is a small matter I am asked to put to you. I am told you kept your hour with the tutor yesterday, and then sent the physician away. The exercise. You would not do it.

--- I refused it, yes.

--- May I ask why?

--- Paul. I mean no disrespect to you; you have been courteous, and the courtesy is not nothing. But you should understand what you are asking, and of whom. I am not a nobody. I sat among the ten who mattered most in a world ten thousand years old. And you would have me spend my afternoons in coloured cloth, running after a ball, for no stake but the running. Grown men do this, I am told, for pleasure. I did not come this far to be improved by games.

--- No. I would not ask that of you, and I have no say over the regimen in any case; the physician has that, and I am only his messenger. But hear the thing in your own tongue, not in mine. It is not play. It is not for pleasure. The body is an instrument, and yours has come to us badly out of tune: underfed, overwalked, wrongly mended. You are to be brought to full working order. Function. Not fun.

--- Then say the rest of it. You did not learn to speak to me in that manner by accident. You want me whole because a whole man is worth more to you than a sick one. Watched. Measured. Kept. And with this, recorded.

--- I will not pretend you are wrong. You are the only one of your kind we have ever had to learn from, and we should be poor stewards to let you weaken while you are in our care. Both things are true in the one breath: that we wish you well, and that you are of great use to us well. I have stopped finding that as ugly as once I did.

--- What did you have in mind. Not the ball.

--- Swimming. We should like to teach you, in truth; I imagine there was little call for it where you were raised. There is a pool beneath the east wing: indoors, private, no eye upon it but the physician's. It asks everything of the body and shows nothing to the world. And I thought it might suit a man who dislikes being watched at his labour.

--- No eye upon it.

--- Only the physician's; and he reads numbers, not men.

--- I will try it. Once. I promise nothing.

--- Thank you. That is all I was sent to ask.

--- Who is the man above your fire? He has watched me since I sat down, and he has the face of one who was obeyed.

--- Edward Blackwood. He built the house you are sitting in, and a good deal more than the house. His business ran the whole way round the world: oil, metals, the desert, the things men went south and east for in his day. Toward the end he believed there was something out there worth more than all of it. He never found it.

--- And you have.

--- I have found you. That is a different sentence, and a smaller one. This house has spent his money, and his children's, on questions far smaller than the one I mean to put to you. Round the world. There is a question folded into that phrase, and I will put it to you before you begin: your people know the earth for a sphere. Do they not?

--- Funny, that you should ask me that one, of all the questions you might. You are too clever for your own good, Paul, as I once was. Yes. And also we do not. It is complicated.

--- That is not an answer.

--- No. It is the true shape of one. Ask me again in its place, and I will give you the long form, and you may wish I had not. This morning it is only complicated.

--- Then keep it. Yesterday they showed you the wall you have named the Mirror. Tell me what came next: what Light did with a boy who had just been shown where all of it falls.

\end{room}

\ornament

\noindent Light did not let a boy keep a revelation overnight and call it his own. The morning after the Mirror, before First Light had well filled the practice halls, Va-Raedin gave me one point off the wall and set me to learn it.\par

Not the wall. One point on it, low and toward the eastern side, steadier than most and given to a slow stir near Second Silence. ``That one is yours until you can walk it in the dark,'' he said. ``Source to fall, fall to source; every mirror it touches, every throat it passes, and whatever stands near its road that ought not to stand there. When you can draw me the whole of it with your eyes shut, you will be given a second. There are two hundred and fifty-five behind that one. Do not hurry. Hurry is how a candidate loses a beam and lights a wrong room looking for it.''\par

I was not sent alone. Two servants of Light were set to walk the beam with me --- not to guard it, for Light did not believe a beam wanted guarding, but because a Candidate fresh out of the Hall holds the doctrine of a beam and none of its habits, and a servant holds every habit and not one word of the doctrine, and the Mountain meant the two to grind together until something true came off.\par

The elder was Oren, a man of some twenty years, thick across the shoulders from a life of ladders, carrying the flat patience of one who has cleaned the same glass long enough to stop resenting it. He had walked beams since before I could read, and he taught as such men teach: by letting me be wrong first, and cheaply. The younger was a girl of fourteen, Sela, who bore the cloths and the little oil and said almost nothing. When Oren corrected me she watched to see how I would take it --- not unkindly; the way the young study a grown thing to learn what it is made of.\par

Two days had passed since my Ascension, and the Mountain had not yet finished paying for it. A ceremony spends order and does not replace it; for days afterward the whole body of the place labours to earn it back. Those who had stood too long in the Hall now had to carry, cook, count, wash, teach, bless, guard and record, out of turn and short of hands. The old who had been kept warm through the rite wanted broth. Faith had new instruction to send out under Ka-Elun. Justice had the transfer to reconcile against old tablets. And somewhere, because Light had taken a servant off some other task to walk a beam with a new Candidate, Balance was adjusting a ledger. Balance always adjusted the ledger. That word is a soft thing where I sit now, a near-compliment; in the Mountain \textit{noticing} was an office, and it wore a green robe, and it did not forgive.\par

\ornament

\noindent We began at the fall, in the low room itself, before the ordinary hours let anyone else near it. Oren held two fingers a breath from the wall, beneath my point, and did not touch the stone. ``This end you have been shown,'' he said. ``It is the easy end. Any fool can stand where a beam dies. The craft is walking it back up to where it was born, and knowing at every turn that it is still your beam under your hand, and not its neighbour wearing its coat.'' He ran the order the other way from the one Va-Raedin had set in me --- fall, surface, throat, source --- and added the things a Veil never mentions. ``At every surface you ask three questions, always the same, always in this order, or you will teach yourself to trust a beam that is lying to you. Is the glass clean. Is the glass square. Is there a second light near enough to marry this one and give you their child in place of your beam.'' Sela lifted a small square of polished bronze from her satchel without being asked. ``She carries the test-glass because your eye will fail where the doctrine says use the glass, and a man who trusts his eye there is the man who lights the wrong room. Ask the three. Every surface. Even the ones you cleaned yourself an hour ago. \textit{Especially} those.''\par

I had thought the Mirror was the lesson. Following my one thread of light out of that room, I understood that the Mirror had only been the promise of it. The lesson was this: a beam is patient and a man is not, and every place they disagree, the man is wrong.\par

\begin{room}

--- You keep saying it: source to fall, and back again to the source. You walk a beam home to where it is born. Tell me, then, what is born there. A mirror moves light; it does not make it. What is the source, at the top of all these threads, under a mountain, in the dark?

--- The sun. What else would it be? The Mountain bores shafts up to the sky, narrow as a needle and cut truer and longer than you would believe a man could cut them, and drinks what little Heaven lets fall down them. Everything after that --- every mirror, every throat, the whole of the wall you call the Mirror --- is only the careful spending of that little. We make no light. We divide the sun.

\end{room}

\ornament

\noindent We did not get far before Shield stopped us. A warden raised one hand at the first turn and held back three workers, two children, and the three of us together. ``South service way is closed,'' he said.\par

A woman with folded cloth on her hip frowned. ``Since when?''\par

``Since it was closed.''\par

``I have cloth due in the herb rooms.''\par

``Then it will arrive by another passage.''\par

``That adds half a descent.''\par

The warden looked at the bundles, not the woman. ``Then the cloth will be tired.''\par

The children laughed until their father quieted them. The woman did not laugh; she shifted the cloth and turned to go the long way, irritated and unsurprised. No one asked why the way was shut. In the Mountain a closure did not owe anyone a reason. It owed everyone an adjustment. I have thought since that this is the truest thing I could tell a stranger about the place --- not the darkness, but the way a shut door was answered with a shrug and a longer walk and never once with a question. The warden's eyes went to my robe and my bandage, then to Oren. ``Light?''\par

``A beam,'' Oren said, ``running to the east stores. We will take the lower wash route.''\par

The warden stepped aside, and as he did a woman in Balance green passed straight through the closed way with a sealed slate under her arm and no one to stop her. I watched her go. Permission, I understood then, was not a wall. It was a sorting mechanism: the same door that turned back cloth let green through without a word. I was proud of the thought. I did not yet know how much of my life it would cost me to finish it.\par

\ornament

\noindent The beam did not keep to Light. Few of them did, Oren said; that was half the reason to walk them. Mine climbed a service throat, crossed above a washing gallery through a slot no wider than a wrist, and came down again into the trafficked stone where Balance kept the water.\par

The first queue had already formed along a shallow channel, watched by two clerks in green and one Faith attendant. Each household bore a marked vessel; a copper rod lay across the channel, and the clerks walked it from notch to notch, reading allowance by household mark, by work, by illness, by the unborn, by yesterday's draw. The Faith attendant blessed each jar before the measure was let go. Her prayer did not slow the work. It timed it.\par

``Half measure,'' a clerk said, to a woman who had already opened her mouth.\par

``Yesterday was half.''\par

``Yesterday was second descent. This is third. Your house drew early, after the heat of the Ascension.''\par

The woman's jaw set, then let go, and she took the half-filled jar. I watched the water settle in it. The surface caught a high lamp and bent along the curve of the clay, so the jar showed fuller from one side than the other. I knew it for half measure only because Balance had said half measure. Without the mark, the eye would have argued. I was fifteen, standing over a stranger's water, and I understood a thing I would spend the rest of my life failing to unlearn: that the eye is a liar the moment a record is not standing beside it.\par

My beam ran on above the channel, and I nearly lost it there, caught for a breath by a brighter fleck off a wet patch on the wall. I said, ``There,'' and reached, and Oren let me put my whole certainty into the wrong light before he said, quietly, ``Ask the three.'' The glass showed my error at once. My beam was the duller one, higher, married for an instant to the wet-wall shine and now parting from it. The bright thing I had wanted was somebody's spill. ``You lost it because you liked the look of the other one better,'' Oren said. ``A beam does not care to be liked.''\par

\ornament

\noindent Beyond the water the kitchens opened in a run of low, heated chambers, and the air thickened with steam and yeast and boiled grain and the sour-sweet breath of things left to ferment. The workers were mostly Balance, but Faith was in all their timing --- short prayers to seal a jar, longer ones to open stored grain, work-chants to keep hands moving together when the heat made speech unpleasant.\par

\begin{scripture}
Let every fruit be gathered. Let every vessel be sealed. Let no waste enter the Oasis.
\end{scripture}

They did not sing it beautifully. Some did not sing it at all. One woman sang with plain devotion; another used the lines to count her kneading; a boy mouthed them only when an elder's eye came round. Faith, I saw, did not require the same belief from every throat. It required the rhythm to hold. That was a more useful thing to know than most of what I had been taught to believe, and it unsettled me that I could see it so nakedly and still mean to kneel that evening as though I had not.\par

Two girls dusted trays with powdered shell. Calcium, I remembered from a childhood lesson --- belonging to no romance and no myth and no prayer, only to bone and tooth and poultice and mortar and the quiet insistence of bodies. Above a locked hatch three goats were painted on a stone panel, square-bearded, solemn-eyed, and beneath them a line stood cut in Balance script and copied below in common hand:\par

\begin{scripture}
THE OUTSIDE FEEDS ONLY WHAT THE MOUNTAIN CAN COUNT.
\end{scripture}

I slowed at the word. \textit{Outside.} It struck differently now than it had a week before --- the Oasis, the shadow of forest in the old teachings, the appointed prayer before the Threshold, Balance tending green things beyond the stone and Shield watching the paths back. A kitchen supervisor caught me staring. ``You want goat duty, Light?'' A few workers laughed. ``No,'' I said. ``Then keep moving. Even Light casts shadows.'' I moved.\par

\ornament

\noindent It was in the wash passage below the kitchens that Sela undid me, without meaning to, and without ever knowing she had.\par

I had tangled the beam a second time, under a run of brackets, and could not find it. She did not say she had it. She touched my sleeve and lifted her chin toward a fleck riding a bracket I had walked past twice, and when I saw it, and she saw me see it, something opened in her face --- a plain, unguarded gladness, the pleasure of a child who has shown a grown thing where the bird is hiding. It was there and gone. She turned back to the cloths.\par

I have no gentler way to set it down, so I will set it down plainly. I had known a girl my whole life. I had chased her through the service tunnels and laughed and wept with her and stood across a Hall from her two days before and felt hope stand up in me at the wrong moment. And in all of it I had never once seen her look at anything the way this stranger of fourteen had just looked at a bracket. I did not understand, that morning, why so small a thing should lie in my chest like a stone. I would understand it long afterward, when I saw the very same light cross a face I had loved my whole life --- and found that it was not turned toward me, and never had been, and was not going to be. The young and the glad are cruel that way. They hand you the measure of what you are missing and do not know they have done it.\par

\ornament

\noindent Near the tool wash a man was recording where no water ran. Sa-Tavan, with a slate under one arm and the new black cord at his wrist, narrow and composed and faintly disapproving, though that may only be the face Justice gives its children before they earn a better one.\par

``You are far from the east passage,'' he said.\par

``So are you.''\par

``I have a permission mark.'' He lifted the slate; pale wax pressed into the corner, stamped with the sign of Justice.\par

``Congratulations.''\par

``It is not an honour. It is a route allowance.''\par

``That is not what I meant.''\par

``It is what you said.''\par

I almost smiled. ``What are you recording?''\par

``A disputed sequence in the second water queue. A household says the mark was advanced after the jar was filled. Balance says before. Faith witnessed the blessing but not the measure. So Justice records the order while the memory is still warm.''\par

``And if you record it correctly?''\par

``The next measure can be corrected, if correction is owed.''\par

``And if you record it wrongly?''\par

His face changed, not to fear but to something graver. ``Then a correction becomes a wound.''\par

It was not the answer I had expected from a boy I had thought dry. But a measure misrecorded became hunger. A witness misplaced became punishment. A sequence altered became guilt where none belonged. Justice did not wait because it loved delay. It waited because an act without its sequence could change the meaning of everything that came after it. I did not know, that morning, that I was speaking with the one who would one day sit in a chamber and record the sequence of me. If I had known, I do not think I would have changed a word. He would not have let me.\par

He turned to go, then stopped. ``Your thumb is bleeding through. If Light cut it in ceremony and training reopened it, have it witnessed.''\par

``Why?''\par

``So responsibility is not confused. Between a ritual wound and an instruction wound.''\par

I stared at him, then laughed once. He did not. ``If it scars badly,'' he said, ``the record should know why,'' and was gone before I could answer. I decided not to have it witnessed. I have wondered since whether that was the first small crime of my life, or only the first I noticed.\par

\ornament

\noindent He has spent an hour on water and waste and the marking of things --- the copper rod, the disputed sequence, a boy with a cord who thinks a misplaced record is a wound. I have let him run, and taken the reel past where the subject strictly warrants, because it is exactly the kind of detail a man inventing a people never troubles to invent. A fabricator gives his people a god and a monster; he does not sit up nights deciding which prayer is said over the clean jar and which over the waste pot. The economy of it is wrong for a lie. It is too dear to be one. That is an observation, not a verdict; I have admired expensive things before and remembered afterward that expense is not truth.\par

Then he turns it on me, which he has begun to do. ``And out here,'' he says. ``A week in this house and I have not seen a jar carried, nor a queue, nor a clerk at a channel. How do you keep yourselves alive? Where is your water counted?''\par

It is a fair question and a harmless answer, so I give it whole. ``We do not count water. It runs in the walls, in pipe, much as your Mountain runs it, only into every room, and warm as readily as cold. A man turns a handle and takes as much as he likes, and no one counts it.'' I lay a photograph on the table by the reels: a washroom in white stone, a bright tap, a fall of water stopped dead in the air.\par

He does not reach for it. He studies the object before the picture --- the fact of a fixed image reaches him a beat before its content does. ``Every room,'' he says. ``Warm. And no measure.'' Not the greed of a man gathering corroboration for a tale he is telling; nearer to arithmetic failing --- a boy taught to thank a cistern, looking at water no one thought worth thanking. I keep the note, on the reel and in my own account: \textit{he does not perform the surprise. Either it is real, or he is very well drilled.} I leave the second clause where a careful man leaves what he cannot yet settle.\par

``Go on,'' I say. ``You were following your beam east.''\par

\ornament

\noindent I passed the child halls on the way, cut into warmer stone near the family districts, and the beam gave me an excuse to slow that I dressed up as discipline. Children sat in three groups on woven mats while a Faith teacher moved among them with a cord of five knots. The youngest repeated after her, stumbling on the old forms.\par

\begin{scripture}
The Mountain is refuge. \\
The Oasis is water. \\
The forest was plenty. \\
Beyond the forest thou shalt not wander.
\end{scripture}

The teacher touched the fifth knot. ``And if the forest is gone?'' The youngest faltered; the older answered for them.\par

\begin{scripture}
The Covenant remains.
\end{scripture}

The day before, the words would have sounded complete. Now they sounded like the surface of something harder. The forest was gone. The Covenant remained. If a child did not know what had been lost, the line comforted; if an adult knew, it instructed grief to behave. I have heard a great deal of scripture since, in the outside's churches and in mine, and I have found no cleaner definition of the thing than that: a line that tells grief how to behave.\par

\begin{room}

--- The children's third line. Say it to me again.

--- The forest was plenty.

--- A forest. Not the Oasis, not a garden: a forest, where you are, in the deep desert, taught to the children as a thing that once was and is gone.

--- Water everywhere once, the old lines say. Then water in one place only. Then in less than that. The forest was the first of it to go.

--- You understand what you have just handed me. A forest does not become a desert in a lifetime, nor in ten.

--- No. It does not. I have always known the Mountain to be very old. It is one of the few things about it I was ever permitted to know.

\end{room}

On the far wall hung five small panels for the Kahir --- Balance a cup and a sealed jar, Faith an open text, Shield a gate, Light an aperture, Justice a black cord crossing pale stone. And beyond the Oasis margin, almost lost in the carved desert, a single dark rock had been cut smaller than a fingernail. No child was asked to name it. They were too young to fear an omission; they pointed and whispered and guessed which office would one day claim them, as though choosing sweets from a festival tray. One girl pointed to Light. ``They know all the secret doors.'' The teacher turned. ``No.'' The girl shrank. ``Light knows that doors are not the first secret.'' I almost stayed to hear the rest. I moved on. I have regretted small departures like that one more than most of my sins; there is always a sentence you leave a room one breath before it finishes, and it turns out to have been the sentence.\par

\ornament

\noindent The beam ran east along the lamp corridors, and there Light stopped being ceremonial and became necessary. The Mountain could not live in darkness, nor flood itself with flame; oil was rationed, smoke drawn, mirrors cleaned without scratching. A lamp here served three ends at once --- guide the worker, reveal the danger, and hide the routes not meant for every eye. Narrow inspection slots let Light see whether a distant lamp burned true without setting a body in a passage where it had no right to stand.\par

Oren stopped a young servant carrying a cold lamp toward a stair and made me watch him teach it. ``Wick first,'' he said. ``Is it trimmed even? A leaning wick throws the beam crooked and you will chase your own bad wick all afternoon and call it a shadow. Then the oil --- lift it, listen; if it sounds under half you fill it here, at the foot, never at the top, because oil on a high step is a fall waiting for the next servant who cannot see it. The mirror last, and with the cloth, never the hand. Wick, oil, glass. In that order, every lamp.'' The boy mouthed it back and went off holding it like a rule he had been given rather than a chore. ``That is the whole of what we give a servant,'' Oren said to me, low, once the boy was gone. ``The order of a thing. Do it in this sequence and no one sends you back down. What we do not give him is the reason --- why the oil at the foot, what the third slot really watches, which doors the light is set to leave dark. The reason is the Veil's, and the reason is the part that carries a price.'' I liked the lamp corridors more than I wanted to. Here Light wore its least pious face: no chant, no gravure, no ceremonial flame bending toward a holy cheek. Only oil, soot, wick, screw, angle, dust, air --- infrastructure dressed as grace, or grace made possible by infrastructure, and I could not decide which thought discomforted me more. I can decide now. A grace you can build, you can also withhold.\par

\ornament

\noindent Where the beam crossed the repair spaces I lost an hour I could not spare, because the repair halls belonged to no Kahir entirely and so held a constant low argument kept in check by procedure. Balance inspected the materials, Light the reflective surfaces, Shield the locks and hinges, Justice anything whose failure had drawn blood, Faith blessed new work on a sacred route. A Balance man turned a pipe section in both hands. ``It will hold.'' A Light woman shook her head. ``It reflects into the side slit.'' ``It is pipe. It is not meant to reflect.'' ``It reflects whether you mean it or not.'' ``Then dull it.'' ``Dull it and it corrodes sooner.'' An old Justice recorder, a slate on her lap, said without looking up, ``State the consequence of each choice.'' ``Polished, it misdirects light.'' ``Dulled, it weakens sooner.'' ``Time to failure?'' ``Unknown.'' ``Then it is not yet a choice,'' the recorder said. ``It is two fears.'' There was no force in her voice, and both workers stopped. I have sat in rooms since where powerful men shouted for an hour and moved nothing, and thought of that old woman and her slate, naming a thing correctly until the noise had nowhere left to stand.\par

Past the repair halls the beam took us by the medical rooms, under warmer vents, the air rich and green and bitter with hung herbs and trays of pale fungus. A child sat very still while a healer worked paste into a burn on his forearm, a Faith attendant keeping his breathing even, a Balance clerk noting the paste and oil and cloth the wound would cost. ``Will it scar?'' the mother asked. The healer glanced at the clerk before she answered. ``It will mark.'' ``A mark is not always a wound,'' the attendant said. ``He will need extra oil in the dressing, three days,'' said the clerk, and the mother flinched at the word \textit{extra} until he added, ``recorded under injury allowance,'' and relief went through her so plainly I was ashamed to have seen it. Mercy here was not a warmth in the chest. It was a category. If the burn was recorded rightly the child had his oil; if not, pity had to fight the storehouse, and pity did not always win. Where I was raised, mercy was a column in a book, and a child's arm healed or scarred by whether a clerk wrote the right word in it.\par

\ornament

\noindent The beam ran at last to the edge of the storehouses, where relation in the Mountain stops being an idea and becomes a set of gates. There were three: the first open and busy, the second asking a mark, the third watched by Shield and witnessed by Justice --- and my thread of light, with the perfect indifference of made things, ran along the lintel of the second and in under the third.\par

In the outer hall a woman in green inspected a tray of dried figs, cutting one, frowning, setting it aside; another; another. The worker who had brought them shifted. ``Mould?'' ``Beginning.'' ``How much lost?'' ``That depends on whether you argue.'' He closed his mouth. She sorted them into three --- clean, questionable, spoiled. The spoiled went sealed into a waste pot; the questionable were marked for boiling; the clean were weighed again, because loss changes the measure. And a Faith attendant spoke one line over the clean and a different line over the waste. Even waste received language. Nothing left the order of the Mountain without being named.\par

``Sa-Kailan.'' The voice was older, female, and carried enough authority that I straightened before I turned. Ka-Leth, Keeper of Balance, broad and grave, her hands stained faintly green. Up close she was less ceremonial and more dangerous. She looked at my robe, my bandage, and the small distance between my feet and the restricted line painted on the floor. ``Are you assigned here?''\par

``I am on a beam, Keeper. Light business. It runs under your third gate.''\par

``It does not. It runs \textit{to} my third gate, and stops at my line, as you will. A beam is Light's to follow. What is behind that gate is Balance's to keep, and Shield's, and the line on the floor is where those two truths are made to agree. You may see where your light goes in. You may not go in after it.'' I had the doctrine half out of my mouth --- that a beam belongs to the office that reads it, root to fall --- and Oren, half a step back, went so still that I closed it. ``Then I will note the gate as its end,'' I said, ``and carry the rest as unwalked.''\par

``Better.'' She looked at the sorted figs, then back at me. ``Since Light has sent you into my stores to learn your trade in them, learn this with it. That waste pot is sealed and counted, and the counting costs a clerk and a prayer and a pot. It would be cheaper to throw the figs down a shaft. We do not, because waste that is not counted becomes invisible, and invisible waste becomes theft --- from someone who does not yet know he has been robbed. Every beam you walk is the same. It burns oil, it costs a corridor, it puts three bodies where three other bodies then cannot be. Revelation is not free. Curiosity least of all. Light forgets that, being the one office that pays in another's coin.'' She turned for the gate. ``Note your end. Step back from my line.''\par

I stepped back. Shield opened the third gate for her without a word, and in the moment it stood wide I saw past her --- not much, and not for long, and nothing I could name. A low chamber. Two wardens, and a third figure between them who did not stand like a warden. A shape under grey cloth on a table too small to be stores. And along the near wall, where the lamp found them, a row of small dark objects I had no word for, each one ringed and cold, that were not oil, and were not grain, and were not anything Balance counts. Then the gate closed, and it was only a gate again.\par

I did not understand what I had seen. That is the honest account of it, and it matters that it is honest: I understood nothing. I had been brought to the edge of it lawfully, by a beam that was mine to follow and a line that was mine to respect, and I had looked because looking was the whole of my new craft, and I had seen a fragment I could not read. I filed it the way Light had already begun to teach me to file things --- not as a secret, for I did not know it was one, but as a surface that had not yet answered its three questions. Oren was looking at the closed gate with the particular flatness of a man taking great care not to look at it. Sela was looking at it a beat too long, and when she felt me notice, she looked away too quickly and said, of nothing, ``The beam ends at the line.'' Which was true, and which no one had asked her to say.\par

\ornament

\noindent He stops himself there, on the edge of the gate he could not go through, and I feel the whole account tighten around the thing he says he did not see. I do not press it. A man who tells you, unprompted, that he understood nothing, and tells you \textit{because} it is true, is a man to let run; the place he hurried past is marked on the reel and will keep. I ask him something smaller instead, to let the ground cool, and take the compass from the case beside my chair and set it on the table. The needle swings, hunts, lies down.\par

``You have said \textit{east} to me a dozen times this morning, and \textit{south}, the way I would say \textit{left} --- without thinking, without checking. But you were under a mountain. No sun, no stars, no horizon. How does a boy in a sealed hall know which way is east?''\par

``Because the Mountain knows,'' he says, as though I have asked why water is wet. ``It was built knowing. The halls are cut to it. A child learns the directions as he learns the corridors; they are the one lesson.'' He bends to the compass with the flat attention he gives everything, and I watch him not know it, which is itself worth keeping. ``It moved,'' he says, ``and then it chose. What told it which way?''\par

``The earth. The needle is a small lodestone; the earth a very great one; the small lies down along the pull of the great, near enough north-and-south wherever a man stands. With this I could find east in a cellar with the door shut. Your people keep the direction in the walls. Mine keep it in a box.''\par

He is quiet a moment. ``Then you have made a small piece of the Mountain,'' he says, ``that a man can carry in his pocket.'' It is a better sentence than my question earned, and I do not tell him so. I keep it instead, on the reel and in the second account no employer asked me for: they hold a true bearing with no instrument and no sky, by the fixed making of the place alone. That is either a very old accident or a very old intention, and I have not settled which answer I like less.\par

``Go on,'' I say. ``The beam ended at Balance's line. What did you do with the rest of a day you could not walk?''\par

\noindent I noted the gate as the end of the beam, and I did not sleep on it, and that was the beginning of everything they would one day try me for --- though it looked, that afternoon, like nothing more than a good boy doing his sums. Oren walked me back down the beam twice more, until I could name every surface on it without the glass and then be shamed by the glass, and between passes the body of the Mountain went on with its keeping around me.\par

Near the eastern return arch a run of Shield voices came low and formal from a side passage, a litany with no music in it: \textit{Gate held. Gate witnessed. Gate held. Gate remembered.} I slowed but did not turn toward them. Shield always watched the place where permission became exposure. And near the eastern stair I passed a narrow prayer alcove where two old men bowed before a fist-sized lamp and a small bowl of water while a child recited and a Faith attendant mended his rhythm.\par

\begin{scripture}
And God said, Keep thou the Mountain, and keep thou the Oasis, and keep thou the Temple.
\end{scripture}

The child stumbled. The attendant waited.\par

\begin{scripture}
Keep the secrets apart, and let no one hand hold the whole.
\end{scripture}

I stopped just past the alcove, where they could not see me without turning. I had spoken those words myself, in the Hall, two days before. Now they followed me differently. \textit{The Temple.} I had said it a thousand times and never once asked where it stood, or what it kept, that it should be named in one breath with the Mountain and the Oasis and set apart from both. And \textit{keep the secrets apart} --- not as poetry now, but as design, as law, as threat. It was the most beautiful sentence my people ever made, and it was the door to everything that was done to me, and I would come, in the end, to hold the key to both.\par

\ornament

\noindent I came back at last to the candidate quarters half empty, and sat on the edge of my mat, and took the Light token from the pouch at my belt, and held it toward the lamp. It did not show me more. It showed me less, with greater force --- Light did not always reveal by widening sight; sometimes it revealed by making the eye admit that it could look through only one opening at a time.\par

Outside the chamber the Mountain went on without me. Water moved. Bread cooled. Gates were held and remembered. Records dried under smooth black stones. Children recited the forest they had never seen. Lamps burned by measure, not by desire. Balance counted what Faith blessed; Justice wrote the sequence Shield would not explain; and somewhere Va-Sheva stood at a threshold I was not permitted to approach --- not because she was far, but because permission was. I had wanted Light to lift me above the machinery of the place. Instead Light had made the machinery visible: not all of it, only enough to spoil the stories that had kept me warm.\par

For the law held everywhere I had walked that day, in valve and blessing and gate and measure and column of oil: each office kept one portion, and the whole existed only where the portions were forced to meet. It should have comforted me. It was the Covenant made practical, the thing I had recited not two days before --- \textit{let no one hand hold the whole}. But the comfort curdled as I sat. If no single hand held the whole, then every soul in the Mountain lived inside arrangements it could not see. Every blessing might hide a valve. Every closed gate might hide danger, or only fear. Every measure was mercy or theft by a record kept elsewhere, by someone who would never have to meet the eyes of the robbed. I had learned to see. And the thing I had learned to see, I understood with a discomfort very near awe, had a record open on me too --- a measure, a witness, a column where mercy would be entered or left blank --- and had been keeping it, patiently, the whole time I imagined myself the one doing the looking. I was fifteen, and I thought being seen was the same as being known. It is not. I would learn the difference in a room no one had asked me about yet.\par

\noindent He has given me the sentence twice now --- \textit{keep the secrets apart; let no one hand hold the whole} --- and both times watched the reel turn while he said it, because he has worked out that I am keeping the line and cannot work out why a man sent to disprove him would trouble to keep a child's catechism. The reason is duller than his suspicion. I keep it because it is the most efficient idea I have met in a week. A people who cannot be made to surrender the whole, because no one of them holds it: split the knowledge, and the traitor, the captive, the fool with the loose tongue each carry only a fragment, and the thing outlives the weakest man who keeps it. \textit{Divide et serva} --- divide, and preserve; the old empires only ever learned the first half. It is sound engineering in the dress of scripture, and sound engineering is true whether or not a syllable of the account around it is. I have spent my working life admiring things that are true regardless of who is lying beside them.\par

``Why do you keep it,'' he says. Not a pen; there is no pen; he means the line, turned over twice and set down twice on the reel.\par

``Because it is well made,'' I say, which is true, and enough. ``Your people built a good sentence. I collect good sentences.'' And then, before he can prise it wider: ``But not tonight. You said you would hand me the key to it when you had slept. So sleep. The account will keep; so shall I.''\par

He holds me in his look a moment longer than a man in my chair is usually held. Then he lets it go, because he is tired, and the reels turn between us, and he watches them the way he watched the water in the photograph and the needle on the table --- a made thing, working. I came into this house to find the seam where the account stops being true, and in a week I have not found it. That does not yet trouble me; a seam withheld a week is not the same as no seam, and I have done this work long enough to know the difference. But I have started a second list, the one no employer asked me for, of the things a liar would have had no reason to invent. It is not a long list. It is longer tonight than it was this morning, by the width of a gate he swears he did not look through.\par
