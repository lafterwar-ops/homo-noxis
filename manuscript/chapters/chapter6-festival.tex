\typesetchapter{Chapter 6}{Festival}

\begin{room}

--- The twenty-fifth of November; quarter to nine; the fifth of our interviews. Shall we start?

--- Not yet. No question has come to me worth the tape, and I should like to wait until your \textit{Civilisation} is finished before I put one.

--- The instruction and the physical training do not resume until tomorrow. We have the entire day.

--- Then let me put a smaller one. Who chooses them? The creatures; then the heavens; then the climb of your kind; and now your cathedrals. Someone set those in that order. A tutor does not build a week; he teaches the hour he is handed.

--- \ldots{} That is not mine to say.

--- No. But you have just said it. You asked me once about the Genesis gravure. Today your question is answered, in its proper place.

\end{room}

I kept arranging the five lessons. Reflection, shadow, focus, turning, threshold return --- I carried them everywhere; the order felt like a path.\par

Weeks passed. The lessons stopped. Instruction became ordinary again, which I found almost insulting. Lamps cleaned, mirrors adjusted, wicks trimmed, old smoke scraped from the high vents. Twice I spent a whole morning learning how an unclean mirror could betray a sealed door by scattering what it should have returned whole.\par

``Boredom is useful,'' Va-Raedin said on the third such morning.\par

When no lesson was given, I searched where searching could be disguised as service. I studied the great gravure on the southern wall until it blurred. I watched how the lamps struck the carved hand of God differently at morning and at evening. I counted where shadows gathered and failed to move. The geometry refused me.\par

\ornament

Then Ka-Syphiron of Light summoned me.\par

The summons came through Va-Raedin at the end of a morning exercise so plain I had nearly fallen asleep standing. He waited until the room had emptied.\par

``Ka-Syphiron will receive you before the second meal.''\par

The Keeper of Light received me in a room with no view and almost no object: a low table, an unlit lamp, three polished stones, one chair for him and none for me. He stood when I entered.\par

He had the beauty of something preserved past kindness --- white hair, fine hands, a face calm enough to make feeling seem a defect in other people. He wore no ceremonial robe, only pale cloth with a thread of gold at the throat, and he looked more like the Keeper there than he ever had in the Hall.\par

``Sa-Kailan of Light,'' he said.\par

``Keeper.''\par

``Va-Raedin tells me you are quick.''\par

I wavered between accepting the word and defending against it. ``I try to learn.''\par

``Trying is common. Learning is less so. Understanding is rarer. Wisdom is rarer still.''\par

He gestured to a stone set before him --- to stand on, a place chosen for me by another man. I stepped onto it.\par

``Tell me what a Veil of Light is.''\par

The answer came easily, and therefore badly. ``A Veil learns who placed the light.''\par

``That is doctrine. I asked what a Veil is.''\par

I hesitated. ``The one who has crossed beyond Candidate instruction.''\par

``By what act?''\par

I looked at the unlit lamp on the table. I already had the answer, and had forgotten it. ``By finding the Veil's Chamber.''\par

``Yes.''\par

The plainness of the word went into me like cold water.\par

``The title is not given for cleverness,'' he said. ``Not for age, not for loyalty, not for hunger, not for the admiration of one's teacher, not for the impatience of one's own mind. A Candidate of Light becomes Veil of Light by finding the Veil's Chamber. Until then he is Candidate, whatever he believes he has understood.''\par

I felt the words arrange themselves around me like bars. ``And if he never finds it?''\par

``Then he never becomes Veil.''\par

``Even if the Keeper dies?''\par

``The Mountain does not owe a man the office he failed to reach.''\par

I thought of Va-Raedin. He had found the chamber long ago; that was what being Veil meant. Had he stood on this stone once, in front of another Keeper, young, hiding his hunger?\par

``Does the Chamber teach the next path?'' I asked.\par

``You are not yet entitled to next paths.''\par

He crossed to the table and touched the unlit lamp, unhurried, the way a man tests a familiar instrument. ``You have the flaw of many gifted candidates,'' he said, and he said it almost warmly, as though it were a quality he had collected before. ``You think a locked door is an insult addressed personally to you. A door has no opinion of you, Sa-Kailan of Light; it was hung before you were named. Sometimes a locked door is only a mercy you have not yet earned the right to resent --- and sometimes it is a very patient kind of grave. From the outside the two look identical.''\par

``Look at me.'' I looked. ``You are seen,'' he said. ``Not celebrated. Seen. There is a difference. Va-Raedin sees speed in you. I see appetite. Speed may serve Light. Appetite must be disciplined before it convinces itself that hunger is revelation.''\par

The younger brother from the Book of Blood came into my mind without my calling him --- small in body, cunning in heart, watching a strength that others loved until \textit{envy entered him as a serpent entereth the grass}. The old story had become too useful against me.\par

``You may go,'' he said.\par

\ornament

And that was all. No clue, no riddle, no test. Only the rule, set down where I could no longer pretend ignorance of it. I walked out worse off than I had walked in. He had closed every posture I had left: stillness would convict me of the failure he had described, and motion would convict me of the appetite he had named.\par

I tried twice to follow Va-Raedin. The first time he stopped without turning and told me I was making noise with my left sandal. The second cured me. I waited near the old Light gallery where I had seen the five markings, certain he would return there after evening instruction, and held still until my legs ached and I was proud of my patience. Then a junior attendant walked in, handed me a folded note, and left without a word. The note read: \textit{A hidden watcher should not cast such a visible shadow.} After that I stopped following Va-Raedin.\par

For several days I stayed away from the Hall of Ceremony, to spare myself the admission that I went there to search rather than pray. Then I went anyway. The chamber received me with indifference; one restless Candidate weighed nothing against stone laid down before my great-grandfathers were named.\par

I began with the architecture and failed. Then I turned to scripture. Faith had preserved the oldest words. Faith kept chambers barred to Light, or claimed to. Faith's rites opened doors no lamp could open.\par

Ka-Elun of Faith was too high and too newly enthroned. I requested audience with Va-Maera of Faith instead, through the public petition line beside the Hall of Ceremony. She was older than Ka-Elun by several years, which made her position strange to anyone outside Faith and ordinary to everyone within it.\par

She received me in a narrow chamber outside the Hall of Prayers. \par

The room held no ornament but a covered bowl of water and a hanging cord of five knots. No lamp showed, yet the light came softly from somewhere above. She stood the whole time. She wore white, a heavier white than the attendants, layered and severe, her hair fully covered, her face unpainted, her eyes dark and steady. I had expected devotion and found administration sharpened by holiness.\par

``You request access to the Hall of Prayers,'' she said.\par

``Yes, Veil.''\par

``Why?''\par

``To study the Covenant.''\par

``The Covenant is read publicly.''\par

``Not all of it.''\par

``No.''\par

``I am Candidate of Light.''\par

``I see your robe.''\par

``I seek the Veil's Chamber.''\par

The words sounded too naked the moment they were out. She kept her face still. ``Then seek it in Light.''\par

I had prepared careful sentences. In the corridors they had seemed subtle. ``Faith works closely with Light,'' I said. ``In ceremony. In mist. In timing. In the way certain signs are made visible. I have seen enough to know that what people call miracle may depend on arrangements not everyone understands.''\par

The air changed. The room became suddenly smaller, and I understood that I had misjudged the distance between allusion and threat.\par

She took one step toward me. ``Say that again.''\par

I kept silent.\par

``Good,'' she said. ``Some doors stay closed because the fool outside them still has one living instinct.''\par

``I did not mean---''\par

``You meant enough. You come to Faith wearing Light's new hunger, and because you have seen mist in a prayer hall, because a lamp and a valve once agreed in front of your clever eyes, you think you stand above the people who kneel? You think you have found a crack in Faith? You have found a crack in yourself.''\par

``I was asking for access,'' I said, and the sentence failed while I was still speaking it.\par

``No. You were suggesting that Faith should fear what you might know. Such suggestions do not endanger Faith. They endanger you first. If danger follows those words, it will find your door long before it reaches mine.''\par

I felt cold under my robe. She came closer.\par

``You have learned small tricks of perception and mistaken them for height. Mist is not Faith. Timing is not Faith. Do you think God is disproved by a valve? You are not the first bright child to discover that blessing may have a mechanism. You may be the first foolish enough to speak of it as leverage in a house of prayer.''\par

``I was not threatening.''\par

``No. You were too clumsy to threaten properly. That does not make you innocent.''\par

I lowered my eyes. I could feel my pulse in my throat. Her voice softened.\par

``We speak with archangels of obedience and demons of appetite. We keep names that may not be spoken where children sleep. We know prayers for birth, madness, treason, plague, thirst, deformity, despair, and the moment before a condemned man understands that mercy will not interrupt justice. We bless marriages and executions. We comfort mothers whose children have been given to roles that will consume them. We know punishments your little training rooms have never heard of, and consolations beyond your imagining. And you come to me with mist?''\par

I stayed where I stood.\par

``Look at me. You understand almost nothing,'' she said. ``That is not your sin. Your sin is that you have begun to suspect it and decided to compensate by becoming dangerous. You are making a chain of mistakes. If you continue, you will not become a god of hidden knowledge. You will become an example, spoken of carefully to other gifted boys.''\par

The terror was that she had made consequence feel larger than Shield, or Justice, or Ka-Elun of Faith. I believed, suddenly and completely, that Faith could destroy me without touching me. Whether any of that was true mattered nothing. It was true enough in the room.\par

I folded. ``I withdraw the request,'' I said.\par

``Of course you do.''\par

``I apologise, Veil.''\par

``No. You submit. Apology is not required. Apology is what people offer when they still hope to manage the injury.''\par

I could barely speak. ``I submit.''\par

She stepped back. ``You may listen in the Hall of Ceremony like any child, elder, worker, mourner, or sinner. You may pray where prayer is permitted. You may not enter the Hall of Prayers. You may not request private scripture. You may not speak again of Faith's arrangements unless Faith first asks you what you think you have seen.''\par

``Yes, Veil.''\par

``And Sa-Kailan?''\par

``Yes?''\par

``If you truly seek Light, learn first that not every brightness belongs to you.''\par

She dismissed me with a movement of two fingers.\par

I left that chamber shaking, and hated the shaking only after I had gone three corridors and found that no one had followed.\par

\ornament

Shame walked with me. I imagined Ka-Syphiron of Light looking at me with calm recognition, as though appetite had behaved exactly as he had predicted. But the Hall was public, and public devotion protected me from suspicion. My kind approved of anyone who drew nearer to God. An old woman touched my sleeve one morning and told me my mother must be proud.\par

Months passed that way. I heard ration prayers, birth blessings, mourning chants, and fragments of the Covenant repeated until repetition became a kind of excavation. Lines that had once seemed ornamental began to catch on hidden hooks in my mind.\par

Va-Sheva found me once in that season, in a corridor near the washing pools, and I was so glad of it that I had answered every question she asked before it occurred to me to wonder why Shield wanted a Candidate's account of who came and went from Faith's chambers. She thanked me. She said I had grown thin, and that I should eat, and that I had always been the quickest of us; and she was gone before the pleasure of it had finished arriving. I spent the remainder of that day composing what I would say to her when next we met. That season kept us apart.\par

The Book of Genesis came often in those months, and once, during the long preparation for the seasonal Festival, in full. Ka-Elun of Faith read it herself that day from beneath the central gravure. Her Keeper's veil fell cleanly around her face, covering the pilgrim scars that had once made me stare. The Hall was full, short of ceremonial-full; even long scripture had to be scheduled against labour. I sat on the northern tier and listened the way a man searches a wall for a seam.\par

She began.\par

The Hall answered with bowed heads. I remained still.\par

--- May I interrupt now? You said before not to interrupt scripture.

--- Yes. That is the whole of the first book of the Covenant, the Book of Genesis. And it is the whole of the Genesis gravure also, which you asked me of on the first morning: those same five days, cut into the southern wall in stone instead of spoken.

--- One moment please, let me fetch a book from the library.

\centerline{[\,---\,]}

--- Here. You cannot read it, for it is in my tongue, English. But let me say it aloud in yours:

\begin{scripture}

In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.
And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters.
And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.
And God saw the light, that it was good: and God divided the light from the darkness.
And God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night. And the evening and the morning were the first day.

\end{scripture}

--- It starts almost exactly the same, but goes on for much longer. How many of these thin pages are there in your book? 

--- More than a thousand.

--- We only have \textit{The Five Books of the Covenant}, and whatever Faith keeps hidden in its chambers. Could you have it translated, just the \textit{Genesis} part? I should like to compare the texts.

--- This, in truth, would be only chapter one of \textit{Genesis}, which contains fifty. That one chapter I could translate myself, and give you when next we meet. Let us continue.

\end{room}

In childhood Genesis had been the first story, and first stories are too large to be questioned, because they arrive before questions. I searched the reading for the Veil's Chamber and found nothing useful.\par

More weeks passed. I learned that scripture keeps its locks however often it is heard; it grows more layered.\par

No one troubled a Candidate who sat through readings with lowered eyes. Faith approved of me; Balance approved of anyone who sat still without consuming resources; even Va-Raedin, if he knew, gave no sign. Perhaps that was the correction.\par

The five lessons stayed inside me like a sequence waiting for a location. The first three I saw everywhere; the Hall was full of them. But turning and threshold return stood outside ordinary prayer. They needed substance, angle, precision: water placed where light had a reason to enter, a beam disciplined enough to be bent, a surface struck at exactly the right condition. The Hall had water in basins and bowls and ritual vessels, and none of it sat in the path of a serious beam.\par

\ornament

Then came the Festival of Equal Light.\par

It fell in the season when day and night were said to stand before Justice and neither could accuse the other of theft. Balance used it to review the stores --- seed, water, births, deaths, labour lost, what could be spared for planting. Faith called it older than accounting: the day on which God's first division of light from darkness was remembered without fear of either.\par

Light prepared the Hall that season in ways I had never been allowed to watch so closely. The upper apertures were cleaned with white cord. The polished inserts in the central gravure were uncovered and wiped with cloths counted before use and after. The lamps were lowered. Three old vents high above the gravure were opened under Justice's witness and marked in the record at once.\par

\ornament

The Hall filled before the Third Measure. I sat on the northern tier, and the tiers packed shoulder to shoulder, and the Hall grew warm with the heat of a thousand bodies long before a lamp was touched. The worker crowded against my side still smelled of the tallow of the shift that had let him go, and shifted from one bare foot to the other through the wait. Ka-Syphiron sat in the eastern alcove, remote and pale, his face unreadable above folded hands. Ka-Elun of Faith stood beneath the central gravure, the baskets of seed and the sealed jars set out before her. Shield held the upper doors. Justice sat with its tablets open.\par

The lamps were dimmed one by one. A murmur passed through the Hall --- anticipation disciplined by rite. Ka-Elun lifted both hands. \par

\begin{scripture}
God who divided the light from the darkness, measure us.
\end{scripture}

The Hall answered. ``Measure us in mercy.''\par

\begin{scripture}
God who set Earth between Heaven and Hell, steady us.
\end{scripture}

``Steady us in shadow.''\par

\begin{scripture}
God who hid the spring beneath the Mountain's hand, keep us.
\end{scripture}

``Keep us from wandering.''\par

The incantation continued, carried in a rhythm that made breath part of the architecture. Faith named the days in their order, down to the five children in the first shadow. I listened only partly.\par

Something in Light's preparations had begun to disturb me. The dimmed lamps. The unsealed upper aperture. The polished fragments inside the gravure. The old vents opened under witness. A ceremony about equal light, held in a Hall built to receive controlled darkness. \par

``\textit{Let there be light,}'' Ka-Elun of Faith said --- speaking the words now rather than reading them, as though the hour answered her voice rather than the turning sky. And at the Third hour, the Measure, the Mountain's noon, the sun entered.\par

At first it was only a pale line high on the wall above the central gravure, so thin I almost missed it. I turned to follow where it fell. It crossed the Hall and touched the carved division between Day and Night on the \textit{pillar of Light}. Ka-Elun of Faith went on speaking. The line brightened. And where it struck the pillar, a mirror I had never once seen took the beam and sent it straight down.\par

My breath stopped. A mirror. It was the \textit{first shadow}.\par

My mind arranged the five lessons so fast it frightened me. Reflection, shadow, focus, turning, threshold return. The Light from Heaven. A fixed beam. A rare day. A public rite. A Festival of Equal Light. Heaven itself had set the angle. Heaven held the source. The path showed itself on the right day alone, however clever the searcher. Months of failure had proved my timing wrong, and nothing more. \par

Unless one waited for the Festival. Unless one chased the beam. Now.\par

Ka-Elun's voice went on above me, solemn and beautiful, and I barely heard it. Va-Maera's warning returned --- \textit{not every brightness belongs to you}. Ka-Syphiron's rule returned --- the Chamber must be found. Va-Raedin's prohibitions returned --- no salt, no vessels, no threshold return outside instruction.\par

The Hall bowed. I stood.\par

I rose before I had decided to rise, and no one near me noticed. The Hall was bowed in prayer, the lamps were dim, and every eye had been trained toward holiness rather than toward one Candidate on the northern tier moving too carefully between bodies. Faith had spent an age teaching them where to put their eyes; Light had spent seven years teaching me to look where nobody had been told to.\par

A Faith attendant met me in the side passage, a covered bowl against her hip.\par

``Candidate?''\par

``Light service,'' I said.\par

The lie was small, and nothing was going to stop me. I was on a clock.\par

\ornament

That mirror had been waiting in the pillar since before my great-grandfathers were named, for a sun to come twice a year and find it. Reflection: the first of the five, handed to me for nothing, in front of three thousand people, by a woman saying \textit{let there be light}.\par

I followed it down.\par

The beam came out at the pillar's foot and fell on the floor.\par

It made rings: a small disc at the centre, and around it ring upon ring of light and dark, close-set, even, going faint at the rim --- the rings a dropped stone leaves on a still pool, and never moving.\par

I had seen that shape before. The place of it escaped me, and the sun was moving, and a man in a hurry will file a miracle for later.\par

Why is there a pattern where there should be a beam? Because something is in the way.\par

Shadow.\par

I looked up. An insert in the ceiling: a thin disc of pale metal on a stem, set square in the beam a little above my head, throwing every ring of it down onto the floor.\par

I took it out of its seat with a thumbnail. It had been made to come out.\par

The rings went out of the world. What lay on the floor was a clean disc of white light with an edge you could have cut bread against.\par

\ornament

The clean disc lay on bare stone and went no further. Then I saw that the stone beneath the disc was a separate piece. The same grey. The same grain. And a seam no wider than a hair, running round it in a circle so true that only a made thing could hold it.\par

It was a lid. It came up far too light for its size, hollowed underneath, and beneath it a shaft went down into the Mountain, and the beam went down with it.\par

There was a stair beside the shaft. I took it, and the dark closed over me. No lamp. No wick. From that moment there was nothing in the world but the beam and what the beam touched.\par

\ornament

The first chamber below was a mirror and a hole and a table.\par

The beam struck a mirror set at the floor, stood up horizontal, crossed the room at the height of my chest, and hit the far wall around a hole a third of the size it needed to be: a broad clean ring on the stone, and a poor middle of it going through into the dark.\par

Focus.\par

On the table lay glass. Discs, bulbs, plates thick at the middle and plates thin at the middle, oil-filled cells, one long crystal bar. Twenty pieces. No marks, no order, no instruction.\par

The first disc gathered the light, crossed it a hand beyond the glass, and threw it out again wider than it began --- a circle the size of my head, and less of it through the hole than with no glass at all. That is what a converging glass does if you let it finish.\par

So I caught the light while it was still opening, with one of the thin-waisted ones that spread, and stood it up straight again.\par

The whole beam went through.\par

\ornament

I opened the door under the hole, went through, and shut it behind me.\par

The beam came through with me, and it had changed. Not one part of it had been thrown away: the same sun, the same quantity, and now the thickness of two fingers instead of a forearm. Where it fell, it fell hard. \par

\ornament

The second chamber turned.\par

A tank stood on a plinth in the middle of the floor. Three-sided: a wedge the width of a man's chest, its point turned toward the right-hand wall. Empty. And in the recess beside it a jar, sealed with green wax, the cup and the sealed jar pressed deep, a black witness line laid across.\par

Turning.\par

Curiosity costs. It is invoiced.\par

I broke the seal and poured. The beam entered the first face and leaned, crossed, came out through the second and leaned again; and two small leanings, taken in the right order, put the light on the right-hand wall a long way from where it had been going.\par

Not far enough.\par

So I scraped the dried mineral off the rim with two fingers and stirred it in. The turn opened; the light walked along the wall and stopped short. Another scrape. It walked again.\par

The beam went into the aperture, square and clean, and turned the corner of the Mountain, and I followed it.\par

\ornament

A mirror threw the beam straight down. A second, set in the floor, threw it straight back up, into the underside of a long tank of liquid --- and was reflected down by the surface of the water.\par

Threshold return.\par

I knew this. This was the trough, and the way out of it was to thin the liquid, and move the threshold.\par

So I remixed.\par

It has no hinge. There is no moment of the light \textit{beginning} to leave. Below the condition the surface is a mirror and gives you nothing --- no glimmer. It is perfectly closed. And then it is open, and the light is already out, and out flat, running along the top of the water so nearly level with it that it is barely a departure at all.\par

I kept stirring, thinning it, and the beam rose, and rose, and struck a mirror on the far wall that I had never known was there.\par

And that mirror threw it to another. And that one to another.\par

I got up off my knees.\par

They came out of the dark one after another as the light found them, five, six, high and low, across the whole width of that room, each lit for the half-breath it took to hand the beam on --- and the room drew itself out of nothing in the time it takes to say it, and the last of them put the light through an aperture in the left-hand wall.\par

I put a finger through the left-hand aperture, it clicked, and a door opened.\par

\ornament

And there it was. The Veil's Chamber. I had found it.\par

It was low and cold and there was nothing holy in it. No altar. No carved angel. No crown of lamps. No window onto Heaven. Walls smoothed by hand and never polished, and a floor worn in one path from the door.\par

The beam came in from the aperture and crossed the room and put its clean two-finger disc on the far wall.\par

And the far wall was carved.\par

Cut into the stone and filled with white paste, in a circle wider than my two arms: a small disc at the centre, and around the disc, ring upon ring, close-set, even, spreading out and going faint toward the rim.\par

The beam's disc sat exactly in the middle of it. Exactly. The centre of the light on the centre of the carving, and all those rings around it holding nothing at all, empty stone, a pattern with no light in it and no reason to exist.\par

I stood in front of that wall for a long time.\par

I had seen it on the floor of the passage upstairs an hour ago and filed it for later.\par

I had carried it against my ribs on the token for seven years.\par

And there was a thing between me and the wall that I had passed over, because it was plain and I had come there for grandeur: a stem rising out of the floor to the height of my chest, and on the stem a shallow cradle of pale metal, empty, set directly under the beam's path.\par

It puzzled me. It held nothing. It was a socket, waiting, and the whole room had been built around it, and it was the only object in the Veil's Chamber of Light.\par

\ornament

``You found it.''\par

He was in the doorway.\par

How long he had been there, I never learned. His robe had dust at the shoulder. He was breathing harder than I had heard him breathe in seven years, and his eyes stayed on the wall, the way a man looks at a thing he has decided to stop fearing.\par

``Va-Raedin---''\par

``Be quiet a moment. There is no time. I am going to say something to you badly, and I would rather say it badly than not at all.''\par

He came and stood beside me.\par

``This is going to be one of the important hours of your life. You should know that while it is happening, because almost no one ever does.'' He was still looking at the wall. ``You have come down a road that took me nine days to find and cost you a morning, and you did it cleanly, and you are standing there thinking that you have finally arrived at the bottom of Light. That you understand it now. Mirrors, shadow, focus, turning, threshold. The five. You think the five is the floor.''\par

``Veil---''\par

``Nothing prepares you for the next part. Nothing. I was not prepared, and I had a better teacher than you have, and I was older than you are. It has been a decade and I am not over it. I do not expect to get over it.'' Then he turned his head and looked at me, and I have thought about that look every year since. ``Are you ready?''\par

``I do not know.''\par

``Good. That is the only honest answer, and it will not help you.'' He nodded at the stem. ``The sun is going. You have perhaps forty breaths. Before the beam goes out --- put your token on the cradle.''\par

I had it in my fist. I had been holding it since the stair without knowing.\par

I put it in.\par

It fitted the way a bone fits a socket.\par

The wall came alive.\par

The beam went through the rings of the token and came out the other side and \textit{opened}. In order. On purpose. Into a disc and a ring and a dark and a ring and a dark, spreading out across the stone, precise as an argument, and every ring of light lay down inside a ring that had been cut into that wall by a hand that has been dust for longer than my people have had a name.\par

Ring for ring.\par

Dark for dark.\par

Out to the rim.\par

It matched. There was no gap anywhere in it wide enough to lay a hair in. Somebody had known --- exactly, to the width of a hair, in a room with no light in it --- what that piece of metal would do to a beam of the sun on a day two thousand years after they were buried, and they had cut the answer into the wall and gone away.\par

Of sitting down I remember nothing. I remember the floor being cold.\par

Because I knew what a shadow was. A shadow is the shape of a thing that stands in the light. I had taken the disc out of the ceiling an hour before and the rings had gone out of the world with it, and I had thought: \textit{a thing stood in the beam and threw a shadow, and the shadow had rings in it.} And I had let that sentence go past me, because I was in a hurry, and because it is an absurd sentence, and because there is no room in a straight line for rings.\par

Everything I had ever been taught forbade this.\par

A line has a position. That is the whole use of a line --- it is why the Mirror works, it is why two hundred and fifty-six of them can tell an old man which passage a body crossed at which hour. Everything I had been taught, every lesson, every room, every correction, every proud small triumph of seven years, stood on light being a thing that goes from one place to another place and can be told where to be.\par

And here was the sun, coming through a hole and around an edge, and lying down on a wall in rings.\par

Like water.\par

I had the token wrong from the day it was put in my hand, and every man in my office had it wrong with me, and the ones who had come down here and seen this had kept their mouths shut, because their oath required it.\par

It was a picture of \textit{light}, and light does what water does.\par

``There it is,'' Va-Raedin said, very quietly, somewhere above me. ``Yes. That is the face people make.''\par

The beam went out while I was still on the floor.\par

There was no fading; the sun simply moved off the pillar seven floors above us and the room shut. He had a lamp, and lit it, and the little yellow flame made the carved wall look like a decoration again, which I found obscene.\par

He took the token out of the cradle himself and put it into my hand and closed my fingers on it.\par

``Now listen, because we have very little time and you are of no use to anybody in this condition.''\par

``It spreads,'' I said. ``When it is narrowed. It spreads.''\par

``Yes.''\par

``Two openings would cross. They would cross like the water in the bowl. There would be places with nothing---''\par

``Yes.''\par

``Behind an obstacle---''\par

``\textit{Yes.}'' It came out of him like a door slamming. Then, level again: ``And you will not test one word of it.''\par

I looked up at him.\par

``You will not touch the cradle again. You will not scrape the paste out of that wall. You will not reproduce any part of this in a washing bowl, a jar, a cup, a channel, or a puddle in the goat pen. You will not ask Balance for mineral water. You will not ask Faith whether their singing crosses. You will not ask Shield what stands behind a barrier. You will not ask Justice whether two causes may cancel one another. Do you hear the shape of the prohibition?''\par

I heard it.\par

Every other Kahir in the Mountain had just become dangerous by relation. Balance water. Faith sound. Shield barriers. Justice causes. Light waves. The Chamber was Light standing in the middle of the Hall and pointing at every other charge in the building and saying: \textit{that too, and that too, and that too.}\par

``Yes,'' I said.\par

``No.''\par

``\ldots{} I agree.''\par

``Better.''\par

He was already moving --- resetting something at the base of the stem, quick and practised and without one moment of reverence, which after what I had just seen felt like a man wiping his boots on scripture. He sealed the Balance jar with new wax out of a fold in his sleeve. On the way up he put the disc back into its seat in the ceiling of the passage with his thumb, in the dark, without looking at it, and the rings came back onto the floor behind us as we walked away, and he kept his eyes front.\par

``You have done this before.''\par

``I found the Chamber before you.''\par

``That is not what I meant.''\par

``It is the answer you receive.''\par

The way out ran elsewhere. He opened a service way behind the third chamber and walked me up a stair that had lamps in it, and handrails, and a smell of cold metal and old water, and I understood that the road I had bled down had a corridor running beside it the whole way, for the people entitled to know it.\par

Discovery had felt like a door. Permission turned out to be a passage built long before my courage.\par

At the foot of the stair he stopped. Above us the Festival was giving its last answer and three thousand people were getting to their feet, and the floor of their holiest hour had stood open all afternoon with nobody the wiser.\par

``You will say nothing.''\par

``To whom?''\par

``To anyone.''\par

``Ka-Syphiron---''\par

``I will speak to Ka-Syphiron.''\par

``It is mine,'' I said. ``I found it. I took the disc out of the ceiling and I built the glass and I raised the light out of the water with my own hands, and you were not there for any of it. Let me tell him. Let me be the one who---''\par

I stopped, because I could hear it.\par

``Finish the sentence,'' Va-Raedin said. ``No? Then I will. \textit{Let me be the one who is seen.}''\par

The wall of the passage was very close and very cold.\par

``You have had the best hour of your life,'' he said, ``and you have spent the last minute of it asking to be congratulated. That is not a small fault, Sa-Kailan. That is the fault. Everything they will one day do to you will come in through that door, and it will not even have to knock.''\par

``And what am I now?''\par

``Wait.''\par

``For how long?''\par

``Until the Chamber's existence no longer belongs to your appetite.''\par

He climbed two steps and stopped, with his back to me.\par

``A Candidate learns where light falls,'' he said.\par

``A Veil learns who placed it,'' I answered, because I had been saying it since before I could reason.\par

``No. That is the sentence boys repeat before they know the price of it. A Veil learns that the path he found was laid before he was born, and that finding it binds him before it honours him. That door opens once, and only toward you. You are carrying it now, named for it or not. There is no Candidate left for you to go back to being.''\par

He went up into the noise.\par

I spent the day after the Veil's Chamber doing exactly what I had been told to do.\par

I reported to the eastern practice halls at the appointed hour and was given no lesson. A senior attendant set me to copy lens inventories in a side alcove where no one else worked. At second meal I sat with the other candidates, though the talk around me thinned whenever my hand moved too near my cup. No one asked where I had been the day before. No one failed to know. The Mountain had ways of becoming silent around a fact before the fact was spoken.\par

I waited --- for correction, explanation, punishment, elevation, dismissal, anything that would take the thing I had done and make it a sequence. None came. Men and women of Light crossed my path and adjusted by nearly nothing: a pause too short to name, a glance at my hands, a slight lowering of the voice after I had passed. Once, near the mirror stores, two attendants stopped speaking when they saw me. They looked as though I had arrived before the proper line had been read.\par

By evening I had learned that waiting could be sharper than examination.\par

The summons came after the lamps had been reduced.\par

A girl no older than twelve stood at the entrance to the candidate quarters with a small black token on her open palm. It bore no name, only the sign of Light cut through a circle so narrow that the aperture seemed almost a wound.\par

``Sa-Kailan of Light,'' she said. ``You are expected.''\par

``Where?''\par

``Behind the old gallery.'' Then she closed her fingers over the token, turned, and left.\par

Behind the old gallery.\par

The place was older than the polished eastern halls, and less obedient to the official dignity of Light. Mirrors hung where no teaching arrangement required them; some were cracked and kept anyway. One passage held a copper frame that had been half-melted and then mounted on the wall, as if failure, properly preserved, became instruction. The lamps were fewer here, and stranger: one burned too blue, another breathed irregularly behind a hood of beaten tin.\par

The gallery itself was long and low. Generations of lamp smoke had climbed the walls and stayed, and the ceiling bore old stains in the shape of experiments no one had admitted aloud. Sketches had been pinned straight into the stone with copper nails: lens paths, impossible staircases, circles cut by straight lines, and three drawings of a goat viewed through increasingly ridiculous arrangements of glass.\par

On a shelf near the end, a row of cracked lenses rested beside a sign written in an old hand:\par

\begin{scripture}
DO NOT DISCARD.
FAILURE STILL BENDS LIGHT.
\end{scripture}

The girl stopped before a narrow door half-hidden behind a hanging cloth so stained with soot that it had ceased pretending to be grey.\par

She knocked once and withdrew before anyone answered.\par

``Come in, Sa-Kailan,'' Ka-Syphiron called from inside.\par
