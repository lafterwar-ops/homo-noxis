\typesetchapter{Chapter 6}{Festival}

\noindent You want to know when I stopped waiting to be given the way in and decided to take it. It was not the night over the basin, Paul, and it was not the morning of the five shadows. Those only taught me to see. This is the season after --- the long one, when I had been forbidden to touch what I had learned and could not stop looking at it, and two of the most powerful people in the Mountain took me into small rooms and told me, each in their own grammar, exactly how far above myself I had climbed. Then the Mountain waited until I had been humiliated enough to be dangerous, and on one measured day it opened a door in front of a thousand bowed heads and let me watch it close. I have spent the years since trying to make men like you understand that I did not break in. I was shown the way, once, on purpose, by a house that was certain I would never dare to follow.\par

\ornament

\noindent I could not stop arranging the five lessons. Reflection, focus, shadow, turning, threshold return --- I carried them everywhere, the way a man in the dark touches stones to remember where the wall begins. The order felt like a path. That was the danger, and knowing it was the danger did not help me.\par

For the first days after Va-Raedin ended the exercise I tried to obey him. I did not speak of salted water to another Candidate. I did not ask Balance for salt outside assignment. I did not attempt threshold return in basin, cup, washing pool, oil jar, channel, or any other vessel whose ordinary use had begun to seem beneath its possible one. But obedience of the hands is not obedience of the eye. Every wet surface became an invitation, every lamp set near water an insult. I watched light enter clear water and come back bent, move through oil and thicken, break along the curved jars in the kitchen stores, run flat through the measured channels below Balance's rooms where water moved under stone lips for flow and not for sight. Most of it taught me nothing.\par

That too was information. The Mountain did not waste complexity; if a thing had been made difficult, it was difficult because someone had needed it difficult. Threshold return did not belong to ordinary bowls. Turning did not arrive because a boy leaned over a basin and wanted revelation. The rooms of Light had required a fixed source, a fixed mark, a fixed angle, a fixed substance. Precision was not the atmosphere of the secret. It was the condition under which the secret could exist at all.\par

Weeks passed. The lessons did not continue. The season did, and instruction became ordinary again, which I found almost insulting. Lamps were cleaned, mirrors adjusted, wicks trimmed, brackets inspected, old smoke scraped from the high vents. Twice I spent a whole morning learning how an unclean mirror could betray a sealed door by scattering what it should have returned whole.\par

``Boredom is useful,'' Va-Raedin said on the third such morning.\par

``To whom?''\par

``To anyone who mistakes amazement for discipline.''\par

I accepted the correction and hated accepting it.\par

When no lesson was given, I searched where searching could be disguised as service. I studied the great gravure above the dais until it blurred --- Adam, Eve, the five children, the Mountain, the Oasis, the forest, the rivers, the desert Threshold. I watched how the lamps struck the carved hand of God differently at morning and at evening. I counted where shadows gathered and failed to move. I stood in the public tiers and tried to decide whether any line from the Light alcove might pass through Faith's bronze rail and down into the lower stone. The geometry refused me.\par

I searched the public routes from the eastern halls to the ceremony floor. I watched the maintenance crews change lamps before dawn and after the evening work, and I learned that the Mountain's light was less mysterious seen from a ladder and more mysterious afterward. A miracle could become a bracket, and a bracket a hidden bracket, and a hidden bracket a question, and a question a wall.\par

I tried three times to follow Va-Raedin, and failed three times, each worse than the last. The first time he stopped without turning and told me I was making noise with my left sandal. The second, I trailed a glimpse of pale robe through two corridors and discovered, after twenty breathless minutes, that I had been stalking a hanging cloth left near a vent to dry. The third cured me. I waited near the old Light gallery where I had seen the five markings, certain he would return there after evening instruction, and held still until my legs ached and I was proud of my patience. Then a junior attendant walked in, handed me a folded note, and left without a word. The note read: \textit{A hidden watcher should not cast a visible shadow.} It was not signed. After that I stopped following Va-Raedin.\par

\ornament

\noindent He has been telling me how he tried to follow his teacher through the corridors, and failed each time, and was handed a note that said a hidden watcher should cast no visible shadow. He offers it as a boy's clumsiness. I let it be that.\par

It is a good rule. I have kept a version of it all my working life, and I keep it now, in this chair, with the reels turning between us where he can see them and has chosen not to. He is the finest watcher I have met, and he does not know he is being watched --- which is not his failing but his training. They taught him to look for the hand behind the light and never once to ask who lit the room he stands in.\par

``You are hard on the boy you were,'' I say. ``He learned the note. He is here, telling it to me, and casts no shadow he can see.'' It is meant kindly and lands kindly, and it is also true; true things cost me nothing when they double as flattery.\par

He almost smiles. ``You listen like a man who has already decided the account matters more than the teller,'' he says.\par

Nearer than he knows, and offered as a compliment. I answer it the house's way. ``The account first,'' I say. ``The teller keeps. Go on --- they had shown you a rule and forbidden you to keep following it. What does a boy do with a forbidden season?''\par

\ornament

Then Ka-Syphiron summoned me.\par

The summons came through Va-Raedin at the end of a morning exercise so plain I had nearly fallen asleep standing. The other Candidates were dismissed; their footsteps withdrew into the eastern passage; Va-Raedin waited until the room had emptied.\par

``Ka-Syphiron will receive you before the second meal.''\par

My stomach tightened. ``For correction?''\par

``If correction is required, you will learn.''\par

``That is not an answer.''\par

``No. It is preparation.''\par

The Keeper of Light received me in a room with no view and almost no object: a low table, an unlit lamp, three polished stones, one chair for him and none for me. He stood when I entered, and that frightened me more than if he had stayed seated.\par

He had the beauty of something preserved past kindness. Age had narrowed him without weakening the impression of design --- white hair, fine hands, a face calm enough to make feeling seem a defect in other people. He wore no ceremonial robe, only pale cloth with a thread of gold at the throat, and he looked more like the Keeper there than he ever had in the Hall, because nothing in the room competed with him for meaning.\par

``Sa-Kailan,'' he said.\par

``Keeper.''\par

``Va-Raedin tells me you are quick.''\par

I did not know whether to accept the word or defend against it. ``I try to learn.''\par

``Trying is common. Learning is less so. Understanding is rarer. Wisdom is rarer still.''\par

``Yes, Keeper.''\par

He gestured to a stone set before him --- not to kneel on, to stand on, a place chosen for me by another man. I stepped onto it.\par

``Tell me what a Veil of Light is.''\par

The answer came easily, and therefore badly. ``A Veil learns who placed the light.''\par

``That is doctrine. I asked what a Veil is.''\par

I hesitated. ``The Keeper's successor.''\par

``Sometimes.''\par

``The one permitted deeper Light knowledge.''\par

``Too vague.''\par

``The one who has crossed beyond Candidate instruction.''\par

``By what act?''\par

I looked at the unlit lamp on the table. My mouth had gone dry. ``By finding the Veil's Chamber.''\par

``Yes.''\par

The plainness of the word went into me like cold water.\par

``The title is not given for cleverness,'' he said. ``Not for age, not for loyalty, not for hunger, not for the admiration of one's teacher, not for the impatience of one's own mind. A Candidate of Light becomes Veil of Light by finding the Veil's Chamber. Until then he is Candidate, whatever he believes he has understood.''\par

I felt the words arrange themselves around me like bars. ``And if he never finds it?''\par

``Then he never becomes Veil.''\par

``Even if the Keeper dies?''\par

``The Mountain does not owe a man the office he failed to reach.''\par

I thought of Va-Raedin. He had found the chamber long ago; of course he had; that was what being Veil meant. But had Ka-Syphiron guided him there, or withheld from him the same mercy now being withheld from me? Had he stood on this stone once, young, trying not to look too hungry?\par

``Does the Chamber teach the next path?'' I asked.\par

His face did not change. ``You are not yet entitled to next paths.''\par

``I only meant---''\par

``I know what you meant. That is why I answered the sentence beneath the one you spoke.''\par

He crossed to the table and touched the unlit lamp. ``You have the flaw of many gifted Candidates. You think a locked door is an insult addressed personally to you. Sometimes a locked door is only a mercy you have not yet earned the right to resent.''\par

I lowered my eyes.\par

``Look at me.'' I looked. ``You are seen,'' he said. ``Not celebrated. Seen. There is a difference. Va-Raedin sees speed in you. I see appetite. Speed may serve Light. Appetite must be disciplined before it convinces itself that hunger is revelation.''\par

The younger brother from the Book of Blood came into my mind without my calling him --- small in body, cunning in heart, watching a strength that others loved until envy entered him as a serpent entereth the grass. The old story had become too useful against me.\par

``You may go,'' he said.\par

And that was all. No clue, no riddle, no test, no mercy hidden in the grammar. Only the rule, set down where I could no longer pretend not to know it: a Candidate of Light became Veil by finding the Veil's Chamber. I have sat in rooms since, Paul, where a man tells you the one law that governs the whole of your life and then declines to tell you how to keep it. They are always the quietest rooms. Ka-Syphiron taught me the shape of that silence long before any of your courts did.\par

\ornament

\noindent ``The quietest rooms,'' he says, and looks at me as though I might be one of them.\par

I am. He has just described the method exactly --- give a man the single law of his life and withhold the keeping of it, and do it softly, in a room with nothing in it to look at but the one who withholds --- and he does not know he has described this one. The fire is the only thing here I did not set for that purpose.\par

He has stopped, though. He is looking past me at the wall beside the fire, where the warmth enters the room from no hearth he can find. Four days in the house, and I have watched the question gather.\par

``There is no flame in that wall,'' he says, ``and the stone is warm. In the Mountain, heat was carried --- water run under the floors, close to the rooms that had earned it.''\par

``Here too,'' I say. It is the cheapest kind of truth and I spend it without counting. ``Water heated below the house, moved through pipes in the walls. No fire near you at all. The whole house kept warm by one you never see and never feed.''\par

He sits with that, and I watch him lay it against his own account --- a blessing with a mechanism, a warmth with a hidden channel. I do not hurry him. A man handed one true wonder guards the next thing he says a little less.\par

``Go on,'' I say. ``You left the Keeper's quiet room and did not become an ordinary man. Where does a forbidden boy look next?''\par

\ornament

For several days I stayed away from the Hall of Ceremony, because I did not want to admit I was going there to search and not to pray. Then I went anyway. The chamber received me with indifference; one restless Candidate did not trouble stone laid down before my great-grandfathers were named.\par

I began with the architecture and failed. Then I turned to scripture --- not because humility had opened me to God, but because scripture remained. Faith had preserved the oldest words. Faith kept chambers Light did not enter, or claimed not to. Faith's rites opened doors no lamp could open.\par

I did not seek Ka-Elun. A Keeper was too high and too newly enthroned. Instead I requested audience with Va-Maera, Veil of Faith, through the public petition line beside the Hall of Ceremony. She was older than Ka-Elun by several years, which made her position strange to anyone outside Faith and ordinary to everyone within it; Faith did not always move by age. It moved by devotion, trial, burden, and kinds of obedience the other Kahir found difficult to measure.\par

She received me in a narrow chamber outside the Hall of Prayers. Not inside it. That distinction was the first answer.\par

The room held no ornament but a covered bowl of water and a hanging cord of five knots. The walls were warm. No lamp showed, yet the light came softly from somewhere above. She stood the whole time. She wore white, a heavier white than the attendants, layered and severe, her hair fully covered, her face unpainted, her eyes dark and steady. I had expected devotion and found administration sharpened by holiness. I had expected softness and found a blade wrapped in linen.\par

``You request access to the Hall of Prayers,'' she said.\par

``Yes, Veil.''\par

``Why?''\par

``To study the Covenant.''\par

``The Covenant is read publicly.''\par

``Not all of it.''\par

``No.''\par

``I am Candidate of Light.''\par

``I see your robe.''\par

``There may be passages concerning Light that are not read in public.''\par

``There are passages concerning many things that are not read in public.''\par

``I seek the Veil's Chamber.''\par

The words sounded too naked the moment they were out. She did not react. ``Then seek it in Light.''\par

``I have. I am.''\par

``Then continue.''\par

I had prepared careful sentences. In the corridors they had seemed subtle. ``Faith works closely with Light,'' I said. ``In ceremony. In mist. In timing. In the way certain signs are made visible. I have seen enough to know that what people call miracle may depend on arrangements not everyone understands.''\par

The air changed. Not visibly, not with any sign --- the room only became suddenly smaller, and I understood that I had misjudged the distance between allusion and threat.\par

She took one step toward me. ``Say that again.''\par

I could not.\par

``Good,'' she said. ``Some doors stay closed because the fool outside them still has one living instinct.''\par

``I did not mean---''\par

``You meant enough.''\par

I swallowed. She did not raise her voice, and that made every sentence worse.\par

``You come to Faith wearing Light's new hunger, and because you have seen mist in a prayer hall, because a lamp and a valve once agreed in front of your clever eyes, you think you stand above the people who kneel. You think you have found a crack in Faith. You have found a crack in yourself.''\par

``I was asking for access,'' I said, and the sentence failed while I was still speaking it.\par

``No. You were suggesting that Faith should fear what you might know. Listen to me carefully, Sa-Kailan of Light. Such suggestions do not endanger Faith first. They endanger you first. If danger follows those words, it will find your door before it reaches mine.''\par

I felt cold under my robe. She came closer.\par

``You have learned small tricks of perception and mistaken them for height. Mist is not Faith. Timing is not Faith. A hidden channel is not Faith. Do you think the sacred is diminished because it consents to pass through matter? Do you think God is disproved by a valve? You are not the first bright child to discover that blessing may have a mechanism. You may be the first foolish enough to speak of it as leverage in a house of prayer.''\par

``I was not threatening.''\par

``No. You were too clumsy to threaten properly. That does not make you innocent.''\par

I lowered my eyes. I could feel my pulse in my throat. Her voice softened, which was worse.\par

``We speak with archangels of obedience and demons of appetite. We keep names that may not be spoken where children sleep. We know prayers for birth, madness, treason, plague, thirst, deformity, despair, and the moment before a condemned man understands that mercy will not interrupt justice. We bless marriages and executions. We comfort mothers whose children have been given to roles that will consume them. We keep fasts that would break your pride before your body. We know punishments your little training rooms have never heard of, and consolations beyond your imagining. And you come to me with mist.''\par

I could not move.\par

``Look at me.'' I looked. ``You understand almost nothing,'' she said. ``That is not your sin. Your sin is that you have begun to suspect it and decided to compensate by becoming dangerous. You are making a chain of mistakes. If you continue, you will not become a god of hidden knowledge. You will become an example, spoken of carefully to other gifted boys.''\par

The terror was not that she would call Shield, or Justice, or Ka-Elun. The terror was that she had made consequence feel larger than any of them. I believed, suddenly and completely, that Faith could destroy me without touching me --- that it could name my hunger wrongly in the right chamber, set my words in the wrong order before Justice, turn my discoveries into contagion and my cleverness into evidence and my ambition into a spiritual deformity. Whether any of that was true did not matter. It was true enough in the room.\par

I folded. ``I withdraw the request,'' I said.\par

``Of course you do.''\par

``I apologise, Veil.''\par

``No. You submit. Apology is what people offer when they still hope to manage the injury.''\par

I could barely speak. ``I submit.''\par

She stepped back. ``You may listen in the Hall of Ceremony like any child, elder, worker, mourner, or sinner. You may hear what is read in public. You may pray where prayer is permitted. You may not enter the Hall of Prayers. You may not request private scripture. You may not speak again of Faith's arrangements unless Faith first asks you what you think you have seen.''\par

``Yes, Veil.''\par

``And Sa-Kailan?''\par

``Yes?''\par

``If you truly seek Light, learn first that not every brightness belongs to you.''\par

She dismissed me with a movement of two fingers.\par

I left that chamber shaking, and hated the shaking only after I had gone three corridors and found that no one had followed. You have asked me more than once, Paul, why I did not simply stop there --- why a boy warned by the Keeper of Light and broken by the Veil of Faith in a single season did not take the warning and become an ordinary man. The honest answer is that Va-Maera taught me the very thing she meant to forbid. She wanted me to learn that a blessing may have a mechanism and remain holy. What I learned was that the holiness and the mechanism could be separated --- that if God could work through a valve, then a man who understood the valve was standing nearer the lever than the people on their knees. She told me I had found a crack in myself. She was right. She only did not know that showing a boy the crack is how you teach him where the wall is thin.\par

\ornament

At first shame walked with me. I imagined Va-Sheva laughing, though I knew she would not. Worst of all I imagined Ka-Syphiron looking at me with calm recognition, as though appetite had behaved exactly as he had predicted. But the Hall was public, and public devotion protected me from suspicion. The tribe approved of anyone who drew nearer to God. An old woman touched my sleeve one morning and told me my mother must be proud. A Faith attendant nodded to me before a water thanksgiving. Children slid aside on the benches, because a Candidate of Light sitting through recitation looked serious rather than desperate. No one asked whether I had come to pray or to search.\par

Months passed that way. I heard ration prayers, birth blessings, judgement remembrances, harvest petitions, mourning chants, water thanks, lamp rites, and fragments of the Covenant repeated until repetition became a kind of excavation. Lines that had once seemed ornamental began to catch on hidden hooks in my mind.\par

The Genesis chapter came often in those months --- sometimes in fragments, sometimes nearly whole, and once, during the long preparation for the seasonal Festival, in full. Ka-Elun read it herself that day from the central dais. Her Keeper's veil fell cleanly around her face, covering the pilgrim scars that had once made me stare. The Hall was full but not ceremonial-full; workers had been released in shifts, children brought in teaching groups, Balance clerks seated with tablets because even long scripture had to be scheduled against labour. I sat on the eastern tier and listened the way a man searches a wall for a seam.\par

She began.\par

The Hall answered not with words but with bowed heads. I remained still.\par

I had heard those lines before, scattered across childhood and ceremony, but never with that much attention, and never with failure sitting so close beside me. In childhood Genesis had been the first story, and first stories are too large to be questioned, because they arrive before questions. Now every line seemed to hold a possible seam. Light divided from darkness. Heaven from Earth, and Earth from Hell. Waters gathered beneath the ground. A spring hidden in shadow. The desert set as Threshold. The eye of Heaven, and the forgetting of God's shadow. Five children, but not yet five charges --- no Blood, no Beast, no Cataclysm, no Covenant named openly in that chapter, only beginnings, only the world arranged before the law had explained itself. A later book could command. A beginning could only imply.\par

I searched the reading for the Veil's Chamber and found too much. The first opened his eyes unto the light; the second drank from the waters; the third laid his hand upon the stone; the fourth took up the blade; the fifth lifted his voice unto Heaven. Light, water, stone, blade, voice. The sequence was no help, because it was everywhere. I needed an occasion.\par

That thought did not arrive at once. It came slowly, through failure. I grew familiar with the Hall, and familiarity did not comfort me. I heard Genesis in pieces, the Book of Blood in fragments, the short water prayers of Balance, the threshold blessings of Shield, the correction hymns of Justice, the lamp petitions of Light, and I learned that scripture does not unlock itself by being heard often. It grows more layered, not less. No one troubled a Candidate who sat through readings with lowered eyes. Faith approved of me; Balance approved of anyone who sat still without consuming resources; Justice saw nothing irregular in attendance; Shield did not care where a boy prayed so long as he stayed where prayer was permitted; even Va-Raedin, if he knew, gave no sign. Perhaps that was the correction. Perhaps the answer did not come because there was no answer to be taken.\par

The five lessons stayed inside me like a sequence waiting for a location. Reflection, focus, shadow, turning, threshold return. The first three I saw everywhere; the Hall was full of them --- the gravure that accepted certain lamps and refused others, bronze rails that caught faces at the edge of sight, shadows in the upper vaults that hid seams and vents and old repairs and once, to my brief excitement, a service niche that led only to a soot chamber. But turning and threshold return did not belong to ordinary prayer. They needed substance, angle, and precision: water placed where light had a reason to enter, a beam disciplined enough to be bent, a surface struck at exactly the right condition. The Hall had water in basins and bowls and ritual vessels, and none of it sat in the path of a serious beam. The Mountain, I began to think, was withholding not the answer but the occasion.\par

Then came the Festival of Equal Light.\par

It fell in the season when day and night were said to stand before Justice and neither could accuse the other of theft. Balance used it to review the stores --- seed, water, births, deaths, illness, labour lost, animals increased, what could be spared for planting. Faith called it older than accounting: the day on which God's first division of light from darkness was remembered without fear of either. Shield treated the gathering as a density problem --- too many bodies in one Hall, too many exits, too much attention turned toward one place. Justice opened its tablets before the first lamp was lowered.\par

Light prepared the Hall that year in ways I had never been allowed to watch so closely. The upper apertures were cleaned with white cord. The polished inserts in the central gravure were uncovered and wiped with cloths counted before use and after. The lamps were not raised but lowered. The bronze lips beneath the carved waters were checked by two attendants and sealed again. Three old vents above the dais were opened under Justice's witness and marked in the record at once.\par

The hidden people did not speak of the heavens the way the wanderers did. But they knew the seasons, because the seasons measured survival. The Festival was not curiosity. It was order.\par

The Hall filled before the second morning silence. I sat on the eastern tier, where I had sat for my Ascension, though I no longer felt like the boy who had stood there with a new bandage and a new name. Va-Raedin stood below with the attendants of Light. Ka-Syphiron sat in the eastern alcove, remote and pale, his face unreadable above folded hands. Ka-Elun stood beneath the central gravure. Balance had set baskets of seed and preserved fruit and sealed jars before the dais; Faith had laid the Genesis bark beside the festival prayers; Shield held the upper doors; Justice sat with its tablets open.\par

The lamps were dimmed one by one. A murmur passed through the Hall --- not fear, but anticipation disciplined by rite. Ka-Elun lifted both hands.\par

\begin{scripture}
“God who divided the light from the darkness, measure us.”
The Hall answered.
\end{scripture}

``Measure us in mercy.''\par

\begin{scripture}
“God who set Earth between Heaven and Hell, steady us.”
\end{scripture}

``Steady us in shadow.''\par

\begin{scripture}
“God who hid the spring beneath the Mountain’s hand, keep us.”
\end{scripture}

``Keep us from wandering.''\par

The incantation continued, not sung exactly, but carried in a rhythm that made breath part of the architecture. Faith named the first day and the second, the waters beneath, the Mountain raised, the Oasis hidden, the forest of plenty, the desert Threshold, Adam and Eve, and the five children in the first shadow. I listened only partly.\par

Something in Light's preparations had begun to disturb me. The dimmed lamps. The unsealed upper aperture. The polished fragments inside the gravure. The old vents opened under witness. A ceremony about equal light, held in a Hall built to receive controlled darkness.\par

Then the sun entered.\par

At first it was only a pale line high above the central gravure, so thin I almost missed it. It touched the carved division between Day and Night. The Hall did not move; Ka-Elun went on speaking. The line brightened. It slid --- not downward exactly, but across the stone at an angle no lamp inside the Hall could have made. It passed over the carved waters beneath the Earth, struck the raised Mountain, and flashed.\par

I stopped breathing.\par

The polished insert threw the light downward through another cut, then toward the lower structure beneath the dais. Dust lit inside it. The carved Oasis shone. The five children came out of the darkness as if they had just been made. The people bowed lower. Some wept. I did not bow.\par

The beam struck, turned, narrowed, and went into the Mountain. Not faded, not scattered --- gone. A path that had not existed yesterday and would not exist tomorrow.\par

My mind arranged the five lessons so fast it frightened me. Reflection, focus, shadow, turning, threshold. But not in a training room, not under an ordinary lamp. A fixed beam. A rare day. A public rite. A Festival of Equal Light. The angle could not be altered, because Heaven itself had set it. The source could not be moved. The path could not be found by searching on the wrong day, however clever the searcher. Months of failure had not proved that nothing existed. They had proved only that I had been looking when the door was not there.\par

Unless one waited for the Festival. Unless one chased the beam now.\par

Ka-Elun's voice went on below me, solemn and beautiful, and I barely heard it. Va-Maera's warning returned --- \textit{not every brightness belongs to you}. Ka-Syphiron's rule returned --- the Chamber must be found. Va-Raedin's prohibitions returned --- no salt, no vessels, no threshold return outside instruction. The beam trembled, still alive in the hidden structure.\par

I rose before I had decided to rise. No one near me noticed at first. The Hall was bowed in prayer, the lamps were dim, every eye had been trained toward holiness and not toward one Candidate on the eastern tier moving too carefully between bodies. I stepped down once, then again, keeping to the shadow between two bench lines. I did not know where the beam went. But for the first time in months, the Mountain had shown me where to begin.\par

You will want me to tell you that I hesitated, Paul --- that somewhere on that stair a boy weighed the Keeper's warning and the Veil's threat and his teacher's prohibitions against the light going into the wall, and chose. I did not choose. That is the part I need you to write down correctly, because the whole Trial would later turn on it, and everyone in that chamber would insist that I had. The truth is smaller and worse. They had spent a season teaching me to see, and forbidding me to touch, and telling me I was seen, and telling me I was nothing, until the only thing left in me that could still move was the part that followed light. I rose the way water finds the lower ground. The Mountain built the slope. I only failed to resist it.\par

\noindent He has brought me to the beam --- the sun let into the Hall on one measured day, striking the carved Mountain and passing into the stone --- and he is proud of it still, the boy who found the door the Mountain kept. I let him have the beam. It is not the beam I came for.\par

``Before the light,'' I say. ``The prayer. You said the voices were carried in a rhythm until breath was part of the building --- a thousand of them, in a hall cut from rock.'' I keep it idle, a servant tidying one more detail for the record. ``Where did the sound gather? Was there a place on the tiers where the voices doubled and came back on you --- and another, close by, where a man might stand in a full Hall and hear almost nothing?''\par

It is one question too many, and I know it before the end of it clears my mouth. A man keeping a record does not ask where the standing wave sits. He asks what was said.\par

He does not answer at once. He looks at me the way he has just described looking at an unclean mirror --- the look of a man who has found a scattering where the light should have come back whole. For one breath I have shown him a shape of my own, and he has seen it.\par

``You ask about the sound,'' he says slowly, ``like a man who already knows the answer and only wants to hear whether I do.''\par

``I have sat under a great many sermons,'' I say --- which is a lie, and a good one, because it is small and grey and asks for no admiration. ``A man learns where the priest stands to be heard. Nothing further.''\par

He lets it go. I watch him decide to let it go --- not because he believes me, but because the account is his to give and he has resolved to keep giving it, trap or no trap. That is the thing in him I did not expect and have not yet set down: he sees the shadow I cast, and speaks into the room anyway.\par

``That is enough for tonight,'' I say. The reels turn. He has handed me a boy who followed a light into a wall, certain none would dare come after him; and without meaning to, in one idle question at the end, he has told me the Hall sings --- that the rock keeps a note. I let the fire go down, and do not write that part where he can see.\par
