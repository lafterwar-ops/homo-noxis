\typesetchapter{Chapter 23}{Build}

We took up the next of the sheets in the beam and read on without stopping to speak, and the measure went on as it had begun, numbered and rhymed and cut to be sung, which is the thing I have never got past: that he set the worst of it down in the metre my people use for morning prayer.\par

\ornament

Paul stopped there, and set the sheet down, and for a moment he could not speak for the shape of the thing. ``He is singing it,'' he said at last. ``He put that in a hymn. Do you understand what I am holding? The founding law of the whole Mountain --- that no one hand may hold the whole --- was a labour-management technique, made into a religion so the division would outlive its reason, and he has set the confession of it to the metre they sing on feast days. There is no philosophy in it anywhere.'' And I said, because it was mine to say and no one else's, ``It is worse than that, Paul. The same wall that stops the workers seeing the whole stops any one Keeper ever firing it alone. He built the division to hide the weapon from the builders, and it turned out to be the only thing that ever kept the weapon safe.''\par

Here Sarah took the sheet from Paul's hands, read that passage again to herself, and put both her hands flat on the cold stone of the machine, and was quiet for a long time. And when she spoke she did not speak to us. ``A few drops of water,'' she said. ``Metered onto the deep fire. Five even blows and a long soft roll. He is describing the thing I heard at Reggane when I was twenty-seven. The exact signature I chased across sixty-six years and two continents and everything I ever destroyed. It was a succession. Somewhere under that desert, in the year of the first French bomb, one of his offices buried a Keeper and raised a new one and proved the covenant held, and I was sitting forty kilometres away with a drum-paper on my knees, and I thought I had discovered something. I had heard his heartbeat.'' She took her hands off the stone. ``He knew one day someone with an ear would hear it and come. He built that risk in on purpose, and I am the ear he knew would come.''\par

Paul had to stop reading here, and it was Va-Sheva who took the sheet from him, Va-Sheva of Shield, and she read the passage about the children in the flat Shield voice, and when she had finished she said only, ``I was one of those children. So were you,'' to me, ``and so was every soul in this Mountain, back and back, a hundred thousand years of children taught that a cage was a sky. And the worst of it is the part he will not let himself lie about even now: that the first generation may have loved them. My mother loved me. She taught me the sky was above me and the cage was the world, and she was wrong, and she had been taught it too, and she loved me while she did it. You cannot even hate it cleanly. He built a prison so loving that the guards and the prisoners are the same people, and every one of them was somebody's beloved child.''\par

\ornament

We were quiet a long time after that, in the cold, and I could not look at Va-Sheva, because she had said \emph{so were you}, and it was true. The child he had built, the one raised to believe the cage was a sky, was me, exactly me, Sa-Kailan of Light, who had knelt around the forbidden centre and felt the holy dread at its edge and called it God. And the strangest thing is that it did not make me feel like a victim. It made me feel that the thing I had been raised inside had a shape, and an outside, and a maker, and could therefore be stood apart from and looked at. You cannot refuse a sky. You can refuse a design. He had left, at the very bottom of it, the one door through which the design could be seen; and I was standing in it; and I understood that the reading would carry me past forgiving him and past hating him, into deciding what a man does when he has been handed the truth about a machine that only he, of all the people alive, has both the eyes to see and the standing to stop.\par

that they had always lived down here.\\
\medskip
Some did not go away at all.\\
\medskip
The account is long. I kept it. It is\\
the worst and longest page I own.\\
I have set it apart from this.\\
I do not ask you read it. Leave it alone.\\
\medskip
Believe me, I could not require\\
it of myself, and, quailing,\\
I did it through others, and looked away.\\
That is the family failing.\\
\medskip
I have no defence.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xli}\\
Then they were gone, one way or another,\\
and the great gates were shut at last\\
against the world --- the total sealing\\
would wait for an age beyond my past.\\
\medskip
Inside there was a people, a few thousand:\\
the kept seed, the raised children, and\\
the ageing actors. On the first morning\\
of the sealed world, they woke to their land,\\
\medskip
and did their offices, and said their prayers,\\
and knelt around the forbidden ground.\\
The children born down there did not know\\
a world had been, or that one could be found,\\
\medskip
and would teach their own children so.\\
I stood among them where I stood,\\
an old man who had done the thing,\\
and watched the lie draw breath. It was good.\\
\medskip
God forgive me, it was the most\\
beautiful thing I have ever seen:\\
a whole world, on the first morning\\
of its life, believing it had always been.\\
\medskip
I knew that it would hold.\\
Not one of them would ever thank me.\\
Not one would ever know that I was real.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xlii}\\
I am closing the record. I have said it all.\\
I set it here beneath the god\\
that I invented, in the one hand\\
I trusted, for the one reader\\
\medskip
I could not build the system to prevent.\\
Judge me. You are the first in an age\\
with eyes to do it, and I made sure\\
that you would be a heretic,\\
\medskip
because I could not bear to be\\
judged by anybody who believed.\\
Do the thing that I could not.\\
Look at the whole. Say what it is for.\\
\medskip
I was Noxius Zero. I was first\\
of us, and maker of you all,\\
and I was a man whose father died\\
by the outside, through the woman he loved.\\
\medskip
I chose certainty. Given my century,\\
my evidence, my dead, a man\\
chooses certainty; and if you would not,\\
then nobody ever set the choice\\
\medskip
in front of you, and you should be\\
slower than you are going to be\\
with your verdict.\\
\end{scripture}

Paul turned the last sheet, and there were no more, and the equinox beam had gone off the wall entirely while we read, and the Archive was lit only by our own lamps now, the borrowed sun withdrawn, the machine turning on in the dark as it had turned for an eternity and would turn whether we lived or died. And I found that I was weeping, which I had not done since a cell in a country I could not find, and that I could not have said whether I wept for the drowned workers, or for the lied-to children, or for Ka-Elun under her roof, or for the whole ended world above us, or for a woman named Ithe, an eternity dead, who was used by an enemy and accused by the man who loved her and asked, by neither of them, what she had truly known, or for myself, who had read my own possible life to the end of it in another man's hand and found that the only difference between us was a fact-checker and a machine and one night's kindness in the desert. I think I wept for all of it. I think, by then, it was all one thing.\par
