\typesetchapter{Chapter 23}{Build}

We took up the next of the sheets in the beam and read on without stopping to speak, and the measure went on as it had begun, numbered and cadenced and cut to be chanted, which is the thing I have never got past: that he set the worst of it down in the versicles my people use for morning prayer.\par

\ornament

\begin{scripture}
\textbf{xv}\\
We found it in the third year out. I knew it before my engineers did. I had stopped hunting the ground that men want, and I hunted the ground that no one goes near.\\
It offered nothing. Hear that first: its nothing was its first defence. No green, no game, no road, no reason --- a shoulder of old stone, and distance.\\
It lay in a waste that kills by width alone, a country that has nothing and gives nothing to the warm folk wanting south, who would not turn their heads toward it in a hundred lifetimes.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xvi}\\
Then, under the nothing, season upon season, my water-men laid their rods against the stone, and one by one it gave them up --- each one a prayer whose words I had not known until the thing itself was granted me.\\
Water; so much of it that my chief sat down upon the stone and put his face into his hands and made no sound.\\
More than a siege could drink. Enough to hold a whole sealed people. And more --- though it was years before I let myself know what the water was for.\\
And heat beneath it, the old heat, shallow enough to reach and to hold, a fire that never wants for feeding, so that the sealed shall not be cold.\\
And ways: faults, and voids, and the promise of more that could be cut and made, until a whole world might be threaded down into the rock and laid there.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xvii}\\
Understand the joy of it, before the rest. Later I called it grief, and strife, a burden taken up in sorrow. It was the best year of my life.\\
Three years of a road that killed my people and told me hourly that I was mad, hunting a thing my own men swore was nowhere on the earth --- and I had it, and I was glad past anything I had dared to want.\\
We laughed like boys in the deep shade, and every rod we sank said yes to us. A man who finds that the world has laid its tools out ready to his hand feels \emph{chosen}.\\
Nothing in my life since has equalled it. That is the whole of what I will say in my own defence.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xviii}\\
I saw the other thing that year. I cannot tell you --- and it is true that I have asked it of myself --- whether I sought that place because I already knew, or whether it was accident that damned them all.\\
The deep fire never dies. Water flashed upon it turns to steam; drive that steam down a throat that runs through the roots of a world, and what you hold is a hand laid on the world's own heart.\\
He told me, innocent, at the bottom of the shaft, what pressures he could raise. That was the start of it.\\
The ground had offered me my people's living, and it offered, in the one same stone, the means to do to all the world what the world was doing to my own --- and worse, and all at once, and hidden.\\
I told myself, that year, that the hand was only ever a defence.\\
\end{scripture}

\ornament

\begin{scripture}
\textbf{xix}\\
Do not take the building for a wonder. They who came after me made it out to be God's own masonry, and that is the first and the largest lie. It was labour. It was me.\\
The most enormous labour ever gathered into one place, and done by people --- tens of thousands of them, brought down that killing road one by one, across the better part of my one life.\\
It cost. I paid the cost in the one coin such things are ever bought with: the lives of other people, lost.\\
I kept the account. The account is long.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xx}\\
We learned by failing; set that, too, against the marble they have made. The first press, built to prove the hand, killed the men who built it.\\
They paid for what no man yet knew: what water does when it flashes in a shut place. I stood at the gallery's mouth and watched them carry out the rest, and I kept my face still.\\
I did not stop the work. I corrected the drawings. A stair cut too near a fault took a hundred in one hour; a wall let go in the night, and a whole shift was drowned, and it set us back ten years.\\
We did not learn the rate at which to meter the water down until men had died of it; and I did not wait. Every increment was purchased. I did not flinch. I had learned upon the road how to spend the parts.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxi}\\
You will want a number. I have one. I kept the count, and I kept it true; that is the one virtue I can claim with no lie folded through it.\\
More than forty thousand came down that road while I drew breath. Fewer than a third of them ever went up again. Do the subtraction. It is a kind of death:\\
twenty-seven thousand stayed inside the rock they cut --- in flood, in fall, in steam, in the slow grinding waste of a country that wanted nobody alive at all.\\
I built the greatest refuge ever built, a thing to keep my people whole, and I built it on a floor of their bones. I did not stop. I did not slow.\\
When a chief came to me white in the face with word of a fresh hundred, I asked him first whether the work they had died upon had held. That question was the worst thing in me.\\
I watched the hour he stopped loving me, and I let him stop. A man who spends the parts cannot afford the love of those who count them. There it ends.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxii}\\
Then I did the thing that let them do such work, to such an end, and never rise against it. Here is the seed: they did not, and they could not, comprehend.\\
I divided up the knowledge. No team was given more than its one piece. The water-men knew water, and believed they were sinking a well against a siege.\\
The light-men knew their bending surfaces, and thought they were bringing the sun downstairs to a hidden people. Those who cut the deep throat thought the work was theirs alone, and drainage all that it was for.\\
Those who raised the gates believed they raised them against armies. Not one team in a hundred ever held two pieces.\\
Only my own knew that the well, the sun-ways, the drainage, and the gates were one machine; and that the machine was a weapon aimed through the core of the Earth. It waits.\\
Hear the reason, and hear it exactly. I divided it against no enemy outside. I divided it to keep the work safe from the very hands inside:\\
so that no man would ever hold enough of it at once to look at the thing he had made, and set it down, and refuse. I let that harden. And it took.\\
It became the soul of all that followed.\\
\end{scripture}

\ornament

Paul stopped there, and set the sheet down, and for a moment he could not speak for the shape of the thing. ``He is chanting it,'' he said at last. ``He put this in the office. Do you understand what I am holding? The founding law of the whole Mountain --- that no one hand may hold the whole --- was a labour-management technique, made into a religion so the division would outlive its reason, and he has set the confession of it to the versicles they chant on feast days. There is no philosophy in it anywhere.'' And I said, because it was mine to say and no one else's, ``It is worse than that, Paul. The same wall that stops the workers seeing the whole stops any one Keeper ever firing it alone. He built the division to hide the weapon from the builders, and it turned out to be the only thing that ever kept the weapon safe.''\par

\ornament

\begin{scripture}
\textbf{xxiii}\\
Five great works, and so five teams, and over each I set a head. The whole lay divided among the five, never gathered, never spoken.\\
I could see even then that I would die, and that the thing must run without me, longer than a name is kept. So, before I was done,\\
I did the thing that turns a work into a people. I stopped calling the teams teams. I began to make them lines.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxiv}\\
Water and stone and heat and measure, the material world and the keeping of its accounts --- that team I taught to hand itself down from master to child, in small amounts,\\
with signs, and pride, and jealous keeping, until the work it did became \emph{Balance}: a name, a blood, a calling, and no man in it knowing whence it came.\\
Those who listened for the deep return, and knew the timing of the strokes, I made the keepers of the holy hour, and taught them that it was God who spoke inside the stone. \emph{Faith.}\\
The light-men, who would set the shot and hold the aim, I made the keepers of a mystery of alignment, and I gave it a name: \emph{Light.}\\
Those who held my books and my permissions, who alone might say that a thing may pass, and who kept the order of each act that cannot be recalled --- \emph{Justice}, at the last.\\
And the men of gates and containment, of secrecy and, at need, of violence, I named \emph{Shield}, and it was done. They never felt it. They kept silence.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxv}\\
I gave them nothing new to do. I took the doing that they already had, and I wrapped it round in inheritance, in pride, in mystery, in a little dread.\\
There is one way, and only one, to make a man repeat himself unchanged for a thousand years: you must convince him that the task is a self.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxvi}\\
To each line I gave a discipline to hide its true work inside. And the discipline grew a real knowledge, freely taught, deep and absorbing and entirely true,\\
laid down beside the secret, so that a clever child of the line might spend a brilliant life upon the cover and never once fall through to the end of it.\\
To Balance, honest measure; so that the finest engineer of his age might live and die inside his craft and never see the weapon on the page.\\
To Faith, the true art of the stone, of rhythm and resonance and return --- and I called it prayer, so that the best ear born in a hundred years would learn to spend itself on the hearing of God, and never know that it had heard a weapon breathe.\\
To Light, the bending of a wave down to a single point; and holy was the sheath. To Justice, the record, the sequence, the weighing of whether a thing may lawfully proceed; and folded into it, out of sight, the permissions that a firing would require.\\
Truths, all of it. I gave them truths, arranged in a ring about a hole, and I taught them that the hole was God.\\
And at its very lip I taught the soul to feel a particular holy dread, which I built into the teaching for that purpose and no other: so that a curious mind would flinch back before the one question, at the exact hour it grew fit to ask it.\\
I taught them that flinch. They have flinched a hundred thousand years. The mask has never once slipped. Not one of them has ever known that he was doing it.\\
Of all the things in all this Mountain, that is the piece I am proudest of.\\
\end{scripture}

\ornament

\begin{scripture}
\textbf{xxvii}\\
Then I did the thing that beats beneath the whole of what you have read. I did it for a reason that was sound. That is the worst thing I have said.\\
The machine had one flaw, and it was the flaw of all my kind: it must be kept by persons; and a person changes, and a person turns to dust.\\
Each generation the five hands would fail, and new hands would take their place; and a new hand is an untried hand. A Keeper might know only the face of the rite that hid the engine, hold his office forty years, and never know whether, at the hour of need, he could do the thing he was made for.\\
And so: call five such hands a throne; call them a weapon; give them forty years, and they are five frightened priests who have forgotten they were ever engineers, standing above an engine that not one of them has ever run.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxviii}\\
So I built a test, and I hid the test inside a devotion, and I made it law. At each succession, when one Keeper went down and a new one rose, the people saw only the five gather, secret and apart, to do the thing I had dressed for them as prayer: the renewing of the covenant.\\
Under the prayer, a firing. I put it there. A small one --- large enough to prove that the engine answered when it was told, small enough to leave the world unhurt, and proof that the five hands could still hold.\\
A few drops metered onto the fire, five times, and then the long wait that is due; for the machine knows one measure only, and a test struck off its measure proves nothing true.\\
And the world, struck five times in its own slow interval, would answer back one wait behind the fifth; and that returning note would climb the track of the deep stone to the listening ear, and say that the covenant held, and that the thing still lived.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxix}\\
I made it law. I made it secret. I made it holy --- all three, so that frightened, faithful folk would keep it perfectly, and never let it slip past the last of them who could have said what the five strokes were ever for.\\
I called it the readiness. I told them: God confirms the Keeper, and nothing more.\\
It was the hour my machine confirmed itself.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxx}\\
The first time, in my own lifetime, to prove it, I stood in the Hall built for that toll and heard the Earth give back the shape of it: five even blows, and a long soft roll behind them, like a struck world turning over once inside its sleep.\\
The most beautiful and the worst thing I ever made, and mine to keep. I knew that I had given my machine a heartbeat --- one beat to a generation --- that would go on sounding under the sand as long as the Mountain held its station.\\
And anyone whose ear was fine enough might, one day, hear that heart beating down there in the ground, and come looking for the body. I built that in, with care.\\
\end{scripture}

\ornament

Here Sarah took the sheet from Paul's hands, read that passage again to herself, and put both her hands flat on the cold stone of the machine, and was quiet for a long time. And when she spoke she did not speak to us. ``A few drops of water,'' she said. ``Metered onto the deep fire. Five even blows and a long soft roll. He is describing the thing I heard at Reggane when I was twenty-seven. The exact signature I chased across sixty-six years and two continents and everything I ever destroyed. It was a succession. Somewhere under that desert, in the year of the first French bomb, one of his offices buried a Keeper and raised a new one and proved the covenant held, and I was sitting forty kilometres away with a drum-paper on my knees, and I thought I had discovered something. I had heard his heartbeat.'' She took her hands off the stone. ``He knew one day someone with an ear would hear it and come. He built that risk in on purpose, and I am the ear he knew would come.''\par

\ornament

\begin{scripture}
\textbf{xxxi}\\
The machine and the offices were the easy part. Here is the hard part, the thing no later people could ever invent, being themselves its offspring.\\
A machine in trained hands lasts some few lives. It cannot last an age. An age on, the trained hands have forgotten what the training was ever for, and it is gone.\\
A people that has lost the why of an exact thing will, at the last, stop doing it exactly. And then the machine is dead, and all that I spent my life upon is past.\\
I had not paid my father's world, and forty thousand in the rock, to build a thing that ran six lives and failed. I meant to stop the clock.\\
There is one method. Only one. I built a people to be the keeping. I built them so that they could not stop.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxxii}\\
I took children. I have said it once; I say it again, and let it stand. Some were ours, the kept seed; some I bought; some I gathered off the failing land.\\
I raised them inside a thing that was not so: that the Mountain had always been, where I had built it; that the offices were holy, where I had written down a duty and a roster; and that the engine was a god, a covenant, the very order of the world, where I had made a weapon. That is what it all meant.\\
I wrote them a past, and I aged it well, so that the newest child would find, wherever he turned, more evidence that this was old past all counting, and would never doubt that his own life was a late leaf on a tree past reckoning.\\
I set among the first generation actors who knew the whole of it, and who yet agreed, for love or fear or promise, to spend their lives pretending faith; so that the second generation, watching them, believed without pretending, and came to faith;\\
and in the third, no pretence lived at all: they simply knew it, the way you know the sky.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxxiii}\\
I carved the Genesis myself, in the worst nights of the build. The aim was plain: the outside had to be a horror. I am not proud of how easily it came to me.\\
So that no child would ever want it, I took the true thing and I turned it one quarter --- which is the only turn a good lie ever needs --- as I had learned to do.\\
The true thing was a murder. Keep the murder: a real thing is what anchors a lie. But set it in the first of the ages, and make the dead man a brother, standing beloved and envied in a father's place;\\
and let the killer's line be assigned the open land, cursed and many, hating forever the shade they cannot find.\\
I gave them a woman also: taken as spoil when the murderer fled, blameless as a stolen lamp; a thing done to us, and never done with her. So I said.\\
That much I could cut into the stone. What I filed away, and kept within, and never let come near the marble, was the truth that lay beneath her: that the knife went in by a hand I loved, and that I chose, from that hour onward, never to ask that hand what it had known.\\
A people can be taught to dread a born murderer. No people yet was taught to dread a wound that a man had brought half upon himself. So I let that part go.\\
I let her stand in the story as pure loss, and I drowned the question of her choosing in my father's grave, in the very same ground.\\
I have watched Keepers read it out to trembling boys as the world's own word, the deep truth of everything; and every time, beneath the marble of it, I have heard the one question I never put to her.\\
I did it in a single evening. It has worked better than anything else I ever built.\\
\end{scripture}

\ornament

Paul had to stop reading here, and it was Va-Sheva who took the sheet from him, Va-Sheva of Shield, and she read the passage about the children in the flat Shield voice, and when she had finished she said only, ``I was one of those children. So were you,'' to me, ``and so was every soul in this Mountain, back and back, a hundred thousand years of children taught that a cage was a sky. And the worst of it is the part he will not let himself lie about even now: that the first generation may have loved them. My mother loved me. She taught me the sky was above me and the cage was the world, and she was wrong, and she had been taught it too, and she loved me while she did it. You cannot even hate it cleanly. He built a prison so loving that the guards and the prisoners are the same people, and every one of them was somebody's beloved child.''\par

\ornament

We were quiet a long time after that, in the cold, and I could not look at Va-Sheva, because she had said \emph{so were you}, and it was true. The child he had built, the one raised to believe the cage was a sky, was me, exactly me, Sa-Kailan of Light, who had knelt around the forbidden centre and felt the holy dread at its edge and called it God. And the strangest thing is that it did not make me feel like a victim. It made me feel that the thing I had been raised inside had a shape, and an outside, and a maker, and could therefore be stood apart from and looked at. You cannot refuse a sky. You can refuse a design. He had left, at the very bottom of it, the one door through which the design could be seen; and I was standing in it; and I understood that the reading would carry me past forgiving him and past hating him, into deciding what a man does when he has been handed the truth about a machine that only he, of all the people alive, has both the eyes to see and the standing to stop.\par

\ornament

\begin{scripture}
\textbf{xxxiv}\\
I will not explain to you every stone. The later people have bowed down to these stones, and made of them a fetish, and I will not feed it. But a few. You have knelt to them; you are owed.\\
The great disk swinging in the Hall, that my late children take to be the very face of God at work, weighing the world: it is a clock, and free of any god. It is the trigger's own.\\
Its slow round --- three days in the coming back to where it stood --- is the firing's own measure, disguised: the counting of the strokes made slack and holy, a devotion to the hour, so that the men who tend the engine beneath could take their timing in the open, before the kneeling people, and no wonder and no suspicion ever fall upon the holiest object in the room: which is a fuse dressed as a prayer, a slow hand travelling toward a doom.\\
The Bowl, with its Ear and its Mouth, that gives the true note back and leaves a false note bare: it is Faith's own instrument, dressed as the vessel of the last prayer.\\
The tokens Light keeps, that no Keeper fires without, and that must be turned together with Shield's key: that is the safeguard, built so that I, who burned --- even enraged, even at the very end --- could never fire it with one hand. Proof, in the palm, that the whole cannot be held by one; and made so grand, so holy a relic, that no one would ever think to melt it down.\\
The beam that comes down at the equinox and names the children: it is the winding of the year-clock, as you have found.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxxv}\\
And the cleared centre of the Hall, the empty ground that no foot may cross, that they hold sacred beyond all else: to stand above that throat is death.\\
It is kept empty because a man who stood there when the great stroke came would die of it, and I would take no worshipper into that flame.\\
So I made the danger holy. I called the death-radius a sanctuary. They have knelt about its edge and worshipped the one thing there that they should flee: the one spot in all the Mountain that would kill them; and they have been kept alive by the worshipping of it.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxxvi}\\
Two black gravures face each other, and the people kneel forever penned between their dawn and their undoing. The first is the lie. Hear how it will end:\\
the second, the Cataclysm, is the one true thing I let them own --- an honest picture of the work, hidden in the open, plainly shown, that a people, reading it, takes for prophecy, its diagram of ruin left unread. A warning that is worshipped is a warning that is never read.\\
The Seal, the falling gates, is the plainest, the least dressed, the barest work of all. There are things even I could not contrive to hallow: a plain wall that drops to keep the sky out. That is one.\\
And this room --- the clock, the ledgers, the door that only a firing frees --- I built it last, and I told no one, not even the five. By slow degrees, in three lives, it would pass below the offices' own memory, and grow into a thing that Justice, to this very hour, did not know that it did not know:\\
a hidden heart inside a hidden body, waiting in the dark, an age, for you.\\
\end{scripture}

\ornament

\begin{scripture}
\textbf{xxxvii}\\
Why keep a record at all, when the whole of what I was lay hidden? I have wondered why. There is more than one true answer, and the answers give each other the lie.\\
I kept it because I wished to be known. That is the smallest of the reasons, and the one that shames me most. I made the subtlest thing a mind has ever done, and I made certain that none who lived within it would ever know that a mind had been there at all;\\
and then I could not endure to vanish whole into the work, and leave no heir. I wanted one, at least, to see the hand. Vanity outlasts even grief. Here you are.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxxviii}\\
I kept it to defend myself. When I began, I still supposed that a reader who saw all that I had seen would find, when the account was closed, that I had done the only thing a great man could, and would clear my name.\\
I believe that no longer. I have set myself down too honestly; the clear-eyed reader who comes to this door will be exactly of the kind that uses the door to cast me off. I find that I mind it less than I had expected.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xxxix}\\
And last, the reason I could not admit until the other two were written. I wanted there to be a door.\\
Under the lord, under the plan, under the wound, there was a man who knew that a thing which can never be stopped should never have been made, and who could not quite bring himself to build an engine with no way back out of it.\\
So I hid one. I put the whole truth here, at the bottom, behind a door that only the firing can open --- so that it falls to a generation that has actually used the Beast; and among them, only to an eye trained to see what has been placed on purpose: the exact eye my whole system was built to breed out of them.\\
I am an age dead as you read this. But I built the door. Whatever else you settle upon concerning me, hold on to that: at the very bottom of the worst thing ever made, its maker left a door, and left it open for you.\\
\end{scripture}

\ornament

\begin{scripture}
\textbf{xl}\\
The end of the build was the cruellest part. I did it cleanly. I had grown old by then, and I had stopped being able to feel the parts I spent. I had gone cold.\\
The tens of thousands who had cut it could not be loosed to bear its pieces away into the world. Divided knowledge is safe only while it stays divided; but hands dispersed, and grown old, will meet, and drink, and slowly reassemble the parts that each was kept from.\\
What I did to prevent it made me tremble. I will not write it.\\
Some went away: sworn, and scattered, and watched. Some stayed, the first to labour there in the sealed world, and were told that they had always lived down here.\\
Some did not go away at all.\\
The account is long. I kept it. It is the worst and the longest page I own. I have set it apart from this one. I do not ask you to read it. Leave it alone.\\
Believe me: I could not require it of myself, and, quailing, I did it through the hands of others, and looked away. That is the family failing. I have no defence.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xli}\\
Then they were gone, one way or another, and the great gates were shut at last against the world --- though the total sealing would wait for an age beyond my own.\\
Inside there was a people, a few thousand: the kept seed, the raised children, and the ageing actors. On the first morning of the sealed world they woke to their land, and did their offices, and said their prayers, and knelt around the forbidden ground.\\
The children born down there did not know that a world had ever been, or that one could be found; and they would teach their own children so.\\
I stood among them where I stood, an old man who had done the thing, and I watched the lie draw its first breath. It was good.\\
God forgive me, it was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen: a whole world, on the first morning of its life, believing that it had always been.\\
I knew that it would hold. Not one of them would ever thank me. Not one of them would ever know that I had been real.\\
\medskip
\textbf{xlii}\\
I am closing the record. I have said it all. I set it here beneath the god that I invented, in the one hand I trusted, for the one reader I could not build the system to prevent.\\
Judge me. You are the first in an age with the eyes to do it, and I made certain that you would be a heretic, because I could not bear to be judged by anybody who believed.\\
Do the thing that I could not. Look at the whole. Say what it is for.\\
I was Noxius Zero. I was the first of us, and the maker of you all, and I was a man whose father died by the outside, through the woman that he loved.\\
I chose certainty. Given my century, my evidence, my dead, a man chooses certainty; and if you would not have chosen it, then nobody ever set the choice in front of you, and you should be slower than you are going to be with your verdict.\\
\end{scripture}

Paul turned the last sheet, and there were no more, and the equinox beam had gone off the wall entirely while we read, and the Archive was lit only by our own lamps now, the borrowed sun withdrawn, the machine turning on in the dark as it had turned for an eternity and would turn whether we lived or died. And I found that I was weeping, which I had not done since a cell in a country I could not find, and that I could not have said whether I wept for the drowned workers, or for the lied-to children, or for Ka-Elun under her roof, or for the whole ended world above us, or for a woman named Ithe, an eternity dead, who was used by an enemy and accused by the man who loved her and asked, by neither of them, what she had truly known, or for myself, who had read my own possible life to the end of it in another man's hand and found that the only difference between us was a fact-checker and a machine and one night's kindness in the desert. I think I wept for all of it. I think, by then, it was all one thing.\par
