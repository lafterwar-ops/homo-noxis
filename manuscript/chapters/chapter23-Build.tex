\typesetchapter{Chapter 23}{Build}

We found it in the third year of the search, and I knew it before my engineers did, because I had stopped looking for a place a person would want and started looking for a place a person would refuse, and this was the most refusable ground I had ever stood on.\par

It offered nothing. That was the first of its perfections. A tired shoulder of old stone in a waste that killed by distance alone, with no green, no game, no road, no reason; a place the new people, who wanted warmth and company and the easy south, would never once turn their heads toward, because there was nothing in it a living person could love. And under the nothing, in stages, over a season of my water-men putting their listening rods to the rock and their ears to the rods, it gave up its secrets one at a time, and each one was the answer to a prayer I had not known how to phrase until it was granted.\par

There was water. Not a spring, not a seep, but water in a quantity that made my chief of water sit down on the stone and put his head between his knees, a reserve held in the deep rock past anything a siege could exhaust, enough to keep a people alive through the long dark I meant to make, and enough, though I did not understand this part for years, to be more than a drink. And there was heat, deeper still, the old heat of the Earth close enough to the surface here to be reached and stolen and put to work, a fire that would never go out and never need feeding, so that a sealed people need never be cold and need never burn the wood they did not have. And there were paths through the stone, faults and voids and the promise of more that could be cut, so that a whole civilisation could be threaded down into the rock and hidden and defended. The place could hold survival. I saw that in the first month.\par

I want you to understand the joy of it, before the rest, because the later scripture made the founding a solemn thing, a burden taken up in grief, and it was not. It was the happiest year of my life. I had come three years down a road that killed my people and told me every hour that I was mad, seeking a thing my own engineers swore did not exist, and I had found it, and it was better than I had dared to want. I walked the deep faults with my chiefs and we laughed like boys, because the rock kept saying yes: water here, heat here, a void that wants only a little cutting to be a hall, a fault that runs true for a mile as though it had been left out for me. A man who has decided to save the world, and then finds the world has laid the tools ready for him, does not feel like a mourner. He feels chosen. That is the most dangerous feeling a person can have, and I have never felt anything to equal it, and I would give the rest of eternity to feel it once more and know, this time, to be afraid of it.\par

I saw the other thing in the first year, and I have never been able to decide, across a hundred thousand years, whether I sought the place because some part of me already knew it would be there, or whether it was only the accident that damned everyone. Because the water and the heat and the deep stone were not only the makings of a refuge. Water that can be flashed to steam on a fire that never dies, driven down a throat cut through the roots of a world, is not only a drink and a warmth. It is a hand. And I stood at the bottom of my refuge, in the dark, with my chief of water explaining to me, in wonder, in innocence, the pressures he could raise if he ever wished to, and I understood that the ground had offered me, along with the survival of my people, the means to do to the world exactly what the world was doing to my people, and worse, and all at once, and from hiding.\par

I told myself, that year, that the hand was only a defence. I have spent the rest of time noticing that a wounded man never finds a hand and calls it a defence and then leaves it in its sheath.\par

\ornament

I will not give you the build as a wonder, because it was not a wonder, and the ones who came after made it a wonder, made it God's own masonry, and that is the first and largest of the lies, and I will not tell it even now. It was labour. It was the most enormous labour that had ever been gathered in one place in the history of the world, and it was done by people, tens of thousands of them, brought down that killing road in relays across the better part of my one life, and it cost, and I paid the cost in the only currency such a thing is ever paid in, which is other people's lives, and I kept the account, and the account is long.\par

We learned by failing. I want that set against the marble the later scripture made of it. The first pressure system we built to prove the hand killed the men who built it, because we did not yet understand what water does when it flashes in a closed space, and I stood at the mouth of a gallery and watched them bring out what the steam had left, and I did not stop the work, I corrected the drawings. A stair we cut too near a fault took a hundred workers in a single hour when the roof found the flaw we had missed. A whole reservoir wall, a year's labour, let go in the night and drowned a shift and set us back a decade. We did not know how to make the light travel true through the deep rock until we had ruined a generation of surfaces learning it; we did not know the metering of the water until men had died of learning it; we did not know any of it, and the knowing was purchased, every increment, at a price that a lord who had decided to save the whole pays without flinching, because I had learned, on the road, to spend the parts, and a build is nothing but the spending of parts.\par

You will want a number, because your kind wants numbers, and I have one, and I have kept it honestly, which is the one virtue I can claim without lying. More than forty thousand came down that road across my lifetime, in relays, and fewer than a third of them ever went back up; and of the two-thirds who stayed, most stayed in the rock they cut, in the falls and the floods and the steam and the slow grinding waste of enormous labour in a place that wanted no one alive. I built the greatest refuge in the history of the world, a thing to keep my people from ending, and I built it on a floor of my own people's bones, and I did not stop, and I did not slow. When a chief came to me white with the news of a fresh hundred lost, I asked him first whether the work they died on had held, and only after that how many, and I saw the moment he stopped loving me, and I let him stop, because a man who is spending the parts cannot afford the love of the men who count them. That is what it costs to save the whole. Not your enemies. Your own. You spend your own, by the thousand, and tell yourself they would have died anyway in the slow shrug, and you are even right, and it does not help, and you do it. It is the one thing I could have taught the woman who is reading this over the boy's shoulder, except that she learned it herself, in her own desert, with her own dead. We are the same, she and I. We spent our own to keep the whole, and we were right, and it does not help.\par

And I did the thing that made it possible for people to do such work for such a purpose without rising up against me, and it is the thing I am least able to defend and the thing the whole later shape of the Mountain grew out of, so attend to it, because it is the seed. I divided the knowledge. No team knew what it built. The water-men knew water and thought they built a well against a siege. The light-men knew their surfaces and thought they built the ways to bring the sun down to a hidden people. The men who cut the deep throat thought they cut drainage. The men who raised the great gates thought they raised defences against an army. Not one team in a hundred held two pieces, and no team but mine, and the few I trusted with the whole, understood that the well and the sun-ways and the drainage and the gates were one machine, and that the machine was a weapon aimed through the core of the Earth. I did not divide the knowledge to protect the secret from the outside. I divided it to protect the work from the workers, so that no man would ever hold enough of the whole to look at what his hands were making and refuse. It was the most useful thing I ever discovered, and it became, when I ossified it, the soul of everything that followed.\par

Paul stopped there, and set the sheet down, and I saw him go somewhere private and hard, and he said, ``There it is. The founding law of the whole Mountain, the sacred principle that no one hand may hold the whole, the thing five judges have killed for and a woman built a Beast around and this boy pulled a cord over. It is not a philosophy. It was a labour-management technique. He divided the knowledge so the workers would not down tools. And then he made a religion of it so that the division would outlive the reason for it, and by the time your council was killing men to keep the offices apart, no one alive remembered that the whole holy architecture of it started as a way to get a frightened workforce to build its own doom without noticing.'' And I said, because it was mine to say and no one else's, ``It is worse than that, Paul. It works both ways and he knew it. The same wall that stops the workers seeing the whole is the wall that stops any one Keeper ever firing it alone. He built the division to hide the weapon from the builders. And it turned out to be the only thing that ever kept the weapon safe. My people's whole terror, the thing that damned us and the thing that saved the world five times, is a construction foreman's trick that grew a soul.''\par

\ornament

There were five great works, and so there were five great teams, and I set a commander over each, and the commanders answered to me, and among the five of them, only distributed, never gathered, lay the whole. And because I could see, even then, that I would die, and that the thing had to run without me for longer than any name would be remembered, I began, in the last third of the build, to do the thing that turned a project into a people. I stopped thinking of the five teams as teams. I began to make them lines.\par

The team of water and stone and heat and the measuring of all of it, the team that held the material world and its balances, I made into a thing that would pass itself down, master to chosen child, with its own signs and its own pride and its own jealous secrecy, and I let it grow a name for itself out of the thing it did, which was to keep the balance of the deep water and the deep fire, and Balance is what it became, long after the last person in it understood that it was ever a team of engineers. The team that listened, that read the return of the deep strokes and knew the timing of them, the rhythm, the phase, the exact instant, I made into the line that kept the sacred time, and taught them to feel it as devotion, as a listening for God in the stone, and it became Faith. The light-men, who bent the deep pressure through the rock with their surfaces and their apertures, who aimed the thing, became the keepers of a mystery of alignment and revelation, and became Light. The men who kept my accounts and my sequences and my permissions, who alone were allowed to say whether a thing might lawfully proceed, who held the records and the order of every irreversible act, became Justice. And the men of the gates and the containment and the secrecy and, when it was needed, the violence, became Shield.\par

I gave them nothing they did not already do. I only took the doing and wrapped it in inheritance and pride and mystery and a little fear, so that it would be performed perfectly, forever, by people who would come to believe they were born to it, which is the only way to make a person do an exact thing for a thousand years: convince him it is not a task but a self.\par

And I gave each line a discipline to hide its true work inside: a real knowledge, freely taught, that was true and deep and absorbing and lay just beside the secret, so that a clever child of the line might spend a whole brilliant life mastering the cover and never once stumble onto the thing beneath it. To Balance I gave the honest sciences of measure and material and the turning of water and heat, so that a Balance might be the finest engineer of his age and never know his engineering was the tending of a bomb. To Faith I gave the true art of listening to the stone, of rhythm and resonance and the reading of the deep return, and called it prayer, so that the most gifted ear in the Mountain would spend itself hearing God and never know it was hearing a weapon breathe. To Light I gave optics and alignment and the whole real mystery of how a wave may be bent and brought to a point, and let them think it holy. To Justice I gave the true discipline of records and sequence and the weighing of whether a thing may lawfully proceed, and hid inside it the permissions of a firing. I did not give them lies to study. I gave them truths to study, arranged around a hole, and taught them to call the hole God; and to feel, whenever they came near its edge in their studies, a particular holy dread that I had built into the teaching for exactly that purpose, so that a curious mind would flinch from the one question at the precise moment it grew able to ask it. That flinch is my finest work. I can feel it in the boy who is reading this, even now, even in a heretic, even after all of it: the trained recoil from the centre. I put that in him a hundred thousand years before his mother bore him. Forgive me, boy. I could not think of a better way, and I did try.\par

\ornament

And then I did the thing that is the heartbeat under everything you have read, and I did it for a reason that was, God help me, sound, and that is why it is the worst thing in this account.\par

The machine had a flaw, and the flaw was the flaw of all my kind: it was kept by persons, and persons change. Every generation, the hands that held each of the five offices would die, and new hands would take them, and a new hand is an untested hand. A Keeper who had never once worked the true machine, who knew only the ritual that hid it, might hold the office for forty years and never know whether, at the hour of need, he could actually do the thing his office existed to do. And five such untested hands, at the hour of need, is not a weapon and not a government. It is five frightened priests who have forgotten they were ever engineers, standing over an engine none of them has ever truly run.\par

So I built a test, and I hid it inside a devotion, and I made it law. At every succession, whenever a Keeper died and a new one rose, the five would gather, in secret, apart, and they would perform what I told them was the renewal of the covenant, the rite by which the new Keeper is joined to the ancient chain: and what it truly was, under the prayer, was a firing. A minimal one. A test-shot of the great weapon, small enough to harm nothing, large enough to prove that the machine still answered, that the five new-or-old hands could still, together, unanimously, work it; that the weapon and the government had survived one another for one more generation. A few drops of water, metered onto the deep fire. One small controlled stroke into the roots of the world. And the world, struck, would answer, and its answer, coming home up through the deep stone to the listening Faith, would be the sign that the covenant held and the machine lived.\par

I made it law, and I made it secret, and I made it holy, all three, because a test that men perform because they are ordered to will be skimped the first time it is inconvenient, but a test that men perform because it is the most sacred rite of their office, the very joining of a new Keeper to the ancient chain, will be performed perfectly by frightened devout people forever, long after the last of them could say what the five strokes are for. I called it the readiness. I told them it was the hour in which God confirms the new Keeper. It was the hour in which my machine confirmed itself. And I set the size of it with great care, small enough that the world would take no lasting hurt from a single stroke a generation, large enough that its answer could never be faked or mistaken; and I have wondered since whether even that care was a mercy or only an engineer's tidiness, and I have decided it was both, and I have long since given up trying to pull the two of them apart.\par

The first time we did it, in my own lifetime, to prove it worked, I stood in the Hall we had built for it and heard the Earth give back the shape of the stroke, and it came in parts, five even blows and a long soft roll behind them, the sound of a struck world rolling over in its sleep, and I thought it the most beautiful and most terrible thing I had ever made, because I understood that I had just given my machine a heartbeat, a slow one, one beat a generation, that would go on sounding under the desert for as long as the Mountain kept its covenant, and that anyone, in any age, with an ear fine enough and a reason to listen, might one day hear the Mountain's heart beating in the ground and come looking for the body.\par

Here Sarah took the sheet out of Paul's hands, and read that passage again to herself, silently, and then set it down, and put both her hands flat on the cold stone of the machine, and was quiet for a long time. And when she spoke she did not speak to us. ``A few drops of water,'' she said. ``Metered onto the deep fire. Five even blows and a long soft roll. He is describing the thing I heard at Reggane when I was twenty-seven. He is describing the exact signature I chased across forty years and two continents and everything I ever destroyed. It was a succession. Somewhere under that desert, in the year of the first French bomb, an old Keeper of one of his five offices died, and a new one rose, and they went behind their door and proved the covenant held, and the world answered in five parts, and I was sitting forty kilometres away with a drum-paper on my knees, and I heard a hundred-thousand-year-old man test his weapon, and I thought I had discovered something. I had heard his heartbeat. That is all my life was. I heard the heartbeat of a dead man's machine, and I spent everything I had and everyone I touched trying to cut the body open to see it.'' She took her hands off the stone. ``He knew. He wrote it here. He knew that one day someone with an ear would hear it and come. He built that risk into it on purpose, and I am the risk. I am the thing he predicted. I am the ear he knew would come.''\par

But the machine and the offices were not the hard part, and I will tell you the hard part now, because it is the part the later people could never have invented, having been its product; only its maker could set it down; and setting it down is the nearest I will come, in this account, to a confession.\par

A machine kept by trained hands can survive a few generations. It cannot survive a hundred thousand years, because in a hundred thousand years the trained hands forget what they are training for, and a people that no longer remembers why it does an exact thing will, sooner or later, stop doing it exactly, or stop doing it at all, and then the machine is dead, and the whole was not kept. I had not spent my one life and my father's world and forty thousand lives in the rock to build a thing that would run for six generations and fail. I meant it to run forever. And to make a thing run forever, kept perfectly by people who no longer understand it, there is only one method, and I found it, and it is the true horror at the centre of everything I made, worse than the weapon, worse than the drownings, worse than the divided knowledge: I did not build a machine and hope a people would maintain it. I built a people, on purpose, to be the maintenance, and I built them so that they could never stop.\par

I took children. I have said it. Some were ours, the kept seed; some I bought or gathered from the failing edges of both peoples; and I raised them, and had them raised, to believe a thing that was not true, which was that the Mountain had always been, that it was not built but given, that the offices were not jobs but holy inheritances, that the machine was not a weapon but a god and a covenant and the very order of the world. I wrote them a past. I aged it deliberately, so that the newest child would find, everywhere he looked, evidence that the Mountain was ancient beyond counting and that his small life was a late leaf on an immemorial tree. I carved a Genesis for them, a story of a first morning and a first murder and a stolen holy speech, so that they would understand the outside as curse and themselves as the wounded keepers of a light, and never guess that the curse was a wounded man's memory of a real murder and a betrayal he refused to measure, dressed as the wrath of God. I gave them a scripture that was an operating manual set to hymns, so that they would keep the machine by keeping the faith, and never know the two were the same act. And I set actors among the first generation, people who knew the truth and agreed, for love or for fear or for the promise of the whole, to spend their lives pretending to believe, so that the second generation, watching them, would believe without pretending, and the third would simply know, the way you know the sky is above you, that the Mountain is God's and always was.\par

I wrote the Genesis myself, in the worst nights of the build, and I am not proud of how easily it came. I needed the outside to be a horror to them, so that no child would ever long for it the way the children of my own dying people had longed for the warmth of the new people; and so I took the true thing and turned it a quarter, which is the only turn a good lie ever needs. The true thing was a murder. The outside had reached into my house through the woman I loved, and killed my father with what she carried, and I had refused, ever after, to learn how much she had known. So I made a Genesis of it, cleaner than the truth. I kept the murder, because the murder was real, and a real thing is what anchors a lie; but I moved it back to the first age of the world, and made the victim a brother, strong and beloved and envied, in place of a father, and let the murderer's line become the outside, the open-land people, cursed and multiplying and hating the shade they could not find. Every part of it is a truth turned by a quarter: there was a murder, and the outside did it, and it did found the hatred. What the scripture cannot hold is not the woman --- I gave them a woman, a daughter of Faith carried off as spoil when the murderer fled, blameless as a stolen lamp, a thing done to us and never a thing that might have been done with her consent; that much I could carve. What I filed away and never let near the marble was the truth under her: that the knife was guided into my father by a hand I loved, and that I chose, ever after, never to ask that hand what it had known, because a people can be taught to fear a born murderer and cannot be taught to fear a wound a man brought half upon himself. So I kept \emph{that} out, and let her stand in the story as pure loss --- and buried the question of her choosing in the same ground as my father. That was the alchemy: I took a real murder and a betrayal I would not measure, and made of them one clean eternal curse; and I have watched Keepers read it to trembling boys as the deepest truth of the world, and every time, under the marble of it, I heard the one question I never put to her, and knew that I had turned my own refusal into the terror of a hundred thousand children so that they would keep my machine. The making of a private wound into a public dread is the thing for which there is no name low enough, and I did it in an evening, and it worked better than anything else I built.\par

Paul had to stop reading here, and he did not pretend otherwise, and it was Va-Sheva who took the sheet from him, Va-Sheva of Shield, who had spent her whole life inside the thing this man was describing building, and she read the passage about the children in the flat Shield voice, and when she had finished she said only, ``I was one of those children. So were you,'' to me, ``and so was every soul in this Mountain, back and back, a hundred thousand years of children taught that a cage was a sky. And the worst of it, the thing I cannot get past, is the part he will not let himself lie about even now: that the first generation may have loved them. That the actors who lied to the second generation with their whole lives may have loved the children they were lying to. My mother loved me. She taught me the sky was above me and the cage was the world, and she was wrong, and she had been taught it too, and she loved me while she did it. You cannot even hate it cleanly. That is his final cruelty. He built a prison so loving that the guards and the prisoners are the same people, and have been, for a hundred thousand years, and every one of them was somebody's beloved child.''\par

We were quiet a long time after that, in the cold, and I could not look at Va-Sheva, because she had said \emph{so were you}, and it was true. The child he had built, the one raised to believe the cage was a sky, was me, exactly me, Sa-Kailan of Light, who had knelt around the forbidden centre and felt the holy dread at its edge and called it God. He had described the making of me a hundred thousand years before my mother bore me, with the flat accuracy of a man describing a tool he had filed to a purpose. And the strangest thing, the thing I have never been able to explain to anyone who was not down there in the cold with us, is that it did not make me feel like a victim. It made me feel that the thing I had been raised inside had a shape, and an outside, and a maker, and could therefore be stood apart from and looked at, and I had never once in my life been permitted to feel that. You cannot refuse a sky. You can refuse a design. He had spent a hundred thousand years making certain his people could never see that the sky was a design, and then, at the very bottom of it, for reasons even he could not fully name, he had left the one door through which the design could be seen; and I was standing in that door; and I understood, in the cold, that the reading was not going to end in my forgiving him, or my hating him. It was going to end in my deciding what a man does when he has been handed the truth about a machine that only he, of all the people alive, has both the eyes to see and the standing to stop.\par

\ornament

I will not explain every stone to you, because the later people made a fetish of the stones and I will not feed it. But you will want a few, because you have lived among them and knelt to them, and you are owed the knowing.\par

The great disk that swings in the high Hall, the Pendulum that my later children take for the very face of God weighing the world: it is the trigger's clock. Its slow wandering circle, its split of the hours, the long patient turning that takes near three days to come round to itself, is the cover-grammar of the firing rhythm, the timing of the strokes made to look like a devotion to the passage of time, so that the men who tend the true engine below could take their measure from it in the open, in front of the whole kneeling people, and no one would ever see that the holiest object in the Mountain is a fuse dressed as a prayer. The Bowl with its Ear and its Mouth, that returns the true note and keeps silence for a false one, is the listening instrument of Faith, the thing that hears the world's answer and knows the phase, dressed as the vessel of the last prayer. The token that Light keeps, that no Keeper may fire without, that must turn together with Shield's own key: that is the safeguard I built so that even I, even in a rage, even at the end, could never fire the thing with one hand; it is the physical proof that the whole cannot be held by one, made into a holy relic so that no one would ever melt it down. The beam that comes down at the equinox, that names the children and blesses the balanced: it is the winding of the year-clock, as you have found. And the cleared and forbidden centre of the Hall, the empty place no foot may cross, that the people take for the most sacred ground in the world: it is only the place directly above the throat of the weapon, kept clear because a person standing there when the great stroke is made would be killed by it, and I could not have my faithful killed at their own devotions, and so I made the danger holy, and called the death-radius a sanctuary, and they have knelt around the edge of it for a hundred thousand years, worshipping the one spot in the Mountain that would kill them, and being kept alive by their worship.\par

The two great black gravures that face each other across the Hall, so that the people kneel always between their beginning and their doom: the first is the lie I have just confessed, my Genesis, my inverted wound. The second, the Cataclysm, is the one true thing I let them keep, the honest picture of what the machine does, hidden in the open where a whole people would take it for prophecy and never for a diagram, because a warning that is worshipped is a warning that is never read. The great Seal, the falling gates that shut the Mountain against the ending, is the plainest and least dressed of all my works, because there are some things even I could not think how to make holy, and a wall that drops to keep the sky out is one of them. And this room, the Archive, the year-clock and the ledgers and the door that only a firing can open: this I built last, and told to no one, not even the five, so that within three generations it would pass out of the memory of the offices and become a thing Justice itself did not know that it did not know, a hidden heart inside the hidden body, waiting, in the dark, across a hundred thousand years, for you.\par

\ornament

Why did I keep this record, when the whole of my genius was the hiding of the truth. I have asked it of myself for longer than your kind has existed, and I will give you the true answer, which is that there is more than one, and they do not agree, and I have made my peace with keeping several true things at once, as the best of you learn to do.\par

I kept it because I wanted to be known. That is the smallest and truest of the reasons and the one I am most ashamed of. I had made the most sophisticated thing a mind has ever made, and I had made certain that no one who lived inside it would ever be able to know that a mind had made it, and I could not bear to vanish so completely into my own success. I wanted one reader, someday, to see the hand. Vanity outlasts even grief; I have proven it; here you are.\par

I kept it to justify myself, because I believed, when I began the record, that a future reader, seeing the whole of what I saw, the ending of my people, the vastness of the loss, the impossibility of any other rescue, would judge me and find that I had done the only thing a great man could do, and would vindicate me. I no longer believe that. I have written myself too honestly. I have watched, across the writing of it, the true shape come up through the reasons like a stone through thawing ground, and I know now that a reader clear-eyed enough to reach this record will be exactly the kind of reader who will use it to reject me. I have built, in the very act of defending myself, the instrument of my own conviction. I find I do not mind as much as I expected to.\par

And I kept it, last, for the reason I could not admit until the others were written, which is that I wanted there to be a door. Somewhere in me, under the lord and under the plan and under the wound, there was a man who knew that a thing which cannot ever be stopped is a thing that should never have been made, and who could not quite bring himself to build a machine with no way out. So I hid one. I put the whole truth here, at the bottom, behind a door that only the firing of the weapon could open, so that it would fall only into the hands of a generation that had actually used the Beast, and among them only into the hands of a reader with an eye trained to see what is placed on purpose, which is the exact eye my whole system was built to breed out. I built a door that only a heretic could open, and behind it I put the one thing that could end the whole design: the truth, entire, and with it the knowledge that the design was a design, and could therefore be refused. I do not know if any reader will ever choose to open it and refuse. I am a hundred thousand years dead as you read this. But I built the door. Remember, whatever else you decide about me, that at the very bottom of the worst thing ever made, its maker left a door, and left it for you.\par

\ornament

The end of the build was the cruellest part and I did it cleanly, because by then I was very old and had stopped being able to feel the parts I spent. The tens of thousands who had cut the Mountain could not be allowed to carry its secret back into the world; a divided knowledge is only safe while it stays divided, and a workforce dispersed is a workforce that gathers, over ale, in old age, the pieces it was kept from. I will not write down what was done with them. Some were sent away broken and scattered and sworn and watched. Some were kept, and became the first labourers of the sealed world, and were told they had always lived there. Some were not sent away at all. The account is long, and I have kept it, and it is the longest and worst page, and I have set it apart from this one, and I do not require you to read it, and I ask you to believe that I could not require it of myself either, and so I did it through others, and looked away, which is the family failing, and the failing of every hand that ever built a terrible thing, and I have no defence.\par

And then the outside workers were gone, one way or another, and the great gates were sealed at last against the world (the Cataclysm-sealing, the true and total one, would come in a later age), and inside the sealed Mountain there was a people, a few thousand of them, the kept seed and the raised children and the aging actors, and they woke on the first morning of the sealed world and did their offices and said their prayers and knelt around the forbidden centre, and the children among them, the ones born down here, did not know that there had ever been anything else, and would teach their own children so, and I stood among them, an old man who had done it, and watched the lie take its first clean breath, and it was good, God forgive me, it was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen, a whole world believing itself eternal on the first morning of its life, and I knew that it would hold, that I had built a thing that would keep the whole of my people alive and hidden and ignorant and safe for as long as the desert lasted, and that not one of them would ever thank me, because not one of them would ever know I had been real.\par

I am closing the record now. I have said the whole of it. I set it here under the god I invented, in the one hand I trusted, for the one reader I could not build the system to prevent. Judge me. You are the first person in a hundred thousand years with the eyes to do it, and I made certain you would be a heretic, because I could not be judged by anyone who believed. Do the thing I could not. Look at the whole, and decide what it is for.\par

I was Noxius Zero. I was the first of us and the maker of all of you, and I was a man whose father was murdered by the outside, through the woman he loved, and who chose certainty over her because certainty can be built into stone and grief can only be carried. I could not let the wound be the size it was, and so I made it the size of the world. Everything else is commentary.\par

Paul turned the last sheet, and there were no more, and the equinox beam had gone off the wall entirely while we read, and the Archive was lit only by our own lamps now, the borrowed sun withdrawn, the machine turning on in the dark as it had turned for a hundred thousand years and would turn whether we lived or died. And I found that I was weeping, which I had not done since a cell in a country I could not find, and that I could not have said, if they had asked me, whether I wept for the drowned workers, or for the lied-to children, or for Ka-Elun under her roof, or for the whole ended world above us, or for a woman named Ithe, a hundred thousand years dead, who was used by an enemy and accused by the man who loved her and asked, by neither of them, what she had truly known, or for myself, who had read my own possible life to the end of it in another man's hand and found that the only difference between us was a fact-checker and a machine and one night's kindness in the desert.
