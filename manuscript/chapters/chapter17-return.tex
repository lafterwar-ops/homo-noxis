\typesetchapter{Chapter 17}{Return}

I was three men before I was myself again. Va-Elise had given me the three the way she had once given me a name, reading them off a card while a country slid past a window, and I wore each of them for a stretch of the road and shed him at the place she had marked: a tired functionary flying south out of a private field, a mineral company's translator changing aircraft in a shed where no hand touches a bag, a nobody in a helicopter that had crossed those hills so often nobody looked up. Paul learned his own three beside me and was bad at them, being an honest man, and I told him that honesty is only another accent and that he should stop apologising to the men whose papers he was pretending to hold, and he took it, because he had begun, somewhere over the sea, to do the thing I did, which is to watch the people who are watching you.\par

And then the ordinary journeys ran out, as Sarah had promised they would, and there was the desert, and there was no more cleverness to be had from it. A man met us at the last road with animals and two others and a face wrapped against the light, and he was young, and he was of the veiled people, and when Sarah spoke a name to him he did not answer to it but to the silence after it, which was its own answer. Amastan was thirty years dead. This was the office that had outlived the man, a son of a son, and he did not tell us his name and we did not ask, and he took us up off the last road into folds of rock that no map I have ever seen since has drawn, because the men who draw the maps have still never been let close enough to find the seams.\par

We travelled at night, mostly, and lay up through the day under cloth the colour of the ground, and I learned that a desert crossing is not the clean romance the Mountain's own children are taught to picture when their elders tell them the outside is death. It is thirst measured out by the mouthful, and animals that must be tended before men, and a great deal of waiting, and Sarah bearing all of it without a word of complaint at an age when most people are being kept warm by others, because, she said, she had crossed worse ground for worse reasons and had never once let a desert see her tired. The young man of the sand spoke to none of us and watched all of us, and once, when Paul went down on a scree in the dark and swore in his northern tongue, the young man put out a hand and steadied him without turning his head, the way you steady a thing you have been made responsible for and do not love. That was the whole of the tenderness the desert had to offer, and it was enough, and it brought us to the folds with our water nearly gone.\par

I will not pretend I felt nothing, coming back into that country on the back of an animal with a false name in my mouth. The desert does not care that you are exiled. The light was the light I was made under. The cold that comes down after it was the cold I had learned the world was, before Switzerland taught me the world was also lake and lamp and a man who buys sweets for no reason. Somewhere ahead of us, under a shoulder of stone that looked like every other shoulder of stone, five thousand people were breathing who believed with their whole hearts that no one alive knew they were there, and I was riding toward them with two Outsiders and a machine that had recorded my confession, to tell them the one thing their whole world was built to make unsayable.\par

\ornament

We came on the first of it two days out from the folds, and it was one of Sarah's own.\par

She had told me there were stations, a thin scatter of them across that quarter, small and buried and manned by two or three, the far nerve-ends of the thing she had spent sixty-six years growing: men who watched the drums and the borders and the fuel and reported north, and turned a survey aside when one strayed, and otherwise sat in the heat and were bored, as she had taught them to be, because boredom was the sound of the work going well. The young man of the sand took us to it without being asked, and I understood from the set of his shoulders as we came up on it that he had already been, and had wanted her to see.\par

The door of it stood open to the desert, which she said no door of hers had ever done. Inside, the instruments were still running. That was the thing that turned the air in my chest: the drums still turned, the styli still drew, the little machine that made its own power still ticked in the corner, and on the paper coming off the nearest drum was the shape I had learned to dread in a Swiss house, the five even strokes, over and over, patient, arriving out of the ground while we stood there, because the sounding had not stopped, would not stop, cared nothing for the men who had kept this station or where they had gone. There was blood. It was on the floor and on the edge of the table and dried black in the heat, and it was a great deal of it, and there was no one there to have shed it, no bodies, nothing to bury. On the table a message lay half-written in a hand that had stopped in the middle of a word, the pencil still lying in the trough of the page where it had rolled. Someone had burned a box of paper in the corner and done it badly, in a hurry, so that the middles of the sheets survived, curled and brown, and Sarah knelt and turned them with one finger and did not let any of us see her face while she did it.\par

``They were taken,'' she said at last, standing, her voice scoured flat. ``Not killed. Taken. You do not burn your own papers and leave three men dead; you burn your papers because you are being made to leave and you have a moment to deny them the drawer. And there are no bodies because bodies are of no use to them and living men are. My people know this desert, this quarter, these routes, my methods, my notation. Whoever has them now has all of that, in a room somewhere, being asked questions.'' She looked at the drum still drawing its patient poison. ``I built a nervous system for this Mountain. And someone has begun, very methodically, to pull it out of the body while it still feels.''\par

She had found something else in the half-burned papers; I saw it pass into her coat before any of us could ask, and she did not speak of it in that station, nor for a long while after. She told me, when there was a later, what it had been: a duty roster in the station's own careful hand, charred to a brown lace at the edges, listing a resupply that had come up from the south three weeks before the place fell, by a quarter and a route that only a handful of hands in all her sixty-six years had ever been trusted to hold. Her network had not been broken into from without. It had been opened from the inside, with a key, by one of the few she had ever cut a key for. And the hand that turned it --- though I did not know it, kneeling in that blood-smelling room, and Va-Sheva did not know it for far longer than any of us --- was Jean-Charles.\par

I do not have the whole of him, and I have decided that this is the truest thing I can tell you of the man. He was of the French service; his charm was a trade before it was ever a gift; and long before he carried me out of the desert under a cloth he had gone to Va-Sheva because a Veil of Shield who stood at the Horizon threshold was the most useful door into my people the outside had ever found, and he had opened it the way he opened everything, patiently, by being impossible not to trust. Through that door his masters had the innermost routes, and my people's methods, and at the last the way to the Mountain itself. Whether the feeling in him turned real, and when, and whether at the end he gave his service less than they asked of him --- these I cannot answer, and Sarah could not, and Va-Sheva spent what was left of her life refusing to ask, exactly as a man a hundred thousand years dead refused to ask them of a woman named Ithe. What I can set down is only the fact the roster held, which no later love or courage erases: that the enemy reached the Mountain through a hand it had won by love, and that the hand was his, and that he was, even as we knelt there, the one man alive who could walk us in through the ring he had himself helped to draw.\par

Sarah knew it in the station; I am certain of that now, from the way she folded the paper away and did not once look at him for the rest of that night. And she said nothing, because we were two days from the Mountain and he was our road in, and because the one soul in the world that knowledge would unmake was climbing toward us through the stone with a lamp and could not be handed it on a stair in the middle of an assault. So she carried it. Watching her decide to carry it was the loneliest thing I have ever watched happen to another person: a woman who had spent sixty-six years making certain that no single soul but herself ever held the whole of anything, handed one more unbearable whole, with nowhere in all the world to set it down.\par

Paul had the small machine out. He held it low, and he did not narrate to it or explain himself to it; he only let it hear the room, the tick of the instruments, the wind in the open door, Sarah's scoured voice. He had stopped being the man who checks whether a thing is true. Somewhere over the sea, without telling anyone, he had become the man who makes certain that, whatever became of the rest of us, the thing that was true would have been written down.\par

We saw the camp on the fourth day, from the high broken ground the young man brought us to before dawn, and I lay on cold rock beside a woman older than my grandmother would have been and a fact-checker from a northern city and looked down at the thing that had come for my people, and the first feeling it gave me, before the fear, was a kind of vertigo of recognition, because I had seen its shape before. I had lived inside its shape. It was built like the Mountain.\par

From above, Sarah said, they had eyes we could not see, and she named them by their signs: the dish on its mount that spoke to something over the weather, the pattern of the camp that assumed it was watched from the sky by its own side and wished to be read by them and by no one else. Twice while we lay there something crossed the sky very high, a fleck with no sound to it that caught the early light a moment before the ground did, and she followed it with her eyes and said it was theirs, and that it would come again on a schedule she could have written out for us on the rock, and that every line and shadow in the camp below had been arranged to be invisible to every eye in the world except the one that owned that fleck. They were hiding this operation from their own allies and their own rivals as carefully as they were hiding it from us. It was, she said, the most costly kind of secret there is, the kind one state keeps from all the others; and they were spending it, all of it, on my Mountain. In the middle distance, on the ridges, in the throats of the approaches, at the lip of the Oasis road, there were men dug in and facing inward, and instruments on tripods that were not for looking at the sky, and she read the placement of every one of them aloud in a low even voice, why this one sat where it sat, what it could hear, what it was waiting for something to walk across. And below all of it, in the flat pan before the folds, were the trucks I had seen in the high pictures, the squat heavy ones that struck the ground, and beside them the taller thing, the rig, and around the rig the beginnings of a hole going down, and Sarah told us the depth it was reaching for without needing to be told, because she had drawn the map it was reaching into, and she knew to the metre where the voids were, the galleries, the vents, the throat of the Hall itself.\par

``Above, around, below,'' she said. ``They are not surrounding a place. They are surrounding a body. They mean to know the whole of it, the skin and the bones and the soft parts, before they open it, so that when they open it they lose nothing.'' She was quiet a moment. ``They have no intention of destroying this Mountain. You do not spend this to destroy a thing; you drop one bomb on it, as my husband would have, and go home. This is the other kind of operation. This is the kind you mount when what is inside the thing is worth more than the thing, and you want it whole, and alive, and yours.''\par

She read the particular parts of it to us then the way a physician reads a shadow on a plate to the family, quietly, naming each thing and what it meant. The truck on the western ridge, set down where no ore had ever lain, was there to cover an old drovers' track that comes to the Oasis from the north, the one Shield had let fall out of memory because no Outsider had ever found it; someone had told them exactly where to look for it. The deep borehole, the one with the rig over it, was not aimed at the Hall at all, but at a point she named a little east of it and lower, and she went grey giving me the reason, because it was aimed at the water, at the great hidden reserve that Balance kept against the worst of the lean years, for a besieger who means to take a place alive must first find its water, and one who means only to break it need do no more than foul the well. And the tents at the centre, ringed twice, with their own generators and their clean white cloth and the marked vehicles built to carry a man lying flat, were a hospital; and the sight of a hospital in that camp frightened her more than the guns, because you do not raise a hospital to raid a mine. You raise one when you expect to take prisoners you must keep alive, and to spend your own men in the taking, and to be there a long, long while.\par

Paul asked her, in a whisper, the fact-checker to the last of it, how she could be so certain of any of this from a mile off in poor light. She said she was certain because she had stood in a hundred camps built to that same pattern, on the buying side of them, and because a camp is a sentence and she had spent her life learning the language it is written in, and because, God forgive her, a good half of this one was written in her own hand. Then she was quiet a while, and when she spoke again it was to the ground and not to us. ``Some part of me wanted it found,'' she said. ``The whole time. So that it would be over. So that I would not have to be the only one left holding it.'' She did not take her eyes from the camp. ``Well. It is found. And this is what found looks like.'' And she said nothing else for an hour.\par

And here is what I could not stop seeing, and could not say aloud for a long while, and finally said to Paul's machine in the dark because I could not carry it alone. The camp was divided as we are divided. The men who drilled did not speak to the men who guarded; I watched a guard turn a driller back from a truck that was not his to approach. The men who read the instruments sat apart from the men who spoke to the sky, and neither went near the tents where, Sarah said, the questions were being asked of her taken people. No one down there held the whole of it. Each knew his own piece and was walled from the rest, so that if you took one man you took only a corner. They had built, for the stealing of my people's secret, the exact instrument my people had built to keep it: a machine of parts that trust no single part with everything. We had done it out of terror, so that no one hand could ever end the world. They had done it out of thrift, so that no one hand could ever betray the operation. The same wall. Built by fear on my side of it and by discipline on theirs. And theirs was newer, and faster, and I understood, lying on that rock, that my people's eleven-thousand-year advantage had been that no one outside was clever enough or patient enough to want in. That advantage was over. It had been over, Sarah would have said, since a bored woman folded a paper at a bomb. We were only the last to be told.\par

Jean-Charles had gone down in the dark and come back before the light, which is a thing I still do not fully understand, and he did not tell us how, and he brought two things up out of the camp with him. One was a man, gagged and bound and furious and very frightened, an ordinary man of the outside in the clothes of the perimeter, a guard from the edge who had wandered a few metres too far into the dark to make water and met, in that dark, the smiling Frenchman who had once carried me under a cloth. The other was a flat hard case of documents and a black instrument for speaking across distance, and Sarah took the case from him and opened it on the rock and went through it fast with her hands, and I watched her come to a single page and stop.\par

``There,'' she said, and turned it so Paul's machine could see it though the machine could not read. ``That mark. Top corner. That is not a company. A company does not stamp a survey with that. That is a compartment of the French strategic service, the part that does not have a name you would find written down, the heir to every arrangement my father and Martinez ever made in this desert, and it has put its own quiet mark on a page that pretends to be about minerals.'' She set the page down with a care that was worse than throwing it. ``I have my answer. The rogue operation I was hoping to find, some greedy contractor gone off the leash that I might have called off with a telephone: it does not exist. This is the state. This is the loaf, and the men who took it from me have decided the yellow rock was never the prize. The prize is the thing under the rock. They know there is a mechanism. They do not know what it is. And they are drilling toward it to find out, on my grid, with my methods, and they will reach it, because I taught the world how.''\par

The bound man made a sound behind his gag, and Jean-Charles crouched by him, and did not hurt him, and said something low in French that I did not have, and the man went still and listened, and Jean-Charles turned back to us and said, ``He will talk when we can afford to let him. He is a fence-sitter, not a believer. He thinks he is guarding a mine.''\par

But a bored man on a fence hears a great deal, and over the next hour, unbound to the waist and given water and spoken to in his own tongue by a man who knew precisely how to be gentle with someone frightened, he gave Jean-Charles the shape of the thing he was guarding without ever knowing there was a shape to it. They had been told it was a mineral survey of the highest secrecy; that there were people in the ground ahead of them, primitives, a lost tribe, to be handled without loss of life wherever it could be managed, because they held knowledge the work required. And they carried orders the man had grumbled over with his fellows on the fence, strange orders for soldiers: that certain persons, once found, were to be taken alive and wholly unharmed whatever the cost, five of them, marked out not by their faces but by what each one did; and that whatever lay below was to be held, and kept whole, and on no account brought down. ``They keep saying it,'' the man had told Jean-Charles, baffled by his own orders. ``Intact. Everything intact. We are the only sappers I ever heard of who were sent in and told to break nothing.''\par

Sarah listened to the translation with her eyes shut. ``Five, by function,'' she said. ``The command authorities. They know there are five, and that the five together are the thing that works the mechanism, and they mean to take the whole set alive and make it answer to a new hand. They have no wish to end the world. They want the key to it, with the keepers still hanging on the ring.''\par

He looked at me then, and the grief was in his face again under the competence. ``The route is open. There is a seam the young man knows that their sensors do not yet cover, on the far side, and it will close within the day when they finish the ring. We go now, or we do not go.''\par

Va-Sheva met us inside the skin of the Mountain, and I had thought, all the way up the last dark seam with an Outsider's hand on my belt and my heart going like a boy's, about what I would do when I saw her, and in the event I did nothing, because she gave me nothing to do it to. She was standing in a low stone chamber I half remembered, in the plain grey of Shield, with two wardens behind her and a lamp between us, and she looked at me the way Shield is trained to look, at the spaces around a person and not the person, weighing what I had brought in with me before she would let herself weigh me.\par

She had rebuilt. I want that set down plainly, because I had carried out of this Mountain the memory of a woman coming apart in a cell, and I had feared, across a whole desert, that I was riding back to finish breaking her by arriving. I was wrong. Whatever she had done in the half-year since she chose the cage over me, she had done it fully. The hands were steady. The voice, when it came, was an office speaking.\par

``You were sent out to find whether the enemy within was real,'' she said. ``Shield's own writ, sealed by the council that could not agree to keep you. You come back with two Outsiders, one of them the maker of the very network that hunts us, and a machine that has recorded God knows what, up a seam no Veil is shown, in the middle hour of an attack this Mountain does not yet know it is under.'' A silence. ``By every law I hold, I should have you killed at this lamp, and them with you, and the seam sealed behind your bodies.''\par

``Then do it,'' I said, ``or hear me, but decide now, because the woman beside me can tell you the depth of the hole they are drilling and the day it reaches the Hall, and no one else alive can, and every breath you spend deciding is a breath she is not spending saving your people.''\par

She looked at Sarah for a long moment, and Sarah looked back, two women who had each spent a lifetime holding a wall, and something passed between them that had no warmth in it and a great deal of recognition, and Va-Sheva said, to me, not to her, ``Jean-Charles vouches for the breach.''\par

``He does.''\par

``I trust his hands. I have worked beside them.'' She had never told me that; there was a whole history in it I would never now have time to learn. ``I do not trust hers, and I am about to have to.'' She turned and gave a low command to a warden, and the warden went, and she looked back at me, and for one breath only the office slipped, and it was not grief that showed under it, it was something worse and steadier, it was the look of a person who has already grieved a thing all the way to the end and put it away where it can no longer be used against them. ``I contained you to keep you breathing,'' she said. ``I did not do it so that you could bring the breathing back inside the wall. That was cruel of you. We will not speak of it again. Follow, and touch nothing, and keep your Outsiders between us.''\par

\ornament

She led us up through Shield's own ways, narrow and unlovely and cut for defence and for nothing else, and as we climbed she spent no breath at all on me and every breath on the thing in hand, throwing questions back over her shoulder that only Sarah could answer, and in the shape of the questions I heard how badly Shield already knew it was losing. How deep. How many. How long since the drilling had begun. Whether a borehole could be fouled from within, or turned, or heard coming in time to clear the galleries beneath it. Sarah answered every one, flat and fast and without a flinch, an enemy of this Mountain telling its Shield exactly how she would take the place if she stood in the camp instead of the corridor, because she was the one who had taught the camp how. There was no time left, that night, for anyone to be only the thing they were.\par

``The council will have to hear all of it,'' Va-Sheva said, and did not slow on the stair. ``You. Her. The machine. Justice will not move a hand's breadth without the whole of it laid out in order, and in this Mountain nothing moves at all until Justice has moved first.'' She said it the way you name a peak you must be over before dark and already know you cannot be. ``They cast you out with one voice, all five agreed. And now I am to carry you back into the sealed hall, and set an Outsider at your side, and the woman who built our death at her other side, and ask the five to believe the three of you. On the night the walls begin to come down.''\par

Once, where the stair turned and the others were a few steps below and out of hearing, she spoke without turning her head. ``Is it true, what the containment cost you. Va-Elise wrote to me that you did not speak for eleven days.'' I told her that it was true. ``Good,'' she said, and there was no cruelty in it; it was Shield; it was a woman confirming that a wall had held under load. ``Then it worked, and you are breathing, and you are here, and I was right to do it, and I will not forgive myself for having been right for as long as I have left to live. Do not make me say more than that. I have a Mountain to lose tonight, and I cannot lose you as well and be of any use to it.'' And she went on up the stair, and I understood that she had rebuilt herself around the shape of me the way a tree grows around a nail driven into it young: sound in every part but the one, and the one grown over, and the whole of it strong enough now to stand in whatever wind was coming. It was not the thing I had wanted from her. It was a larger thing, and it was not for me, and I have tried to be worthy of the difference every day since.\par

She took us by the back galleries, the grey defended ones that no festival ever sees, but there was a single place where the route crossed an open stair above the common wards, and for the length of that one crossing I saw my people again. It was an ordinary evening down there. Lamps at the food-stalls. A line of small children being walked somewhere in pairs by a woman with a switch she plainly never used. Two men arguing without heat over the price of something in a basket. And the smell of the evening bread came up warm around us, the same bread that had risen in this Mountain every evening for eleven thousand years and would go on rising, every soul down there believed, for eleven thousand more, because the desert kept them and the outside could not see them and no ear alive would ever reach the Mountain. I stood on the stair above them with an Outsider's false papers still in my coat and the news of their ending still in my mouth, and I could have shouted it down to them and been cut down for it before the sentence was done, and it would have saved not one of them, because you cannot warn five thousand people against a thing they have been raised from birth to be unable to hear. Va-Sheva did not let me stop walking. ``They do not know,'' she said, very low, at my shoulder, reading me the way she read everyone. ``And they will not learn it from you, tonight, on a stair. Walk.'' So I walked, and the warm smell of the bread came up after us and thinned as we climbed and was gone. That is the last time I ever saw the Mountain at peace.\par

We had not gone much further when the Mountain took the first blow.\par

It did not feel like the drums had made me fear it would. It was not a knock. It was a blow, a single deep flat blow that came up through the stone into the soles of my feet and the bones of my jaw, and dust came down from a seam overhead the way it had come down once over a boy who told himself the Mountain was settling, and one of the wardens put a hand to the wall as though to hold up a thing that weighed more than a range of hills. Somewhere off through the rock there was a long grinding sound, and then a nearer one, sharper, the particular sound of worked stone failing along a line men had cut true a hundred generations ago.\par

Sarah had gone rigid, her head turned, reading it. ``That was not a sounding,'' she said. ``That was a charge. They have put an explosive down one of the boreholes and fired it, close, to break a way in, or to see what breaks.'' She looked at Va-Sheva. ``What is there? On the eastern arc, shallow, near the surface. What did they just reach?''\par

And Va-Sheva, who watched the spaces between things and always had, was already moving toward the sound, her voice gone hard with the first real fear I had heard in it, throwing the answer back over her shoulder as she went.\par

``Faith,'' she said. ``That is a Faith passage. That is the road to the Hall.''\par

And she was gone into the dark toward it, and Jean-Charles a pace behind her with a warden, and Sarah after them already calling for a lamp and a plan of the eastern arc; and for one breath only Paul and I stood still in the emptying chamber. I put my hand flat on the cold stone of the Mountain that had made me and unmade me and would now, it seemed, be opened by the very outside I had spent a week describing to a machine in a warm room beside a lake. Under my palm the stone was moving. Not much. A tremor, deep and slow and wrong, the settling of a thing that had never in eleven thousand years had any cause to settle. Paul touched my arm, the recorder still turning in his other hand, catching the grind of the wounded rock for whoever might one day need to know exactly how the end began, and we ran toward the lamps, and toward the sound, and toward my people, who were about to learn, all at once and far too late, that they could be reached.\par
