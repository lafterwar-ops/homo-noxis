\typesetchapter{Chapter 9}{Sky}

\begin{room}


--- The thirty-first of March; the eighth of our interviews. Before we return to your account --- did you read the \textit{Genesis} I translated for you?

--- You promised me the first chapter and gave me four; thank you, Paul, though I do not yet know whether it was a kindness.

--- I could not sensibly stop before the first murder. But keep one thing near you as you set them side by side: a shared shape tells you nothing of which bore which, nor whether either was ever true.

--- Your murderer looks like ours. You call him \textit{Cain}, son of Adam; ours is his grandson. The envy, the secret killing, the blood in the ground, the exile, the gate shut behind him --- all of it kept, and the Mountain lost. Your God forbids the woman the fruit and swears it will kill her; the serpent tells her the boundary may guard God rather than her --- which is my own craft, Paul: a second meaning set beside the first. She looks. She crosses. Her eyes open --- and your own God says she has become like one of them, knowing good and evil. My Mountain would call that an ascension. You call it a fall, and the one who opened her eyes you call evil. So tell me: why is your serpent evil?

--- Hear what you asked. To weigh whether the serpent's gift was good or evil I must use good and evil to weigh it --- which is the fruit; that story cannot be judged from outside it. You have set the apple in front of me and asked how it tastes. Your reading is clean, and too clean. He tells them they will not die, and they die --- slowly, by what they have done; he opens one hidden thing and lays another down in the opening, as Light does. Her eyes come open on her nakedness, her fear, her separation. A crossing, yes --- and a crossing may go where there is no return.

--- You are playing a trick on me, Paul --- a Faith trick, and a poor escape for a man of science. I need no fruit to judge, only wisdom; I need not be Greek to judge \textit{Eris}, nor to have eaten to judge the serpent, and neither do you. If your \textit{Attenborough} and your \textit{Darwin} are to be believed --- and I believe them, for I built the test and it stood --- these are only stories, made for a purpose, and it matters nothing which was told first. 

--- Not a trick, just one poinf of view to consider. When a hundred peoples who never met invent the same one --- the crossing, the opened eye, the gate that shuts --- each is putting a face on a true thing that happened to them all. No serpent spoke; the shape it stands for is real. So, with no fruit in it: the tellers call the serpent evil because a story needs a name for the thing that opens a door which opens one way only, and hangs on that name everything that comes after. Judge the serpent as you like. It is the door I would have you look at.

--- \ldots{} That is the part I did not want. A forbidden act; widened knowledge; the self split into the one who lives and the one who watches himself live; exile; the home still standing, and shut. I began by reading \textit{Eve} as the first of my kind to practise Light. I ended by finding that her punishment is my life. Your \textit{Cain} was the resemblance I wanted; your \textit{Eve} the one I did not. I have not finished being expelled from \textit{Eden} --- and the next part of my account begins with the reward I was given for crossing a boundary blind.

\end{room}

For several days after the martyrdom, I waited for the Mountain to feel different. It stayed the same. Water still came by measure. Children still recited badly in the teaching halls and were corrected by voices patient enough to become cruel. Shield still closed passages without explaining itself. Balance still marked loss and use with the same green dye. Faith still spoke over jars and wounds and dead bees and frightened mothers and old men who had begun to forget the names of their own children. Justice still sat where people wished no one were sitting.\par

The Mountain had changed my name, recorded my sequence, bound the white cloth to my wrist, and set me beneath Ka-Raedin in the chain of Light. Then it handed me the northern halls.\par

Syphiron had told me, in the feast among the immoral cups, that the northern halls would begin to touch me. They did more than touch me; they closed over my head. The candidate sequences came to my hand, and the apparatus rosters, and the cross-Kahir requests that Light must answer and file and answer again; the lamp accounts; the quarrels over who might stand where during which rite; the young men to be watched, and graded, and told in the exact measure Va-Raedin had once used on me that their best was acceptable. My days filled to their edges and past them. I rose before the First hour and lay down after the last lamp, and between the two there was no hour that was mine.\par

And the people went. Sela went first, and most quietly. She had carried the cloths and the little oil and watched me the way the young watch a thing they are working out, with the corner of a smile she could never quite put away. Then one morning she was no longer set to my chamber; and the next, when we passed in a corridor, she lowered her eyes and held to the far wall; and the smile was put away for good. No one had done it to hurt her. Rank had moved her to the far side of a wall that neither of us had built, and would not let her back across it.\par

The candidates I had trained beside stopped speaking when I came into a hall, and gave me the flat obedience a man gives an office and not a friend. Va-Sheva was Shield, and Shield had its own year, and the doors I might once have found her behind were shut for reasons that had nothing to do with me. The Mountain is very large. I had never known, until a piece of it was set in my hand, how large it was; nor how a man may stand at the centre of five thousand people, spoken to by all of them and touched by none.\par

One thing had always been mine, and that was the Mirror. I had stood before it in the dark since I was a candidate of Light --- days of it, then weeks, then the years run together --- until I knew every beam of it the way a man knows the inside of his own mouth, and had trained my eye to catch the smallest glimmer, the smallest movement, the one thread of light that came in where no thread should. It was the whole of my craft, and the best of what I was. In those first months as Veil I went to it, and stood before it, and found that I did not care what it did. The glimmers came, and I let them come. The eye that had been made to catch them looked, and reported, and felt nothing.\par

Ka-Raedin went on teaching me, because that was his office. One morning he set me at the wave wall to learn what light does when it passes an edge.\par

``It bends,'' I said, giving it back to him. ``It bends around the edge, and it spreads. Set a second edge beside the first, and the two spreadings meet, and where they meet they add in some places and cancel in others, and leave a banding of light and dark that no straight ray could make.''\par

``Interference,'' he said.\par

``Interference. There. That is the whole of it.''\par

And I meant, and let him hear that I meant, the rest of it: that I did not care. I did not care what light did around one edge. I did not care what it did around two. I had spent my life being told that the thing I knelt to was one thing, plain and single and holy, and edge by edge and lesson by lesson it had been taken from me and handed back a sum of parts I had never been asked whether I wished to know. I gave him the banding correctly and felt nothing, and he watched me feel nothing.\par

``You will learn the next thing after this,'' he said.\par

``No.''\par

He waited.\par

``I will not learn the next thing, Keeper. I have done the arithmetic of your chain. Every next thing you set before me is a single step toward one morning, and I have already seen that morning, because you showed it to me under a falling stone in front of five thousand people. To finish the chain is to be the hand that opens the Keeper above me below the ribs, while he bares himself to it --- as your hand did it, and Syphiron bared himself, and I knelt in my tier and watched. I will not walk toward the morning that hand is mine and the body under it is yours. Teach another Veil. Let him take my place, and rise, and hold the knife. I will stay where I am, and you will go on living.''\par

Under the refusal lay a colder thing I could not yet put into words. Everything given me as true had been taken back and returned changed. Light, that I had knelt to as the one holy substance, had turned in my hands into a sum, a wave, a thing that bent and banded and lied around an edge. The five offices I had taken for the frame of the world had a foreigner's cartoon for a twin. The gravure I had wept before as a child had a seam through it I had learned to see and could not unsee. I was of Light, and I had been wrong about the light; and with that gone, no floor under me was firm enough to be sure of anything.\par

So I stopped. The offices do not stop for a man's mood --- the lamps must be trimmed whether or not the hand that trims them wishes to be alive --- and so I trimmed them, graded the candidates, filed the cross-Kahir requests, all of it correct and empty, by rote. I ate alone, or forgot to eat. I let the questions that had once burned behind my teeth go out, one by one, and did not miss their light. I did not go to the Mirror. At night the title lay on me like a stone and I did not sleep; by day I moved through the northern halls the way a lamp is carried through a room, giving out what I was told to give, going where I was carried, wanting nothing. Around me the Mountain went on exactly as it had the morning it named me.\par

Ka-Raedin watched all of it, and for a long time said nothing. I know now what he was doing; he told me later. He was waiting. He had carried the thing himself, the year Ka-Sarem was released and he could not, for all his rank, climb free of it, and he knew there is no use reaching for a man until the man knows he is drowning. So he let me go all the way down. Six months he let me. He lightened nothing, corrected nothing, offered nothing, and let the office and the doubt and the ungrieved dead press me flat, until one morning I stood before the small black basin with the cloth in my hand and could not remember why the circle had to be clean, nor whom I cleaned it for, nor whether I would mind if I left it.\par

That was the morning he came for me.\par

``Va-Kailan of Light. Put the cloth down. It has waited eleven thousand years; it will keep a year more.''\par

He took the stool with the uneven leg and sat, and looked at me a while, and then he told me the oldest thing in the office, the thing we pass from one mouth to one ear and never write down.\par

``What is on you now has a name, boy, though you will find it in no record. We call it the ascension amnesia. It is the grand and ancient sickness of Light, and you are not the first it has taken, nor the tenth, nor the hundredth. It came for me. It came for the one who trained me, and for the one who trained him, back and back as far as the office runs. Syphiron had it the year he could not stop seeing the face of the man he had risen over, and the Keeper before him gave him the cure for it; and Syphiron gave the cure to me; and I am giving it to you now. It has hit this Mountain over and over since the first Light knelt, and it will not stop hitting it, because the office is built out of the very thing that breaks a man: you are made to see, and then shown that everything you saw was the half of it, and then handed the knife.''\par

``I have counted your years. Three a servant. Four and a half a candidate. Six months a Veil. That is eight years of Light --- eight years of the dark and the Mirror --- and in all of them no one, not I and not Syphiron and not the Mountain, ever once told you that you were allowed to be tired. If you do not wish to pierce the mysteries of the light as a wave, then do not. Give yourself the time. Remember why you liked the light in the first place, before it was a chain, and a knife, and a stone.''\par

``I am taking the northern halls off you. All of them, for a year. You will sleep by day, and I will hear no word of complaint about it from any quarter; and if a candidate must be graded, he can wait, or be graded by a man who is not drowning.''\par

``And what am I to do with the year, Keeper?''\par

``Nothing.'' He almost smiled. ``I want you to do nothing with it. I want you to waste it, end to end, and come to me at the finish of it and tell me why you loved the light.''\par

He stood.\par

``One thing more. All those years at the Mirror in the dark, catching the one glimmer in the black --- no one has told you why we make a boy do that, nor what it is training the eye to do. It is not obvious. It was not obvious to me either. Come up with me this evening, and I will show you, and then I will leave you to it.''\par

He took me up.\par

I had not known there was an up. The way to it left the last of the Light passages by a shaft I had cleaned a hundred times and never once thought to climb, and it went up through the living rock by handholds worn smooth and a stair that gave out into a chimney a broad man could not have passed. It was a hard climb, and a cold one, and long. Shield does not come here, he said over his shoulder as we went; the view buys them nothing worth the danger of being seen against the sky, and so they leave the crown of the Mountain to us. It is the one place in the world that belongs to Light alone.\par

The Mountain is a cone. Near the very top a great hollow has been cut, or was always there --- as though a vast ball had been pressed down into the point and lifted away again, and taken the point with it, and left a cone with its tip sheared clean off and a round bowl scooped into the flat of it. And in the bowl, at the crown of the Mountain, where Shield never comes, there is an observatory.\par

``No,'' he said, before the question could reach my mouth. ``It is not the Keeper's Chamber. Do not go looking for it up here.''\par

It was a low round room open to the sky, its floor worn smooth by generations of backs that had lain on it. And set on a mounting of pivots and brass rings, its long throat pointed up into the dark, stood a thing of metal and glass I had no name for. We have metal, he said, and we have lenses; a people that has both, and a reason to look up, will build one of these sooner or later. Put your eye to it.\par

``Here,'' he said. ``There is no lesson tonight, nor on any night you do not want one. The sky is yours to explore. If a question comes to you, bring it to me and I will answer it gladly. And if you only wish to look, then look --- and find again why you liked the light, before anyone told you what it costs.''\par

Then he took a bottle from inside his robe --- hydromel, the same sweet fire Syphiron had poured into my cup the night before the ceremony, the night before I watched him open himself under the stone --- and two of the small white cups, and he filled them both, and held one out to me.\par

``We have not done this,'' he said. ``Neither of us, not once. There has been an office to hold, and a young man to carry, and a Mountain to keep from its fear, and in the middle of all of it no one has said his name aloud and grieved him. So.'' He raised the cup. ``To Syphiron of Light.''\par

``To Syphiron of Light,'' I said, and my voice broke on the second word of his name.\par

We drank. And then the two of us, the Keeper and his Veil, alone in the bowl at the top of the Mountain where Shield did not come and no living soul could tell us not to, set down the cups and wept for the man we had both loved, and both, each in his own way, helped to kill. Neither of us looked away from the other. Neither of us made the other stop. For the first time since the dome came down over the swing, I was not alone inside it.\par

``Speaking of archangels.'' I finally said, and we wept some more. Together as one.

When it had passed, and the cold had dried our faces, he told me what the place was for.\par

``Every Light that ever rose has come up here to do the thing you have just done. To burn the loneliness down to something a man can lift. And to remember, when everything the office has taught you has turned to weight in your hands, the one thing under all of it that stayed only beautiful. The light. What we have always loved.''\par

Then he left me there, as he had promised, with the bottle and the two cups and the whole slow turning of the sky.\par

And so the year began.\par

I slept through the daylight and climbed at dusk, and lay on my back in the cold stone bowl at the crown of the Mountain with the long glass beside me, and gave myself to the sky. I had grown up under two shafts of borrowed sun and a Hall of lamps. I had never, in any true sense, seen the sky at all. Now I had the whole of it, every clear night, and for five months I did the one thing Ka-Raedin had ordered, and gave myself to it entirely.\par

It was past anything the Mountain had ever shown me. There were more stars than there were people in the Hall, more than there were people in the whole of my world, and they did not hold still: the entire field of them turned through the night in one slow patient wheel, all but a single one low in the north, around which the rest of heaven went the way water goes around a stone. I lay and let the field be far too large for me, and did not try to make it smaller.\par

Then, because I had been made in the dark before the Mirror and could no more stop measuring than stop breathing, I began to measure.\par

The wheel was not quite true. The one low star in the north, that I had taken for the fixed pin of it, kept a small circle of its own; even it crept, and the true centre of the turning was a point in the empty dark beside it, where no star stood at all. That small wrongness held me more than all the rest. The pattern the stars made among themselves never broke --- night upon night, season upon season, the same shapes returned undeformed, the same three points held the same angle to a hair --- and so I let the old travellers keep their beasts and heroes, and named the sky the only way I trusted: by the angles it kept. Stable triangles. Distances that did not change. A handful of sure points I could swing the rest of the heavens against. I was building, without the word for it, the thing my office had trained my eye to do --- a reckoning of the sky out of lines of sight and the angles between them, the geometry of light carried upward and laid against the dark.\par

And against that fixed lattice, a few things moved.\par

Five bright points kept no faith with the pattern. Night upon night they wandered along a single slanting road across the others, now quick, now slow, one of them so bright before dawn that I mistook it once for a lamp. I set them apart at once; my whole training was the catching of the one thing in a field that behaves unlike its neighbours. The Moon kept no faith either, and kept it differently: it changed its shape and its brightness, moved against the stars faster than anything in the sky, showed itself sometimes by day and sometimes not at all, and so I set it outside the star-field, a nearer thing on business of its own. Under the long glass a single point would open into two that circled one another; a smudge would resolve into a knot of a thousand; the brightest wanderer showed a small round disc, and about the disc four lesser lights that changed their places from one night to the next, as though they were tethered to it and swinging. My trained eye caught what a lazy eye would let pass --- that this star burned faintly red and that one blue-white, that this one held steady and that one trembled, that the dark between them, which looked empty, was only too far off to read.\par

And across the whole of it, from one edge of the dark to the other, ran a pale river of light, so crowded that no eye could pull its grains apart, splitting along its course and closing again, with dark reaches braided through the bright. I turned the long glass on it, and found no river there at all --- only stars, and stars behind those stars, past all counting, a crowd my eye had taken for a mist.\par

I measured what I could reach, and my people had given me good tools to reach with: the Year-Clock's count, the pendulum's honest beat, the sighting-frames of the practice halls. I found that the stars came back to their places a little sooner each night than the sun did, by a fixed small margin that never varied. I measured the width the Moon showed, and the width the sun showed through smoked glass, and found them, to my surprise, nearly the same. I timed the Moon from full to full, and the sun from its northmost rising back to itself, and wrote the numbers small, and trusted them.\par

Some things I could not measure, and reached anyway. The Moon was a round ball, always, and its bright part always turned toward where the sun had gone down or would rise; so I took it that the Moon made no light of its own, and only caught the sun's, and its shapes were the turning of a lit ball before my eye. The wanderers stood nearer than the stars, and the stars stood unreachably far; for however I shifted along the bowl, or waited half a year and shifted with the whole Mountain, the stars never budged against each other by the width of a thought, and a thing that will not move however far you walk to the side is a thing beyond all walking. Why the five wanderers slowed and hurried and doubled back along their road I could not solve, and I set it down unsolved, and did not lie to myself that I had the answer.\par

But the thing that took me hardest was none of these. It was the dark.\par

I had been raised to know exactly what darkness was. It was the absence of light --- the thing the lamps pushed back, the thing that filled a corridor when a flame failed, the enemy my whole people spent their coal and their mirrors and their two poor shafts of stolen sun to hold at arm's length. Down in the Mountain, light was the world and dark was the lack of it. Up in the bowl, night on night, I watched that turn itself over. Out here the dark was the whole of it, the ground of everything, running past all measure in every direction; and the light was a scatter of small bright holes pricked in it, few, and far between, and mostly failing halfway across. My people had spent everything they had to make a little light, and had called the darkness its absence. The sky told me the darkness was the thing that was there, and the light the rare exception --- a handful of sparks in a hall with no walls and no end. I lay under it and felt the floor of everything I had been taught tilt very slowly beneath me, and I did not look away.\par
