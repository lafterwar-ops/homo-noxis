Amastan could not be bought. My father wrote it in a margin, in a fury, because to Edward Blackwood a man who could not be bought was a fault in the machinery of the world. I read that margin as a daughter and as an heir. Edward had underlined the refusal twice and scored the paper on the second stroke. Beside it stood the rates offered, each doubled, then doubled again, each struck through. I gave Martinez the name.\par

Martinez spent the first two of those seven years putting a face and a tent to the name. Amastan ag Malik, descended from the older Amastan whose name he bore, was of the veiled people, the Kel Tamasheq, whom the maps call Tuareg. He moved through the deep Tanezrouft the way I move through a drum-paper, reading a country that looked empty to everyone else. He guided when he chose to, refused more often, and had turned back three of my father's expeditions without raising his voice, simply by declining to take them past a certain line and letting them understand no other man would either. Martinez brought photographs from a distance and reports bought from men who had sat at his fire. The reports named routes, kin and wells. None named the thing he kept the routes away from.\par

I gave Martinez one sentence to carry.\par

\begin{scripture}
A woman who read the ground wished to speak with the man who read the sand about a line beneath the desert that had moved on the morning of the French fire.
\end{scripture}

In the fourth year he entered, under another name and with the papers of an Oran freight broker, a transport line serving the war in northern Chad. It carried fuel, medicine, rifles and messages south from Libya; its western carriers reached the uranium camps around Arlit. He remained inside it for eleven months. A driver from Agadez brought him before a man who had shared Amastan's fire. Two years later the same line carried Martinez to Amastan himself. They met once. Amastan asked about Edward, Reggane, the yellow rock and why I had watched the pen instead of the cloud. Martinez answered and came back without a route. In the seventh year, the filing card he laid on my desk brought me to a disused pump and a boy who watched me arrive with one driver, a canvas bag and a folded coat. The boy left a folded paper on the pump. In a careful French hand not his own were a place, a season and one line.\par

\begin{scripture}
Come without the man from Oran, and without the yellow rock in your mouth.
\end{scripture}

The man from Oran was Martinez. I went.\par

At the pump Martinez took my arm. He never touched anyone. Under the noise of the engine, he told me that whatever kept the country ahead had kept it since before Rome. It wanted no money and no talking to. He could not follow, and would not, for anything I owned. I told him to wait there. He said a fortnight, and no more. If I did not come back, he would put it about that I had died of the heat, because it was tidier, and because --- he said this to the pump, furious at himself --- he had gone and grown a little fond of me, against every rule of the only trade he had. The boy drove me south. Four days later, where the track gave out, he gave the wheel to a veiled man who had been waiting without shade. The vehicle returned with the boy. I went on beside the veiled man, two more days on animals, into folds the maps drew as a single brown smear. He gave no name. Only at the seam did a voice within call him Amastan. I have not set down the route.\par

The Mountain, from the outside, is a tired shoulder of stone in a place there is no reason to go. A satellite would find no gate and no wall. The ways fold into the rock. A stranger could die of thirst within sight of a living civilisation unless a man of the sand led him to the exact seam, set his hand against it and waited. At the outer folds I loosened the reins. I heard the animal beneath me breathe and the leather move under my hands. The quiet source I had followed for fourteen years lay somewhere under the stone and made no sound at all.\par

Amastan put his hand flat against a face of rock no different, that I could see, from a hundred we had passed. A seam opened. Before I crossed, he said the one thing of his I have kept whole.\par

\begin{scripture}
Thank God every day; He has been good to you.
\end{scripture}

It was a saying of his namesake, spoken in France among the family of his friend Charles and kept by the line after him.\par

He lit an oil lamp and put it in my hand. I crossed. He closed the stone behind me.\par

The passage ran straight past three sealed crossings. At each, Shield's mark joined two stone leaves. Daylight lay across its far end. I entered a chamber cut symmetrically about a low lamp-holder, with four round pillars on either side and two stone benches facing each other. A corridor behind the farther bench continued into the Mountain, directly opposite the one that led outside. A round opening through the roof showed blue sky; sunlight made a disk on the floor beside my bench.\par

A man with scarred white eyes sat opposite me. Another stood behind the third pillar on his side, perhaps forty-five, lean, with a shaven head and pale marks across his left cheek. As I fitted my lamp into the holder, he moved far enough from the pillar to see both my hands. I sat. The blind man waited, then spoke in English.\par

``Sarah Amanda Delorme, born Blackwood, seventh of July 1932 in Greenwich. Daughter of Sir Edward James Blackwood, born in 1894 at Greenock, and Lady Beatrice Blackwood, born Beatrice Anne Calder in 1901 at Liverpool, sole heir to Calder Navigation. Widow of Étienne Lucien Delorme, born in 1923 at Nancy, physicist of the French Atomic Energy Commission, died in 1966.''\par

He turned his face towards the man among the pillars. ``You are in the presence of Va-Theran, Veil of Shield under me, and of me, Ka-Xhian, Keeper of Shield.''\par

``You have recited my life,'' I said. ``How old is this place?''\par

``We were here before the Flood.''\par

``What lies beneath it?''\par

``What lies beneath our temple is not spoken beyond it.''\par

``Then it has already spoken. I heard it at Reggane.''\par

``What did you hear?''\par

``Five strokes, twenty minutes apart, after the French motion had passed. Then a slower return through the deep rock. \emph{Rubis} gave me the sequence again.''\par

He questioned the intervals, the station clocks and the direction from which each motion had arrived. I gave him the checks I had made. He asked which other French shots had produced the sequence. I named the intervening tests and told him that none had left a clean repetition.\par

``After Reggane we knew the smaller fires,'' he said. ``The buried one could have been a nearer blow, with another still to come.''\par

``And at Reggane?''\par

``I went to see it. Va-Theran watched the ground beyond the French lines. I watched the fire.'' He raised a finger to one ruined eyelid. ``He brought me home.''\par

``Who has your drawings?'' Ka-Xhian asked.\par

``The records are lodged with solicitors in three countries. Corrected bearings, the Reggane trace, my father's failures. No route. The instructions are opened if I fail to return.''\par

``When?''\par

``A fortnight after I left Martinez at the pump. He will report that I died of heat.''\par

``Who follows?''\par

``Whoever buys what is in the packets. A government could send a survey before anyone settled my estate.''\par

``Can you close them?''\par

``By going back.''\par

He asked where Martinez had stopped and what I had sent from the road. I gave him the pump and the boy's departure. Nothing had gone north since.\par

``What did you come to decide?''\par

``Whether I had found a weapon. If I have, I need to know what it defends before I help you hide it.''\par

``You will not go beneath the temple.''\par

``Then show me what lives above it.''\par

Ka-Xhian called his Veil forward. They spoke in a language I could not follow. Va-Theran answered twice, then returned to the pillar for a folded cloth.\par

``Shield has called a drill,'' Ka-Xhian said. ``The wardens are exercising the closures. Everyone else remains in quarters. Amastan, Va-Theran and I know you are here. Keep the cloth where he puts it.''\par

Va-Theran lifted the lamp from its holder and led us into the corridor behind Ka-Xhian's bench. At the first turn he bound the cloth over my eyes. I held his sleeve; Ka-Xhian put a hand on his other shoulder. Va-Theran stopped us before each step and waited while stone moved ahead of us.\par

When he uncovered my eyes, we stood on a gallery above a round hall. Below us a great disk crossed the floor on a thread whose upper fixing I could not find. A man's face was cut into the side towards us. I counted its crossings against my watch. Marks stood in groups around its path, some freshly set beside the rubbed places of earlier ones.\par

Ka-Xhian told me that they kept their hours by it. He gave the drop in metres: one hundred and twenty-five from the upper fixing to the centre of the disk. I timed another crossing. Then he named the day's five divisions and gave me the interval of the Pendulum's turning.\par

We followed the gallery past closed doors to the far side of the Hall. The other face of the disk bore a great cat with five bared teeth. At one of the doors, a row of small sandals stood beside larger pairs. A slate rested on the lowest shelf of a rack; beside a column of sums someone had scratched a long-eared animal. I bent to look. Va-Theran waited until I straightened. I checked my watch before he covered my eyes again.\par

At the foot of the descent Ka-Xhian stopped us. ``Shield's arch,'' Ka-Xhian said. ``The Keeper's road. Va-Theran, take her through.'' I counted five chambers by the doors closing behind us. The cloth left a narrow view beside my nose. In the lamplight I caught part of a toothed wheel, then a bank of pulleys with a weight suspended between two scales. Va-Theran read out the positions. Ka-Xhian asked him to repeat one and gave a setting in return; a chain travelled, stopped, and the next door opened.\par

Farther down, paired shafts turned at different speeds above a sliding weight. I bent my head to follow the linkage and Va-Theran lifted my chin. In the last chamber he had to hold a lever while the weight settled between two marks. Ka-Xhian gave another setting. The floor trembled when the latch withdrew.\par

Under the cloth I saw the bases of two pillars. Gold picked out roofs and walls half-covered by carved sand; a narrow street continued around one of them and disappeared beneath a dune. Va-Theran kept me moving, turned me and put a hand between my shoulders until I knelt. He took off the cloth. I could make out so little beyond the lamp that I could not tell how large the chamber was. A vast iron gate stood before me, its upper edge beyond the lamplight. The same cat's face I had just seen on the Pendulum was cut into its iron, five teeth bared. My watch showed just under an hour since we had left the gallery.\par

``Beyond that gate is the Beast of Hell,'' Ka-Xhian said. ``The five hold it in its chains. If your people come to take it, this is what they will bring upon themselves. Look.''\par

Va-Theran turned me to the left. Across the void beside the bridge, on a wall at right angles to the gate, a great gravure showed the Mountain above the Beast. Five figures held its chains. Flame and water engulfed cities beyond the Mountain. He held the lamp towards it for a full minute, then let me stand and bound the cloth over my eyes again.\par

On the climb back he again stopped me at doors and steps. When the cloth came away, I was beside the bench where I had first sat. The return had taken a little over an hour. The disk of sunlight had gone; through the roof the sky was still blue, darkening at the rim. Va-Theran refilled the lamp, took a covered basket from a recess and broke bread onto a cloth between us. Ka-Xhian ate before speaking.\par

``Five offices hold the defence,'' he said. ``All five must agree before it is used. You will have no access to what each keeps.''\par

``I can turn a survey away. It takes time. By the time a drilling party reaches your folds, I would have very little to work with.''\par

``An instrument built for another purpose found us,'' he said. ``There will be better instruments. You know where they are built, which governments buy them, which surveys carry them south.''\par

``I have my father's offices. I can stop them looking for you and put them to work on the others.''\par

``Then hear Shield's offer. You return. Your instructions are sealed again. Amastan gives you one channel. When a government, survey or concession turns towards this quarter, you turn it before it reaches our sand.''\par

``And when one of our explosions alarms you, send word. I will need the date and whatever Shield saw. I can tell you what France fired and whether another shot is planned.''\par

We settled what the channel could carry. Shield would report approaching threats through Amastan. I would act outside the folds. I could ask about an approaching survey; I could not ask for an inner road or a means of operating the defence. My instructions abroad would be sealed again after my return, and Amastan would inspect the seals.\par

Ka-Xhian required three oaths. I would never bring another Outsider to the folds. I would never set the way in down in writing or in any machine that keeps a voice. If I could no longer serve Shield's purpose, I would send word by Amastan's line, withdraw, and take what I knew into silence. I said yes three times.\par

``Shield has never relied on an Outsider meaning an oath,'' he said. ``The Exception protects itself.''\par

I asked what that meant. He called Va-Theran to my bench. When I stood, stars showed through the round hole in the roof. The lamp lit Ka-Xhian's face from below.\par

``Keep the inner ways closed until Amastan reports her clear of the folds,'' he told his Veil.\par

Theran gave me the lamp and walked behind me down the straight passage. At its end he reached past my shoulder and touched the stone. It opened on Amastan, who took me by the arm and led me outside. Va-Theran kept the lamp. Amastan waited until the seam had closed, then took me to the animals. We camped beyond the nearest fold and set out north after dawn.\par

The first message of the channel came north by Amastan's own hand. On the outer fold stood one word: \emph{Horizon}.\par

I returned north without an account of the defence's working. I cancelled the release of the packets and recalled the Blackwood enquiries still directed at the folds. Horizon began as the keeping of those three oaths.\par

It began as liaison through the single quiet channel between Blackwood and Amastan: Shield heard the outside, and I was told when I strayed too near. Then monitoring: I gathered every stroke that empty quarter made and told myself Shield was safer the more completely I could hear. Then management: every geologist, satellite pass and ministry rumour, each turned gently a few degrees aside.\par

Turning curiosity required a body large enough to be seen doing other work. In the six years after that stone room I took the Blackwood Foundation, already rich and respectable, and aimed it at the work that could contain mine. It became one of the loudest defenders in the world of peoples the century was erasing: the uncontacted, the wandering, the ones a government calls an obstacle and a company calls overburden. That work was real. The Foundation sent envoys, paid lawyers, contested forced contact in courts, and broke companies off the backs of forest and desert peoples. Some are alive today. I believe they would not be without that work; the files can prove what we did, not the world in which we failed to do it. It could not command a state. It could lengthen a proceeding, expose a concession, finance the people resisting, and make an intrusion cost more than a minister was willing to pay.\par

Among its hundreds of honest files was one deliberately vague entry: a Saharan population, unnamed and unplaced, spoken for through a desert network that answered to Amastan and held behind a confidentiality no court ever cracked. The file could finance resistance to an incursion after the intruder's budget failed. Its existence taught expeditions, ministries and ore-men to expect that resistance. Every serious question about an isolated people entered a Blackwood office and climbed a chain of clerks. Anything touching one quarter of the Tanezrouft reached the last desk. I sat at it.\par

The other files could not be dummies: any hostile audit that opened one hollow protection would put a finger through the Saharan one. Each people therefore had counsel, field money, witnesses and a board prepared to spend in public. When a government waited or a company returned under another name, the case remained funded and the same clerks were still there. Some protections outlived the concealment that had first made me choose them. I signed those renewals too. The screen around the Mountain acquired obligations of its own, to people who never knew their survival had been entered beside another people's secret.\par

Horizon kept files on armies, ministries, ore-men, surveys and satellite passes. The motor race lay under \emph{amusement}. It was young then, and poor, Paris to Dakar across the width of the Sahara, run by amateurs for the love of nearly dying. Its first running had gone from Reggane away east toward In Salah. In the late autumn of nineteen seventy-nine the next route was published. I read it standing up: on the eighth of January more than two hundred machines would leave Reggane and drive due south, toward Bordj Badji Mokhtar, along a line that passed within a lost man's wandering of the outer folds.\par

I went to the Mountain a second time, and it was the last time I saw the blind man. I brought him the route and told him what I had already done, because there was no time left to ask. The Foundation had made itself, in a season, a patron of the race: medicine, recovery vehicles, liaison with the desert peoples the route would cross. Inside that apparatus I had entered drivers as gentleman amateurs and placed my people in the assistance crews, sweep vehicles and radio posts. \emph{We watched governments, geologists and soldiers}, I told him. \emph{None of us watched a race. The machines that hold the line are no danger. The lost ones are. Blackwood will be ahead of them, among them and behind them. On your side, set the fullest seal you know for the days it takes them to pass.} He said that Shield did not require an Outsider to teach it how to shut a door. Then he set the seal. I never spoke with Ka-Xhian again.\par

On the eighth of January I sat in the Foundation recovery post with three radio sets and the route pinned to a canvas wall. Two wanderers accepted fuel and a bearing back to the marked line. The third did not answer. A spotter south of Reggane reported one machine moving alone in bad light, its compass taking it towards the outer folds. He gave the bearing twice, then left the channel clear as he had been trained.\par

``White Six,'' I said into the Foundation set. ``There is a competitor east of your line. Find him. Do not signal race control unless he refuses you.''\par

White Six was a sand-coloured motor Blackwood had entered, our small crest on its door beneath the race number. It found the lost driver less than an hour from the folds. He mistook it for another competitor, accepted the line it offered and followed when it cut back west. The two machines appeared together at the recovery post before dark. The lost one continued south. White Six arrived with its steering box cracked, the left front suspension driven through its mount, and the driver holding the wheel a quarter-turn against a pull that would have killed him at speed.\par

The retirement entered the public record as a steering failure. After the race moved south, my mechanics took White Six back towards the folds and dismantled it where the vehicle could go no farther. Shield took delivery there, load by load. Amastan reported that its pieces had been stored in the Veil's Chamber. He also reported that the great closures at Reggane and during the race had both been presented as drills.\par

The race work left true papers behind it: fuel dockets, medical logs, one water reconnaissance run under the recovery flag. Its cores went sealed into a Blackwood store, and the catalogue still holds them. One hole west of the marked line gave sweet water and an assay heavy in lithium the laboratory queried twice. Nothing of it was ever published.\par

Then routes: ways in and out, cached and tested. Then mapping, with listening stations placed around the Mountain. I extended their coverage knock by knock and kept each trace with the earlier ones. By then Horizon could warn me of a traveller at the outer folds, remove him from the approach, or place another man there in his stead.\par

There was a survey, a consortium of three companies and two governments, that came within a season of running a deep line across the folds for further oil exploration. I did not bribe them. Across two years and eleven hands I let their funding develop a doubt, sent their lead geologist a flattering offer in Western Australia, and tangled their permits in a border dispute I helped to warm. Every piece was true, or true enough to survive a check; none could be traced within a thousand miles of a woman in Switzerland. The survey folded. Its people never knew Horizon had made the decision for them.\par
