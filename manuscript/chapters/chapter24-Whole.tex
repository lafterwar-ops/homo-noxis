Here the ordering hand changes. Paul assembled the account through Ravar's last line: my testimony, Sarah's confession, the live reels, the records of the five offices and the writing on the shrine wall. At his request I have written what follows for the outside, from what I witnessed and from the reports that later reached Horizon.\par

We gave Ravar silence in the cold of the shrine, with all that survived of the last wall read and the great clock turning above us. Paul marked the three chiselled fields in his pages and attempted no missing word.\par

Ka-Raedin turned from Ravar's wall to me as he spoke. ``Aye. The Mountain has carried the breach since before any Keeper took office.''\par

``The road opens only after a firing,'' Sarah said. ``Whoever used that chisel came with a surviving five --- or under their seals.''\par

``They did not silence Ravar,'' Paul said. ``They edited him.''\par

Ka-Leth looked at the damaged measures. ``For safety or obedience. The cuts do not tell us which.''\par

``Arthas Menethil did nothing wrong, right?'' Paul said.\par

``Who?'' Sarah asked.\par

``Never mind. What should Ravar have done? Wait until nobody remained who could speak his language or teach what his people knew? We have Neanderthal bones, some of their ancestry, objects we argue over. Nobody to ask. Was he obliged to accept that for his own people?''\par

I put my hand against the first wall. ``They left willingly. The women found a life they wanted. No one was attacking them.''\par

``A people can disappear through choices that are good for each person making them. Their descendants may be happy. The language still dies. You can decide that preserving it costs too much. You cannot tell me nothing was being lost.''\par

``And what could he make them pay to keep it? Some twenty-seven thousand stayed in the rock they cut. That is his number. He took children and gave them a past they had never had. They could not even name the choice he had taken from them.''\par

Paul laid his pages on the stone. ``Put those lives in the account. Put the lives that followed there too. People who had children, learned a craft, loved somebody, lived to be old. There is a man above us whose name is on that clock's service tablet. I can ask him how he repaired it. I cannot agree that finding a fragment of his work in an excavation would have been just as good.''\par

``They might have made other things. Had other children. You have no account of those lives.''\par

``No. I have these people. They have one mountain, work to do, prayers they want to finish. They should have been able to go on without ever meeting us. Sarah, you paid lawyers to keep companies off other peoples' land. You did not require them to prove that every custom was admirable. Why must these people justify their continued existence?''\par

``They need not,'' she said. ``Ravar has to answer for what he did outside it. You gave me France already. We have now read that his promise to use the weapon only for defence was a lie.''\par

I turned the leaf back to that measure. ``You cannot give him our siege.''\par

Paul read the four lines again. ``No. Nor can I acquit him of killing Ithe. He could have asked her. He chose to kill her. I can still believe the Mountain should have been made.''\par

``He made certain nobody could refuse him again,'' Sarah said.\par

``You wanted a veto too. You called it your father's appetite for dominion. Hide the Mountain, control its approaches, hold the means of stopping it. Keep those means in Blackwood, for whoever you chose to succeed you.'' He looked at her. ``You wanted to make yourself a god upon the earth, Sarah. How does it feel to have helped use the power you meant to control?''\par

Sarah took the Horizon register from under her arm and opened it. ``You think I crossed those call signs off for your benefit? I put those people at their stations. I paid them to stay. I can tell you who answered before the answers stopped.''\par

``And you will still appoint the heir.''\par

``Yes. To what remains. I wanted the veto, and I wanted it to belong to us. You have that on your tape. You will also keep what I said about the people in the flooded hospitals. My wanting power did not make them soldiers.''\par

Paul looked down at the register. ``I have kept it.''\par

``Then believe I meant it.'' She closed the book.\par

The clock continued above us. Paul gathered his pages.\par

``You hear your own decision in his,'' I said.\par

``Of course I do. I helped fire it. We rejected a chance that remained, with Daniel among the people I knew I was putting at risk. Keep that. But I wanted the people here to live, and I still do. I will not call their survival a mistake because I cannot bear my part in it.''\par

``Survival on his terms. He put a knife at the end of every Keeper's life in Light.''\par

Paul looked towards Ka-Raedin. ``That must change. I would like to help change it. I would also like to learn from the people who have kept this place alive. You asked me whether people in my world could find meaning in tending a place and saying its prayers. You wanted me to believe you. I did. On the way in I watched a baker send a child home with six loaves. I knew cakes like those from excavations. I could smell these.''\par

``Some of those people would leave if they could.''\par

``Yes. Others would stay. What they made of their lives belongs to them too, Kailan. The man who began it cannot have made every worthwhile thing they ever did.''\par

``Terrible, yes, but great,'' Paul said. ``I admire him, Kailan. He understood that every teacher would die and every tool would wear out. He provided for the teaching, the repairs, the succession. He spent lives to build it and bound generations to its keeping; he says so himself. I have never encountered a mind that attempted anything on this scale. I wish I could have worked beside him.''\par

The five offices gathered in Justice. Ka-Leth set Ravar's wall, the six cycle count and the present firing as three separate claims on wet clay. Paul placed his ordered pages beside them. Sarah laid the old Horizon register on the fifth ledge and kept her hand on it.\par

``Reverse Horizon,'' Sarah said. ``Preserve the outside account in more than one place and bring it back before another trigger.''\par

Ka-Dhavar put Jean-Charles's field radio between them. ``You ask us to give the whole to the hand that found us.''\par

``Give Horizon the cause, the six endings and the duty of return, not the firing craft. The account below opens only after the Beast. If the next warning begins in that stone, the next world is already ash.''\par

Paul objected. ``Too clean. Somebody will read that distinction as permission. Write down what was kept back and whose hand kept it, so the next reader can see the shape of the hole. Leave the absence visible.''\par

Va-Maera could not hear the room. Sa-Elian repeated each answer where she could read his mouth, and she wrote her question with one slow finger: \emph{Who keeps the keepers of the warning?}\par

Sarah read it. ``No one. Divide the copies, the money and the return roads. Horizon failed under me while I was alive and watching it. Build from that.''\par

Sela stood against the wall beside the box. Until then she had spoken only when asked to identify a seal. ``Who carries it? Not the ruling: the box. Somebody lifts it, walks it out and has to be alive at the far end. Nobody here has said whose back it goes on.''\par

Ka-Dhavar answered from Shield. ``Bring an army and Shield closes. Bring a Keeper and it becomes scripture. Bring an Outsider and Justice calls it theft.''\par

Sarah looked at me. ``Someone who can be accused of all three.''\par

Ka-Leth ruled. ``A warning after the act is a witness. We keep that witness below. A warning that can precede the act must leave under charge to return. Horizon will take the outside account under the seals of the five, preserve it, teach its danger and bring it back before the next trigger. The cause will travel with the warning. Justice will not close it again. Failure begins if the record is first read below.''\par

Before the impressions were made, Paul asked, ``If the outside answers the warning, which office receives it?''\par

No Keeper answered.\par

Va-Maera wrote once more with her slow finger before the clay had set: \emph{Was any law broken in the firing?}\par

Ka-Leth read it aloud and answered for Justice. ``None.''\par

Five separate impressions closed the ruling. Sarah's hand received no place among them.\par

Ka-Leth had carried the fourth cycle's closing tablet up with the six-count, and when the impressions were dry he read it, because Justice reads what stands nearest a new ruling. A hand from a cycle whose name we do not hold had ruled that the craft stay divided and the cause be written down whole. It had kept both below.\par

``Their ruling closed there,'' Ka-Leth said. ``Ours will carry the cause one step farther.''\par

Sarah asked how many of the six had stopped at that same step. Ka-Leth counted the tablets he had brought and gave her the number, which was three.\par

Before they lifted their hands, the five agreed that the outside account would open with a declaration addressed to Sarah and signed in their common name: \emph{The Mountain}.\par

Ka-Cheryn would not let Justice close there. She asked whether the thing could simply be taken out. In eleven days the offices tested what could be removed. Flooding the throat returned water to the hot rock; draining the reserve killed the wards; breaking the sighting instruments left the sky; erasing Faith left an answer an Outsider had already recovered.\par

At the shared air branch I held Sa-Orin's lamp while Shield closed the gate. He put his ear to the pipe and raised a hand. ``Open it.'' The warden looked at Ka-Dhavar. Sa-Orin struck the pipe with his knuckles. ``The lower ward has lost its return. Open it.'' Ka-Dhavar gave the order. The lamp had begun to dull. Sa-Orin listened against the stone until the draught returned. Nobody proposed closing that branch again.\par

Ka-Dhavar answered from Shield. ``Say a cut existed, and Balance found it tomorrow. Then I stand in the Hall and tell the people that the thing their grandmothers were born to keep is gone, that the offices they entered at twelve have no work left in them, and that the Mountain is a hole in rock with a spring in it. They would not go out. They would find a Keeper who said it was still down there, and follow him, and in a hundred years they would be doing all of this again over an empty chamber.''\par

``We have eleven days of failed tests,'' Ka-Cheryn said. ``I will enter those. The enquiry stays open.''\par

Early in those eleven days, Paul took Ka-Elun's recorded prayer to Va-Maera in the room of Faith. Sarah and I went with him. Sarah drew her stool into the light where Maera could read her mouth.\par

``Faith needs a Keeper before the reset,'' she said. ``Let Paul and me help you find the Chamber of the Last Prayer. Soundings might locate it.''\par

Maera drew back a cloth beside her. The Bowl stood in its black cradle, the pale liquid holding a thin reflection of the lamp.\par

``I found it while you were outside, Kailan.''\par

Sarah leaned forward. ``That looks like mercury. Its vapour is poisonous. Years of breathing it can leave you with shaking hands, a failing memory and sores in your mouth. Enough exposure can kill you.''\par

``Mereth's hands shook,'' I said. ``Was that why?''\par

``Possibly. I would need more than that to know.'' Sarah faced Maera again. ``If you have found the chamber, what keeps you from taking Faith?''\par

``My Candidate must find the lower chamber and rise to Veil. She has studied with me for years. She must find her own way in.''\par

``Could you have operated the Beast?'' Paul asked.\par

``Before the roof fell, yes. I know the intervals. I cannot hear the return.'' She put two fingers against his throat. ``Give me what you used.''\par

He spoke each interval he had reconstructed from Ka-Elun's prayer. She stopped him, corrected the third mark from memory, and made him begin again.\par

When they finished, Va-Maera pressed his fingers to the side of her neck and spoke the sequence back. She could never hear it again. She could teach a hearing Veil what to wait for and how to know a false answer. Sa-Elian entered the corrected sequence on Faith's tablet. Sarah's copy withheld the measures needed to reproduce the firing, named the exclusion and carried the warning. Ravar's damaged fields remained in it.\par

Va-Maera sent for her Candidate. The girl came from the wards, still wearing the grey wrap issued after the roof-fall. Paul sounded two returns into a small bronze bowl: one true, one fractionally short. The girl chose the wrong one. Va-Maera made no sign of disappointment. She placed the girl's fingers at Paul's throat, then at her own, and began with breath, pulse and vibration. The lesson continued after we left.\par

``I told you our women were freer than yours,'' I said to Paul. ``When we spoke of Eve. I was wrong. I counted the women who could command me. Their rank told me nothing about who could choose a lover, bear a child or refuse to bear one.''\par

``You asked who wrote our gospel,'' he said. ``The prayer those mothers kept came from the actor when he forgot his lines. Ravar did not write it. Then the actor began praying it himself. Why do men fight for their servitude as stubbornly as though it were their salvation?''\par

``Maera has earned her authority. That girl needs what she is teaching her. Ravar needed women to stay and bear the children who would keep his work alive. I cannot tell you which of our rules he made. The mothers could make prayers of their own and still teach their daughters the life he wanted from them.''\par

I had praised Sheva's refusal for what it had made of me. When she chose Jean-Charles, I went to her for an explanation of who I was to her.\par

An order can make people want the life it requires of them. Their wanting staffs its offices and guards its doors. Obedience brings shelter, respect and power over others; advancement depends on keeping others within the rules. That authority is real, and each promotion gives its holder more to lose by questioning. Parents want the same safety and respect for their children and teach them how to rise. Freedom comes to mean succeeding within the order. They can learn to govern other lives without seeing who has determined the terms of their own. What began as a demand upon them becomes what they believe they owe one another.\par

The outside account could leave on paper and Paul's surviving reels. An inside witness could be cut beside Ravar before the reset made its stair a wall. While those tests continued, Ka-Leth and Paul prepared two versions in Justice's stone hand: a bounded record Justice reckoned could be cut in three days, and the complete causal account.\par

Paul read the short account aloud. ``Listen to it,'' he said. ``I have kept the dates and the machinery and somehow left us out. Someone looking for a reason we had no choice could use this. I argued that we should fire. Sarah objected. I helped restore the instructions. All of that belongs here.'' Ka-Leth bent over the pages beside him and entered the rejected alternative, Paul's argument for firing, Sarah's objection, the five assents and Paul's work at Faith. ``These stay in,'' he said. ``This account can name you and let you leave.'' Paul read the additions. ``Yes.''\par

Ka-Dhavar marked the escape line on the floor. Finish the bounded text and Shield would bring Paul out. ``If you stay longer, cross when I call you,'' he said. ``Leave the tools.'' No office asked for more.\par

Paul stood over the two sets of pages for a long time. At last he laid the bounded version open between us. ``Keep that road open,'' he said. ``I have not chosen yet.''\par

``I came for the coffin,'' Paul said. ``Now I would give up ever seeing it to live here. Ravar writes that his people went gladly to ours. Here is one of ours who wants to go the other way. I want to cross to his side, Kailan. To become his disciple, and add to his work.''\par

``I am afraid,'' he said. ``I want my flat back, with all the books I never found room for. I promised Daniel I would teach him to swim. And there is a chess game on a table at Blackwood, waiting for Thursday.''\par

``With Urs?''\par

``Yes. The board has been standing since March. We have both been losing on purpose for a year.'' He looked down at the two accounts. ``I do not want to die in this cold room, Kailan.''\par

``Take the shorter road, Paul. The reader you want may never come. We have already killed the world for abstractions. I want you out of here.''\par

Before the first day ended he began the complete wall. Sa-Tavan cut beside him from the agreed pages and corrected his grip on the chisel. He cleared a ridge from one of Paul's letters with two light taps of his own chisel. Paul ran a finger through the groove, then set his tool against the next letter. I read the finished lines back while Paul checked them against his copy.\par

On the fourth night I found him asleep against Ravar's first wall, the chisel still closed in his hand. On the fifth he asked Sela to describe the ward at day forty because his reel had failed under the stone-fall. She described changing a woman's dressing, but would not repeat what the woman had whispered. ``That was for me.'' Paul wrote that the words had been withheld and asked which bed she had gone to next. On the sixth, the skin split across his thumb and Sarah bound it. On the seventh he spent three hours recutting one line after discovering that \emph{we could not stop} had replaced \emph{we did not stop}. ``\emph{Could not} is already pleading for us,'' he said. ``The wall gets \emph{did not}.''\par

On the eighth, Ka-Dhavar came with the route open and his hand on the marked line. Paul stopped. He washed the stone dust from his face, ate the bread Sela had brought and walked as far as the line. ``The wall already records the firing and our parts in it,'' I told him. ``The rest is in your pages. Leave it there. You can stay in the Mountain and live. You asked me to take you to the millwright. Come with me.''\par

``Justice has agreed to that,'' Ka-Dhavar said.\par

``I want my account beside his,'' Paul said. ``He asked for someone to know what he had done. I do. I want the next reader to know what we added. This is work I can give your people.'' He turned to Ka-Dhavar. ``I will cross when you call. And I want to stay in the Mountain afterward. I want to pray here, and learn how to do some of the work I have been admiring.''\par

``You could come with us,'' I said.\par

``I could. I have been offered a place here, and there is work I want to do. The desert and the sea frighten me too; there may be no house or meal at the other end. I know why you must take the account out. I want to stay.''\par

Ka-Dhavar repeated the hour at which the road would close. Paul marked it beside the remaining pages, then returned to the wall.\par

On the ninth morning Ka-Leth read us the new succession entry: Maera's Candidate had found the lower chamber and risen to Veil; Maera had taken Faith.\par

On the last morning Sa-Tavan finished the lower panel. Paul's hands shook too badly to hold the pages, so I turned them while he read the complete account back against the stone. Sa-Tavan followed with his chisel and corrected two letters.\par

The final passage was his own name. He first cut it smaller than the others, then recut it at the same depth as mine.\par

Paul put the chisel beside Sa-Tavan's tools and slid the pages into his bag. Sa-Tavan wrapped the blades. We carried them up together. At the marked line Paul stopped and looked back down the stair. Ka-Dhavar waited beside him. After a moment Paul crossed, leaning on my arm.\par

At the hour Balance had fixed, the offices counted their people above the line. Paul stood beside me with the dust still white in his hair. The counterweight began to move.\par

The stone met the stone. The reset opened water to the lower wards, drew air through the sealed city and returned the year faces to their first marks. The five office doors closed. The road to Ravar disappeared.\par

The sound travelled upward after the door vanished: weights taking load, distant water, five shutters seating one after another. In the wards people cheered when the air strengthened. A woman brought her newborn into the corridor and held him toward the returning lamps. Sa-Elian began the first-day blessing. Ka-Maera stood beside him, reading his mouth, and did not stop him.\par

Ka-Leth carried the tablets from Justice into the permanent record. When the blessing ended, Paul stopped Sa-Elian. ``Would you teach me the prayer for the dead?'' he asked. ``For the people outside. I helped kill them.''\par

Sa-Elian brought him a stool. He gave the prayer a line at a time, waiting while Paul repeated it. When Paul lost a word, he began the line again. I sat with them until the ward hands came to change the lamps.\par

Before we left, I took Paul to the millwright whose name was on the service tablet. The man had a wheel clamped to his bench and a row of pale replacement teeth laid on a cloth. He gave Paul the worn pieces to number. Paul set his notebook beside the cloth and asked which face had run against the iron.\par

Before we left the lower wards, Ka-Raedin asked whether I would take Light's road again and complete the search for the Keeper's chamber.\par

``I cannot. I hold the outside account.''\par

``Then Light's chain ends.''\par

``The chamber is inside the great reserve. That much I found at Blackwood. I think a drowned passage leads to it. And what it holds---is it a sphere, with the pale metal from the Bowl of the Last Prayer on its surface?''\par

``Horizon carries the outside chain,'' I said. ``Light's remains yours.''\par

Ka-Raedin wrapped the old gold thread around his hand. ``Then I must find another pupil.''\par

I went to fetch Sela from the bread crossing. The woman who kept the ward tally stood in her place, with two loads waiting and the passage full of people coming up. Sela raised a hand, then lowered it as the woman stopped one load and sent the other through.\par

``We are ready,'' I said.\par

``Then carry that one. I told her I would stay for the change of shift.''\par

I took the board. When I returned, Sela was putting on her pack. The woman caught her sleeve, pulled her close and kissed her cheek before sending the next load past us.\par

We put our packs beside one another at the south doors. Sarah looked from the two packs to us. ``I assume she is part of the inheritance.''\par

``No,'' Sela said. ``He is part of mine.''\par

Sarah inclined her head.\par

Sela tightened the rope around both packs, tested the knot and made me do it again when mine slipped. She would keep the water tally because I forgot to drink when thinking. I would carry Paul's box on the steep ground because her left knee had never healed straight after the fall. If either of us wished to turn back, we would say it before making silence into consent.\par

Amastan came from the folds with ash caught in the seams of his robe. He gave Ka-Dhavar the depth of drift at the first well and asked for four more water skins. ``I can take them as far as the lower cache. We must look beyond it before I promise another stage.''\par

``You will need someone to learn the roads,'' Ka-Dhavar said.\par

``When I have found which ones remain. Choose someone who can wait and listen. I will take them on the next journey.''\par

His wife brought the children to the packs. ``We stay here,'' she said. ``Come back before you try to reach the northern wells. I want to hear what you found.'' Amastan crouched before the children and spoke to them below my hearing. When he stood, she gave him his carrying cloth.\par

Va-Sheva chose to stay, and Justice granted her leave to bear her child in the Mountain. Jean-Charles had died holding its inner gate. She came to the south doors with Ka-Dhavar on one side and Ka-Maera --- Va-Maera newly risen as Keeper of Faith --- on the other.\par

Ka-Maera put two fingers against Va-Sheva's throat and her other hand over the life beneath the dark cloth. What she spoke was almost too low for me to hear. I caught the old stations only: the Eye Above named first, the Beast Below answering, the Stone Between; then an appointed boundary, that through which a thing would continue, and mercy not interrupting justice. The order troubled me. I had heard it before, or words close to it, but by then I had spent too many hours in Faith's halls and heard too many prayers to place which one had returned.\par

Ka-Dhavar drew his Shield blade, laid it flat across both palms and lowered his head. ``Shield concurs with Faith. We will carry out the penalty.''\par

Va-Sheva looked from Faith to Shield. ``I understand.''\par

Then she put her palm once against my chest, counted me among the living and went back into the Mountain between them. I did not follow.\par

At the first bend she looked back. Her eyes passed me and settled on the light from the open south door falling across the floor. Then the stone took her from sight. I believed Ka-Dhavar had agreed that she and the child must remain within the appointed boundary. I did not understand why he had drawn the blade, or why Va-Sheva had answered without looking at me. I have not written what came after, because I did not witness it. I record the prayer, the blade across his hands and her answer. It is all in the past now, and I will not reread my own story.\par

Years after we left, the forest began to return. I did not see it rise. The ashkeepers charted it at the high slots, season by season, and their copies came to Horizon late by the old road. The second and fifth years passed over bare ground. Then came a grey-green stain, scrub, and much later something a child could call a tree.\par

The first report reached us on a strip of cloth carried inside a water jar. The ashkeeper had drawn the old horizon line in charcoal and marked seven shoots where the western ground had held none. Sarah read it twice. She asked whether the scale was reliable, whether the keeper had mistaken salt brush and whether the pump records survived. Two more reports arrived before she let herself believe it.\par

She did not smile. She put the cloth beside Edward's Horizon register and said, ``One useful thing. At last.'' By then her hands could no longer close the clasps on Paul's box. Sela did it for her.\par

The reports named buried water, seed-caches and pumps without a state to own them. One damaged ledger reached us stiff with salt. Its last complete entry read: \emph{Opened before the appointed year. Thirty-two at the gate without water. Seed counted after the living.} Beneath it, another hand had entered nineteen names and two births.\par

A later packet carried three lines copied from a survivor's flour sack: \emph{The woman from the north showed us how to roof the seedlings. She died before the first leaf. Her name was Meriem. Keep it with the well.}\par

No order travelled intact. A cache opened early and was eaten. One pump drew salt. Two guides disappeared with the seed they carried. Elsewhere people who had never heard Sarah's name found the marked wells and did the work because water and shade were useful.\par

Faith stood beneath the gravure and said the promise had been kept: God had remembered the forest and made the desert withdraw. The people wept and repeated the promise. Beyond the gravure, human hands carried seed between marked wells.\par

The outside account passed from Sarah's hand into mine and from mine into divided hands. One keeps the reason, another the sealed route; neither holds the complete return until it is due. Paul's engraving waits until the act is complete. If the next hand first learns the truth there, we have already failed. The box beside me carries his first leaf and the impressions of all five offices.\par

On the morning we left, I stood beneath the Pendulum in the reconsecrated Hall among people who still knelt before it as the face of God weighing the world. The reset had returned its slow count.\par

I knelt one last time in the posture my people used to count grief, terror, gratitude and time.\par

The great disk crossed, turned and crossed again. I rose before it returned.\par

I went out with them. Behind us the Pendulum continued without my witness.\par

Keep your distance. Live.\par

\emph{The Mountain.}\par
