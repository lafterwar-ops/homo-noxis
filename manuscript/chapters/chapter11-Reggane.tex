I was twenty-seven when the French lit their first bomb in the Sahara, one of perhaps forty people in the world positioned to hear it properly. I was bored.\par

France was retreating from empire and testing a weapon above land it still called its own. A tower and device stood in the desert. The device had the harmless name \emph{Gerboise Bleue}, the blue jerboa. The briefing called its light unprecedented. To the instruments it would be enormous and simple. I knew the broad shape it ought to draw.\par

The camp worked. Men queued with enamel mugs; a corporal spent three days making an icebox hum without cooling; petrol found the taste of everything. Each morning a clerk drove past with melons that split before anyone authorised their distribution. Beyond the wire, drivers slept under lorries while mechanics rebuilt machines from the dust inward. Forms moved between every part of it.\par

At our recording station, Moussa Belkacem was paid as an instrument mechanic. He could level a galvanometer by listening to the table and tell from axle dust which road a lorry had taken. He came from the Touat, had cousins along routes the officers called empty, and corrected maps printed north of the sea with a laugh.\par

The afternoon before the test, I said the southern drums could remain idle because there was nothing in their sector worth wasting paper on.\par

“Empty? Old salt road. Two bad wells. One good well, if its owner trusts you. Three families last month; gone now because the aeroplanes frightened their goats. Your map arrived after they left.”\par

Moussa reached past me and switched on the southern instrument.\par

My husband laughed when I told him. Étienne Delorme was nine years older than I was, a physicist, and one of those scarce men the new French state gathered as it gathered uranium --- rare, refined at great cost, and handled by men who did not understand him and could not do without him. He owned a narrow chapel of the device and spoke as though the cathedral had been built to keep rain from it. He had put my name on the list for Reggane; the clearance was his doing, remaining there mine. I was better than half the men at reading the small motions they discarded. In public he said so. In private he checked my calculations twice, then put his hand on my shoulder before returning them.\par

Years earlier he had explained a chain reaction with dominoes set upright across a tray: one small piece, three behind it and nine behind those, each rank taller and heavier than the one before. He tipped the smallest. Three fell, then nine, and the last rank struck the tray with a weight the first piece could not have supplied. “The first one supplied almost nothing,” he said. “It supplied permission,” I answered. He kissed me.\par

Edward Blackwood, my father, moved buried wealth from where it lay to where it would sell, and the Sahara was one of his locked cellars. Yet one reach of it defeated him. Surveys bent around it; guides altered their routes; money returned as apology, or did not return. He pursued the blank for thirty years and died with the loss entered under six respectable headings.\par

The records occupied a real cellar beneath the Blackwood offices. Its door bore our black wax mark: a winter tree with roots and branches made alike, so that on a crate overturned at a port the tree still stood. I had been forbidden that room as a child. Edward called its contents unfinished business. Six headings in his annual accounts carried the losses.\par

The domino tray stood in Paris. Edward's survey failures lay in the cellar beneath the Blackwood offices. I kept them separate.\par

Ground motion carried distinctions. A natural quake arrived untidily and continued arguing with itself; an explosion entered hard through compression and left a poorer later train. One read the whole disorder and asked what cause could most cheaply have made it. Nature did not ordinarily wait twenty minutes, strike again at the same weight, wait another twenty and stop at a number. Five equal strokes at exact intervals required selection and a clock.\par

On the morning of 13 February, Étienne found me checking the southern bank as the final count came from the tower. “For once in your life, look at the thing itself.”\par

“I know what it will do.”\par

“No one knows.”\par

“You have spent three years telling ministers that you do.”\par

One side of his mouth lifted. “Whatever you think of the men, Sarah, watch what they have made.”\par

I stood with him at the shelter opening. Eight faces turned towards the tower. Mine was the ninth, but at the last moment I looked south. Moussa had made me ashamed of the word \emph{nothing}.\par

The light passed through the dark lens, the canvas and the lids of my closed eyes. Every seam became black; each body became an absence cut from a white world. Though I had turned away, my shadow leapt ahead across the floor, sharp at the shoulders and pointing south, beyond the tower and the roads on the operational map.\par

Then the ground moved beneath us with the unity of a decision. Tables walked; glass touched metal in a hundred alarms. An auxiliary recorder reached the edge of its stand. I caught the frame against my hip and put my left hand across the turning drum while Moussa drove a wedge beneath its leg. The stylus crossed my thumb, opened it and went on writing. Blood fell two finger-widths beyond the line. I spent years avoiding the stain when I unfolded it.\par

The sound came late enough to be absurd. Dust left the roof; outside, one of the ripe melons burst, and for several seconds the greatest weapon France had made smelled of fruit. Men cheered. Étienne watched the tower's country rise into itself and said, too quietly for anyone but me, “God.” Moussa was already checking the anchors. When a soldier told him the station had held, he answered, “Then it will not mind being checked.”\par

The great signal filled the useful papers. Men marked times; cigarettes appeared; the event diminished for more than an hour into reflections through layers no one could see. I changed the bandage on my thumb and remained beside the southern recorder.\par

An hour later, the first small stroke entered beneath the tail of the great one: a clean compression, far below the scale of the shot, arriving from the south. I marked its time and waited. Twenty minutes is not a silence when one is watching a line. It is long enough for a mark to seem accidental, for a man to enter, smoke and leave, for attention to become embarrassment. At the twentieth minute the second stroke came at the same weight. I stayed. At forty minutes the third arrived. The fourth struck at sixty, where my pencil said it must. The fifth did the same at eighty.\par

Then nothing.\par

I marked a sixth point twenty minutes ahead. At one hundred minutes, where another clean stroke should have begun, a broad low return spread across the instrument, as though the five small acts had gone down through the world, touched something too large to strike, and come home wearing its breath. It lifted, held and sank.\par

Five strokes. Five waits of twenty minutes. One return.\par

I checked clock, drive, stylus, anchoring bolts and paper feed, then put my fingers to the housing for an engine transmitted through the table. Nothing moved but the drum. The event had come through the ground, from beyond the test site and the roads on the operational map. In the rough log I entered five marks and the return, and left the source column blank. On the line above, beside the tower shot, a clerk had written \emph{SUCCESS}.\par

Étienne asked that evening whether my neglected bank had produced anything worth its paper. I said it had recorded a frame fault after the shot. He kissed the clean knuckle beneath my dressing and said, “At least history has marked you.” The original sheet was already inside my shirt. I slept with the first proof of the Mountain against my ribs and my husband's arm across the place where I had hidden it.\par

The theft measured seventy-two centimetres. Next morning the station chief stood by the shortened roll with Moussa on one side and me on the other. I said blood had fouled the tail and I had removed it before it contaminated the feed. Then he asked whether the five marks in the rough log came from the damaged frame.\par

“No,” Moussa said. “The frame was true.”\par

The chief looked at me, then at the personnel sheet carrying my clearance and my husband's unit. “It shifted under the aftershock.”\par

“Not this machine,” Moussa said.\par

He would not alter the maintenance slip. They moved him to the vehicle shed. An objection from me would have reopened the missing paper, so I watched him carry out his toolbox. At the door he set his washed handkerchief beside my bandaged hand and asked whether I still wanted the southern bank running. I said yes. “Then leave it running,” he said, and went.\par

No charge was entered against him. His name vanished from the instrument roster and appeared beside vehicles.\par

I never saw him again. I do not know what the test put into his body or how long he lived with it; the records do not agree. The three families he had counted in that sector were entered in no record I have ever found, and I have looked. \par

One event may be a fault, a vehicle, a coincidence of reflections, a clock feeding its pulse into an amplifier. I had stolen the trace because I wanted it extraordinary. For four years I tested every ordinary cause I could devise.\par

I obtained another recorder of the same make and loosened its frame by measured amounts. I ran a generator against the wall, drove laden trucks past at different speeds, damaged a bearing, mistimed the feed and bled current into the clock. Through Étienne I obtained engine schedules, blasting logs and vehicle movements; every plausible source failed on place or time. Most dangerously, three other stations had kept enough of the test's tail to catch portions of my event. One lost two strokes; another clipped the return; the third was badly levelled. Together their bearings crossed roughly in the south and proved my recorder had not invented the marks.\par

Moussa's maintenance slip read: \emph{Anchors checked after shot. Frame true. No mechanical settling observed.}\par

Once a month I unfolded the original beneath the same lamp. I measured the gaps, checked the ink against my copies and put it back. The paper yellowed; the blood darkened to old tea. Repeated handling made every fibre familiar and added no evidence.\par

The second event came in the fourth year. There had been other French shots between and no clean repetition; that absence nearly cured me. Then \emph{Rubis} was fired underground in the Hoggar, coupling more of itself into rock. After its hard motion crossed the stations, a southern instrument under my supervision drew five small strokes at the old twenty-minute interval and the same broad return twenty minutes after the fifth.\par

I locked the room, took the Reggane sheet from my case and laid one trace over the other against the window. Different instruments and different paths had altered the lines; they did not coincide like printing. The count, twenty-minute measure, sharp sources and slow answer survived every difference between the drawings. I was sick into a metal bin and cleaned it before anyone could ask why.\par

I returned to the window. Two different events had preserved the same deliberate sequence. Somewhere in the southern blank, a mechanism could put that sequence into the ground and receive a shape through the deep Earth. It woke rarely, after great disturbances but not all of them. I inferred that its operator selected the disturbances it answered.\par

Ka-Xhian of Shield later explained the selectivity. Reggane gave Shield its first local nuclear signature; smaller tests could thereafter pass as known events. \emph{Rubis}, driven into rock, arrived hard enough to resemble a nearer deliberate strike whose second blow might still be coming. Shield answered the possibility of that second strike.\par

There were also later sequences without any Outsider blast. I recorded every one under the same cause. Kylian's succession account in this room supplied another: a Keeper dead, another hand in office, the five proving the transfer. The dates in my files remained without names.\par

The traces enclosed a region larger than some nations. Waves bend through structures one has not mapped, hasten under hard rock, slow through sediment and arrive wearing the country between. My first crossing carried an error of two hundred kilometres.\par

So I began to build ears.\par

Some were scientific stations for research into the Saharan crust. Some were commercial: Blackwood companies already searched for ore, oil and water. Some were absurd on purpose—a study of road vibration, quarry insurance, instruments in extreme heat. Each had a reason that survived inspection. After Algerian independence, I paid for proper agreements and for useful work: several ventures found water, two found ore, one built a road a provincial office had awaited for nine years. The black tree appeared on salary books, water crates and geophone cases across the desert. I kept the transparent map on which their listening arcs met.\par

No crew was asked to bend a reading. I concealed why each ear stood where it did, and separate crews could not compare their placements.\par

The first bearings were wrong. They drew us to a black volcanic ridge more than a hundred kilometres east of the later solution: broken ground, no permanent settlement, approaches visible for miles. Three parties had turned back from it. I sent a mineral team beneath the tree and went with them.\par

I refused to wait for the dust season to pass. On the sixth day, a water lorry broke a rear spring in soft ground. As we unloaded it, a wall the colour of old copper rose behind us. Hamid Ould Ali, the local foreman, gave us six minutes. No one argued. We turned the lorries across the wind, cabled their wheels and buried the instruments beneath canvas. The front reached us before the last knot. Day became brown pressure. Sand found the scar in my thumb, though the cut was four years healed.\par

One water drum tore loose. I ran after it because it held a third of our supply. Hamid caught my shirt and put me face-down behind a wheel. “We can lose water. I cannot bring the director back in pieces and still be paid.” After the air cleared, the drum stood crushed against a shelf of rock.\par

In the ledger he entered \emph{one drum: wind}; beneath it, \emph{director left shelter: own decision}. I said the second line was not an expense. He said it had nearly been the largest one. The line remained.\par

We spent nineteen days around the ridge, firing controlled charges at measured points. Several paths seemed to place the hidden source under its northern shoulder. We opened an old prospect and found mineral stain and desiccated French timber. Then a western charge reached two stations in an order impossible through uniform ground. A buried hard body beneath the ridge bent arrivals from farther west and placed their apparent source under its northern shoulder.\par

Once measured, the distortion corrected the old bearings. The ring contracted towards the reach my father's ventures had refused to enter.\par

The corrected bearings sent me to Edward's cellar. The executors called the seal ceremonial. His key opened the lock; black wax still joined door to frame. I put a chisel through the trunk of the tree.\par

Inside were damp masonry, paper and the tobacco Edward had forbidden everyone else to smoke near records. Survey rolls filled pigeonholes; tin cases carried the tree and a venture number. Nothing had moved since his last winter. Every failed venture remained.\par

For eleven days I read. Separately, each failed venture had a practical explanation: a guide's refusal, missing signatures, dust across an aerial strip. Laid beneath the corrected bearings, the explanations formed a perimeter. Successful ventures were precise—assays had coordinates, wages met names, a lost truck ended in a police report. Here, routes bent before a boundary without recording an obstruction, and adjoining aerial strips disappeared in different years. Every marked route stopped at the same ring.\par

The clearest voice belonged to a Scottish geologist, Alasdair Kerr. Ordered to cross the ring, he wrote: \emph{The guide will take us north, south, or back. He will not take us east. I doubled the rate. He returned the money. I threatened to proceed without him. He sat down and wished me a safe journey.}\par

Edward sent another guide. Kerr's next report ended: \emph{We are not being prevented. We are being allowed to decide against going.} On a third attempt he described lights without a camp, tracks crossing his own, and the same man at different wells who never acknowledged him. None could be separated from fatigue. Kerr returned north, drank until Edward dismissed him and would not discuss the country sober.\par

Edward's later notes listed guides, trading families, fuel purchases and marriages between routes. Beside three failures, years apart, he had written the same name: first with a question mark, then underlined, then hard enough to score the sheet beneath. Amastan.\par

Edward's notes ended with that man. I wrote his name on a clean card and began tracing his debts, kin and roads home.\par

(Paul): --- The recording tells Montel what Kylian heard. The tutor can tell him what changed Kylian.

(Sarah): --- Locate him without calling. Take his telephone and hold him at a Blackwood property; if he refuses, restrain him.

(Kylian): --- Then my instruction stops.

(Sarah): --- For Easter, certainly. Perhaps altogether. We use the empty house to take every record we can: Paul keeps the machine, Elise the reels, Urs the door.

(Kylian): --- Before you go: why does an oil trader speak Noxius?

(Urs): --- Exploration geophysics. Finding oil is easy; moving it through embargoes and wars is the trade. Sarah needed a man from your Mountain moved without a translator or a road back. So I learnt Noxius.

(Paul): --- Montel's photograph goes beside the Reggane trace. Sarah has given us the first road to Amastan; before she gives the second, Kylian gives the door he chose after finding Sarah's photograph inside Shield.

(Kylian): --- The choice I called wisdom because I would not call it fear.

(Sarah): --- Hold the fold open. Begin at that door.
