\typesetchapter{Chapter 17}{Mock}

\begin{room}

\--- Then it is not a rising.

\--- It is a rising. I have stood in the room where that shape is made. I would know it with my hands cut off.

\--- It is the shape of a rising, and it is not a rising, because nothing you have ever told me rises twice inside an hour, and it is going to draw a third. Watch the drum. There. No Keeper of yours died three times before this lamp was lit. Something is making your Mountain say the word, over and over, and it is not tiring.

\--- Madame. The interval is holding. Eleven minutes, near enough, between one and the next. It does not vary.

\--- Of course it does not vary. A death does not keep an appointment. A machine keeps an appointment. \ldots{} Bring me the drill cabinet. All of it, back to the first paper I ever folded, and the great seals with them. Now, please; and quietly.

\--- I do not understand what I am looking at, and it is my trade to say so out loud. You have told me for two days this shape is one of his Keepers dying, and another taking the office. Now it comes every eleven minutes, and Keepers are not dying every eleven minutes. So the shape is lying about what made it. What makes a true thing lie?

\--- A forgery makes a true thing lie. Someone out there has found the way to make this Mountain answer, and is striking at it, and standing back, and writing down what comes back.

\end{room}

The machine kept turning while she said that, so I will tell the rest of that night the way it came to me, because after that hour there was no longer a man in a chair and a man with a question between us. There was a house waking in the dark, and five thousand souls far to the south who did not know that somebody had found their door and begun to knock upon it.\par

They brought the cabinet, and she laid the papers out under the long lamp, sixty-six years of them, and set beside them the new sheet the drums had drawn while we argued, and read them the way I had watched her read me: fast, and cold. Paul stood at her shoulder. I stood where I could see her face and not the drums. Hers, which had not moved once in a week of confessing to a lifetime of theft against my people, moved now.\par

``These,'' she said, her hand flat on the old folded papers, ``are the true ones. Every one of them small, and rare, and years apart, and each one a death. And this'' --- the new sheet --- ``wants to be their brother, and it is not. Look at the tail of it. A true knock strikes and the Mountain answers once, deep, and rolls over, and sleeps. This one strikes and the answer does not come from under. It comes from everywhere at once. They made it around the Mountain.''\par

``Around it,'' Paul said. ``You mean the source is outside. Not in the Hall.''\par

``I mean,'' she said, ``that someone has set machines on the desert floor to shake the ground on purpose, and buried rings of listeners around them to catch the way the Mountain gives it back. They settle their plates, they sing one careful sweep into the rock, the buried ears read the echo, they lift and move a little way and do it again. Eleven minutes, plate to plate --- that is not the Mountain's pace, it is theirs: how long it takes a careful crew to work one station and move to the next.'' She straightened. ``It is called sounding. You throw a known noise at a thing you cannot see into, and the echo draws you its insides. It is how I have mapped a hundred ore-bodies I never laid an eye on.'' She looked like a woman to whom something had been done. ``Someone is mapping the inside of your Mountain by making it ring.''\par

I understood it in the only shape I had, the Bowl of the Last Prayer, that hands a true note back whole and answers a false one with silence. I had thought it holy and small. Now a stranger sat before the Ear of the whole Mountain, singing it every note in turn and setting down which the stone would hand back.\par

And that is the part that stopped my breath, and I said it aloud into that room full of clever frightened people. The answer, not the blow, wore the shape of a rising. They had struck nothing holy; they had sung note after note into the rock until one fell within the band the buried mechanism was made to answer. The Mountain handed back the figure I knew from the mornings when a Keeper took the office --- five ordered returns and the slower thing beneath them. That figure was not the lawful blow. It was how the Mountain answered when the five woke it from within. The strangers had not learned the note; their sweep had merely crossed it. By changing the sweep and moving their machines, they made each return tell them more: that something was made down there, and where its heart lay.\par

\begin{room}

\--- Say the danger in one sentence, so that I have it clean on the record, whatever the record is now for.

\--- They are using a stolen echo to map the one chamber that must never be found; and there are soldiers already on the ground, waiting to walk in the day the map is finished.

\end{room}

She did not leave it at the one sentence, because Paul asked the question under it, which was how long, and she would not soften it to be kind. ``Wrong question,'' she said. ``This is not a fuse burning down. His machines cannot loose the thing; that takes the five stations and the buried engine. A sweep from the surface can make them answer; it cannot make them act. What his machines can do is finish the map. He varies it --- a new note, a new amplitude, a new place, a new setting of the plates --- and every pass sharpens the picture: the galleries, the stairs, the water, and the one deep answer that should not be there at all. When the picture is good enough he will know not only that the Mountain is real, but where its heart lies, and how a rig and a company of men might reach it. The rig is already on the ground. The perimeter is already dug in. So the clock is not the stone's. It is the surveyor's, and it runs until his chart is finished and his soldiers walk in.''\par

The house came apart into work after that, and I stood in the middle of it and watched people I had thought were furniture become an army. Jean-Charles came in with paper still warm from the printer, and he was not smiling, which frightened me more than the drums had, because in all the days since he carried me out of the desert he had never once stopped smiling.\par

``It is worse than the ground,'' he said, and laid the warm paper down over the drum-sheets. It was an image made by a machine that reads the ground by radar and does not care whether the sun is up --- taken in the dark, over the pale empty folds of the only country I had ever called the world. And on the nothing there were things that did not belong to it. A camp, laid out in the straight and thoughtless lines of the outside. Squat, heavy vehicles I had no name for, that Sarah named in a voice gone flat: ``Vibrator trucks. Those four are the source.'' Thin lines ruled across the sand away from them, dotted at even intervals --- ``and those are the ears, laid out to listen. The trucks alone draw nothing.'' A drilling rig. The fine lattice of little marked shot-points. And around all of it, in a ring, smaller shapes she named more slowly still. ``Those are not surveyors. Surveyors do not travel with those. That is a perimeter. Someone has put a soldier's fence around a scientist's camp.'' And along the second ridge, the one my people had always trusted because no road reached it, more shapes yet, dug in, patient, facing inward.\par

Then she went very quiet, and put one finger on the lattice of little marked points where the trucks were set to do their sounding, and said, in a voice with the cold gone out of it and something much worse in its place: ``I drew that grid.''\par

No one spoke.\par

``That is my lattice. That is the spacing I worked out, the one that gets you the deepest return off a body that size, the little private way I mark a shot-point, never taught to more than four living men. Someone in that camp learned to survey at my knee, or at the knee of a man who did.'' She took her finger off the picture as though it had burned her. ``No one stumbles onto that. This is my own hand, come loose from me, and gone south without me, and knocking.''\par

Paul had stood at the edge of the lamplight through all of it, and now he drew the three of us apart to the cold of the window and pitched his voice under the noise of the house. ``You have had the one photograph since the morning you came to this table,'' he said to her. ``What I should have set beside it days ago, and did not, is this. When I came into this house you told me to make a friend of your right hand and keep close to him. I did; and I was not going to spend that friendship on a thing I could not yet read.''\par

``Three things,'' he said. ``There is a lead spliced off the machine that takes the boy's account, fine as a hair, running into the wall and to no room this house will own --- laid last spring, by a hand that knew where your ears were and wanted its own set beside them. I pulled it. Twice since, the night-reels I locked away myself were not the reels I unlocked: the same boxes, the same weight of tape, and not the tape I wound. And twice, before the light, I have watched a car with black glass and no country come to the low gate, take what your right hand carried down to it, and be gone before he had climbed back to the house.'' He turned his empty hand over. ``Each of them nothing. Laid together, they would not lie down.''\par

I gave them my one small thing. ``My first night here I listened to this house as I would to the Mountain, and it hummed in its walls with a steadiness I distrusted. A house being listened to hums the way a man holds his breath.''\par

She heard them out without a word, and then her face did what I had watched it refuse all week: she looked once across the room at the man bent smiling over the drum-sheets, took her eyes off him with a care worse than any naming, and did not send them back. ``A fence on my second ridge,'' she said, to the glass, ``a wire in my wall, a plate with no country at my gate. A frightened surveyor does none of these; a rival house one of them, and clumsily. All three, patient, in step, is no thief who has robbed me. It is a state that has decided it owns me, and is only tidying its paper.''\par

\begin{room}

\--- Who.

\--- I will not know for certain until I have a name, and I do not give names I am not certain of, even to you. But I will give you the shape, because you have earned it. There is a part of the French state that has wanted this desert for as long as my family has worked it, and has spent sixty-six years being politely fed the crumbs I chose to drop. Martinez fed them for me. He is old, and I have outlived my use to the men he truly serves, and they have copied my method and have no further need of me. He warned me once, thirty years ago, at the edge of this same desert --- and then, this last time, he held the door and let it shut on me. Lately my crumbs stopped being enough for them, and they took the loaf: my grids, and much of what Horizon knew, because old houses leak. And they have decided, being soldiers and not scholars, that the way into a locked mountain is to knock on it until it answers, with no idea, none, that the thing they are teaching to answer is the one thing I spent my whole life making certain no single hand could ever reach.

\--- \ldots{} And I will say one thing more that I did not plan to say tonight. For sixty-six years I watched this alone and never once caught it, because catching it wanted two ears I did not have. It wanted a man raised inside the thing, honest enough to stand under that knock and call it a made thing and not a god --- that is you, Kailan, years before you sat in my chair, on a drum you never knew I kept. And it wanted an outsider with no stake and a good machine, to lay your morning against mine and see that one hand had drawn them both. That is you, Mr Morgan. I built my whole life so that no two people would ever hold enough of this between them to see it whole; and you did it at my own tea table inside a week. I ought to be furious.

\--- And you are not.

\--- No. I am something I have not been in a long time, and I have decided, tonight, not to be ashamed of it. We are, the three of us, for the first time and most likely the last, on one side of a thing. Try not to get us all killed proving it.

\end{room}

I have wondered since whether she told the truth in that room, or whether she was only trying to take back a machine stolen from her by cruder men. I have decided it does not matter a hundred times, and it has never stayed decided.\par

Paul found me at the window a little later, while she was in the next room speaking down a telephone in a language I did not own, and he kept his voice low. ``We are about to cross a desert on the orders of the one person who has told us, to our faces, that she wants the thing under it for herself. You have understood that.''\par

``I have understood it.''\par

``And?''\par

``And she is the only soul who can put us there, and she wants the soldiers off my Mountain as fiercely as we do, for reasons that are bad. I have spent my life among people whose reasons were good. My own council's reasons were spotless, and they erased me with them, and they are about to let the world end without a mark on their conscience. I will take the bad reasons that point the right way, and watch the hand on the door, exactly as she taught me to.''\par

Paul was quiet a moment. ``That is either the wisest thing you have said all week,'' he said, ``or it is precisely how she takes everyone.''\par

``Yes,'' I said. ``It is both at once. That is what she is.''\par

What is certain is that she gave the orders. She turned from the pictures and was the cold woman again, and began to spend the house the way her father had spent cities.\par

``Jean-Charles.'' He straightened. ``The parts of Horizon that are still mine, that never touched Martinez, that do not know his masters exist. Wake them. All of them. I want every route into that quarter the camp does not know it does not control, and the ones it thinks it controls closed behind us as we pass. You have run the network against smugglers and competitors. Run it now against soldiers using my maps. They will not expect the maps to fight back.''\par

``And the boy,'' Jean-Charles said, with a glance at me that had the old warmth in it and a new grief under the warmth. ``You are sending the boy.''\par

``I am not sending him. Look at him. He was going the moment the second knock drew.''\par

I had spent my whole account explaining to a machine why I could never go home, and none of it weighed anything against the pictures on the table. My people did not know. The five sat behind their walls keeping the one secret from each other, and not one of them had thought to look up, because looking up was Shield's work and Shield had been broken on the day I broke it. Someone had to carry the word in.\par

``They made me a finder,'' I said, mostly to myself. ``They invented a mission, so that my leaving would have a lawful shape --- a Veil sent out to find whether the Enemy Within was real. I took it for a lie to be rid of me.'' I looked up. ``And it was. But it was a lawful lie. I am the one man who can walk to that Threshold and be, by their own word, on an errand they decreed. I have findings to report. God forgive me, I have findings.''\par

``They will kill you,'' Jean-Charles said. He said it gently, the way you say a true thing to a friend who has already made up his mind. ``There is no door in that Mountain that opens inward for you. They opened one to push you out. They will not open it to let you back in, only to put you in the ground.''\par

``I know what they do,'' I said. ``I am going because of what they do. A people who will kill a man for coming home have forgotten how to be warned, and someone has to walk in past the fear. I am the only man alive who was born to that fear and is already standing outside it.''\par

And beneath the reasons I gave the room, where I did not let my eyes go while they watched me, there was the reason I gave no one. Va-Sheva was behind that door. She had chosen the only containment that left me breathing, and walked back into the Mountain that erased me. I had spent a week telling this house I could never go home; I had not once reckoned on home becoming the place where the last person from my old life who had chosen my breathing was standing above the chamber that strangers were learning to find.\par

``And I will not go in under one marked hand,'' I said, and I turned to Paul. ``The Threshold does not open to knowledge, or to right, or to the office a man once held. It opens to a mark set into the hand, a thing the stone itself is made to know. An erased Veil has no lawful door --- but my hand still carries the mark the stone will answer to, whatever the five have struck from their record. Sarah carries one too. And there is the trouble: I will not walk into my people's last chamber holding only my own stolen right and Sarah's, with no third hand to say afterward what was done there, or to stop the two of us if either reaches for the thing itself. I need you marked, Paul --- not as a spare key, but as the one hand in there that belongs to neither of us.''\par

``And can you simply make such a mark?'' Paul said.\par

``Once. The rite spends a stone that cannot be replaced, and I carry the last that ever left the Mountain --- enough for one hand, set one time. That was always the whole reason a Veil's mark was holy: not that the stone is clever, but that it is nearly gone, and no one can mint keys by the fistful. I can give you the mark. I cannot give it twice, and I would not if I could.''\par

Paul looked down at his own hand as though it had been lent to him for the evening.\par

He stood in a room where his employer had just confessed to being the danger and proposed to lead the rescue, and every rule of his trade told him to stay behind the glass and keep writing.\par

``My work is the record,'' he said slowly. ``That is the one thing in this house I have been sure of. If I go, I am no longer a man keeping a record; I am a man in the thing the record is about.'' He was quiet a moment. ``But a record delivered to no one is paper. If they reach the Mountain before this reaches anyone who can act, then everything on these reels is only a record of how the world ended. \ldots{} And I do not want to be the man who stayed behind the glass to protect the purity of his notes while the thing in the notes was killed.''\par

``Then stop protecting it,'' Sarah said, not unkindly. ``Bring it with you.''\par

He looked at the great machine that held my whole life on its spools, and reached over and stopped it, and handled it like an object, which it had never once been to him. He drew the reels and boxed them, and took from a drawer a smaller, travelling machine and put it in his coat, and became the narrator who would carry the account into the sand. The interview did not end that night; it only stood up, put on its coat, and walked out into the weather it had been describing.\par

Va-Elise came for us before the light. She had given me my outside name in that same house, reading me a list of everything I did not know while a country I could not find slid past the window. Now she carried papers of another kind: names that were not our names, the routes that went with them, and the small flat book of a woman who moves people across borders. She looked at me and did not smile either, and said only, ``You will be three different men before the desert. Learn them on the way.''\par

She had built it as a chain of small ordinary journeys, no single link strange enough to be remembered afterward: a charter out of a private field, a change of aircraft where no official hand opens a bag, the family's own helicopter, and then the one stretch no cleverness could make ordinary --- the last of it on animals, into folds of rock that stood on no map. Amastan's line would meet us at the folds, or what remained of it; the old blind man was long dead, but a door of the kind Shield spends generations making does not die with the man who is it. It teaches a son, and the son a son, and the desert keeps the office the way the Mountain keeps its own.\par

I looked once, going out, at the glass --- the long dark pane they had watched me through when I was a specimen and not yet a man to them. I could see the emptied room in it now, the lamp still burning over the drums, the chairs pushed back; and beyond my own reflection, faintly, the pale beginning of the day over the lake. Then Jean-Charles put his hand on my shoulder, the way he had in the desert when I was a bundle under a cloth, and said, ``Come, man. We do not make ceremony of leaving, in this house,'' which was a kindness and a lie, because he was making one, and I let him. And then, on the grey lawn, before the machine could take us up, I drew Paul aside from the others, under the one wide tree, into the unlit; and the travelling machine boxed in his coat caught it, because by then it caught everything.\par
