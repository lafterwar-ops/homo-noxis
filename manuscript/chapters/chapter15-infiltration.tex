\typesetchapter{Chapter 15}{Infiltration}

Amastan could not be bought, and that is the only interesting thing anyone ever wrote about him, and my father wrote it, in a margin, in a fury, because to Edward Blackwood a man who could not be bought was not a principle but a fault in the machinery of the world, a screw with the thread stripped, a thing that would not take the one tool he trusted. I read that margin as a daughter and as an heir, and where my father had seen a wall I saw a door, because a man who cannot be bought is a man who is guarding something worth more to him than money, and I had spent my whole life learning to find the thing a person guards.\par

It took Martinez two years to put a face and a tent to the name. Amastan was of the veiled people, the Kel Tamasheq, the men the maps call Tuareg and the men themselves call nothing you are owed. He moved through the deep Tanezrouft the way I move through a drum-paper, reading a country that looked empty to everyone else and was not empty to him. He guided, when he chose to; he refused, more often; he had turned back three of my father's expeditions without ever raising his voice, simply by declining to be the man who took them past a certain line, and letting them understand that no other man would either. Martinez brought me photographs of him taken from a distance, and reports bought from men who had sat at his fire, and none of it told me the one thing I needed, which was what he was guarding, and why a herdsman of the sand would guard it against the richest offers a dying empire could make.\par

I did not send Martinez to buy him. I had learned that much from my father's failure. I sent word, through three hands, that a woman who read the ground wished to speak with the man who read the sand, about a thing they had both heard in the same desert on the same night and told no one. And then I waited, which is the one art I have never lacked. It took a year. At the end of the year a boy I had never seen left a folded paper with a clerk in Adrar, and on the paper, in a careful French hand not his own, was a place, and a season, and the single line: \emph{Come without the Spaniard, and without the yellow rock in your mouth.} He meant Martinez, and he meant that I was to come as a reader and not as a buyer. I went.\par

\begin{room}

--- You met a man of the sand who would not sell, and you take that for the beginning of your innocence. It is the beginning of nothing. My people have men in the sand. I did not know it as a boy; I know it now, because you and this desert between you have taught me what I was never let to learn at home. Amastan was Shield's. A man Shield put out past the Threshold, generations deep, to be exactly what he was to you: a wall that money breaks against, and a door that opens the one time Shield decides it should. You did not find a crack in my people. You were handed one, by a Keeper, on purpose. Say that part plainly, before you make yourself the clever girl who picked a lock.

--- I will say it more plainly than you have. I did not pick the lock. I was let in. I have never once pretended to myself that I took the Mountain by my own wit; that is a comfort for smaller people, and I have never been able to afford comforts. I was let in because a man on your side had decided, for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with me, that being let in was worth the risk of me. Everything I built afterward, I built on a door somebody else chose to open. I would like you to hold on to that, because it will matter, at the end, whose hand was really on the door.

\end{room}

He met me at the edge of a country that has no edge. Four days by the last vehicle Martinez was allowed to bring, and then the vehicle sent back with the Spaniard fuming in it, and then two more days on animals, into folds of rock the maps drew as a single brown smear because no one who drew the maps had ever been permitted close enough to find the seams in it. I will not give you the route. I have given a great many things away in this telling and I will not give that, partly because I no longer perfectly remember it, and partly because I find that even now, having betrayed everything I am about to betray, there is one thing in me that will not draw that particular map for a machine to keep. Make of that what you will. I make nothing of it. It is simply the last locked room in a house I have otherwise thrown open.\par

The Mountain, from the outside, is a disappointment, and I understood at once that the disappointment was the whole of the defence. There is no gate. There is no wall a satellite could photograph and a general could want. There is a great tired shoulder of stone in a place there is no reason to go, and the ways into it are folded into the rock the way a letter is folded into an envelope you have decided not to open, and unless a man of the sand walks you to the exact seam and puts his hand flat on it and waits, you would die of thirst within sight of five thousand souls and never know they were there. I had spent years imagining an installation. Machinery, spoil-heaps, the honest ugliness of a thing that does work. There was none of it. Whatever did the work was under, and old, and silent, and the silence was so total that standing at the outer folds I felt the first real fear I had allowed myself in twenty years: not that I was wrong, but that I was right, and that the thing I was right about had been keeping its own counsel since before my language existed.\par

Amastan put his hand flat against a face of rock that was in no way I could see different from a hundred faces we had passed, and he waited, and did not knock, and did not call, and after a time long enough to teach me my own smallness a seam opened in the stone I had been staring straight at and had not seen. Cool air came out of it that smelled of lamp-oil and wet stone and, faint beneath both, of bread. Bread. Five thousand people were breathing and baking on the other side of a rock I might have leaned against to rest. I have crossed a great many thresholds in my life and bought my way through most of them, and I have never felt anything like the one I felt at that seam, which was the plain animal knowledge that I was being permitted, and that the permission could be withdrawn, and that the withdrawing would cost the other side nothing, the way a man closing a door against the weather does not think he has done anything to the weather.\par

They brought me to a chamber in the outer works that I think Shield keeps for exactly this, for the rare admitted Outsider, because it was made to show a visitor precisely as much as Shield wishes shown and not one lamp more. And in that chamber, seated, was an old man, and the old man was blind.\par

His eyes had not been taken by age or by sickness. I have seen a great many kinds of ruin in eyes and I knew this kind: it was burn. The whites of them were scarred, the lids drawn wrong, the whole face set in the particular patience of a man who has taught himself, over long years, to sit very still in a dark that will not lift. They called him, in my hearing, only \emph{the one who was Shield}, for he had given the office up by then. But he told me his name himself, because, he said, a man who is about to ask you to keep his people's secret owes you at least his own. He had been Ka-Xhian, Keeper of Shield.\par

And he had lost his sight, he told me, plainly, the way your boy tells a hard thing plainly, watching a fire in the desert. A fire that was not a fire. A light in the shape of a great cloud that stood taller than his own Mountain, four weeks' walk to the north, that turned the sand to glass and poisoned the wind for a generation. He had gone out himself, as Keeper of Shield, to stand at the safe edge of the world and look at what the Outsiders had learned to make, because Shield's whole office is to look at the thing the rest of the Mountain is forbidden to look at, and he had looked at it too long, or from too near, or simply with eyes that had spent their lives in lamplight and could not bear one glimpse of a man-made sun.\par

I want you to understand what happened in me when he said it, because it is the nearest thing to a religious experience I have had, and I am not a woman built for those. I had been at that fire. Not the same day; I have never known whether it was the same shot or a later one, and it does not matter. I had sat forty kilometres from a French sun with a drum-paper on my knees, watching the loud thing and listening for the quiet one. And on the far edge of the same desert, at the same kind of fire, an old blind man had stood and given his eyes to see the very thing I had turned my back on to listen. We had been at the same event, he and I, from opposite sides of a wall eleven thousand years thick, and we had each come away from it having lost the same thing to it, which was the ability to believe the world was as small as we had been told. He gave his eyes. I gave every ordinary thing a life is furnished with. We had made, without knowing it, the same trade, at the same fire, and now we sat in a stone room at the two ends of it.\par

\begin{room}

--- Ka-Xhian.

--- Yes.

--- He was Keeper of Shield before the line came down to the man I served under. I was told he went blind in the service of the seal, and I let the words mean nothing, the way you let the old words mean nothing when you are young and full of your own office. \ldots{} He went out to look at your fire. At the thing you were sitting under. And you sat there, and you turned your back on it so you could hear us knocking. \ldots{} The two of you.

--- The two of us. I have never told that to a living soul. I am telling it to a machine and to the last Veil of Light because I have discovered, this week, at the very end, that I would rather it were known than kept, and you two are the only keepers left who would understand what was kept.

\end{room}

He did not show me much. I want that on the record against the size of what I later did, because it would be easy, hearing the end of this, to imagine that the old man threw open his archive and I walked out with the Mountain in my pocket. He did the opposite. He showed me exactly enough to make me useful and to keep me hungry, which is what a Keeper of Shield is trained his whole life to do, and which is, I have often thought since, precisely what I would have done in his place, and precisely what I did do, later, to a great many people, including the man I sent to check whether your boy was real.\par

What he let me understand was a shape, and the shape was this. That the Mountain was governed by five, and that the five were kept apart on purpose, walled from one another so that no single one of them ever held the whole of anything. That the five did not pass their offices the way kings pass crowns, by blood and in the daylight, but by something rarer and more deliberate, a succession so guarded and so seldom that it left a mark you could hear from a continent away if you knew to listen, which I did. That somewhere beneath us was a mechanism, old past their own reckoning, that could do to the whole world what the French fire had done to a stretch of sand, and that the entire cruel architecture of the place, the five walled offices, the withheld knowledge, the terror taught to children, existed to make certain that no one hand could ever reach that mechanism and turn it. He did not tell me how it worked. He did not tell me where it was. He told me only that it was, and that Shield's oldest duty, older than the doctrine that the outside was a curse, was to watch the outside precisely because the outside was the one thing that might one day grow strong enough to reach in and take the mechanism for itself. That was why I was in the room. Not for my eyes. For my reach. A Blackwood could see the outside coming, and turn a survey aside, and lose a prospector's report in a drawer, and buy the silence of a man who had wandered too near. He was recruiting me, in that chamber, to help Shield hold the outside off. He thought he was making a gamekeeper. He was quite wrong about that, but he was not a fool, and I have wondered every year since whether the blind man saw more clearly than I gave him credit for, and admitted me anyway, because a Shield with no eyes left had run out of better doors.\par

He asked three things of me, and I gave my word on all three, there in the dark, and I want them in the record, because I kept two of them for thirty years and I have broken all three this week, sitting in this chair. That I would never bring another Outsider to the folds. That I would never set the way in down in writing, nor in any machine that keeps a voice. And that if the day ever came when I could no longer serve Shield's purpose, I would send word by Amastan's line, and withdraw, and take the whole of it into my own silence, and leave the Mountain as I had found it, unreached. I meant every word as I said it. I have wondered since whether he believed me, or whether a Keeper of Shield gathers promises the way I gather drum-papers, knowing most of them are false, keeping them because the shape of which promises a person is willing to make tells you more than the truth of whether they will be kept. I said yes, and yes, and yes. He turned his ruined face toward the sound of my voice as though he could weigh it in his hands, and he told me I did not have to mean it; that Shield had never once in its history relied on an Outsider meaning anything; that the Exception protected itself. I did not understand him then. I understand him now, and it is the coldest lesson the old man taught me. He meant that the drawer would outlive us both. He meant that a photograph in a Shield archive is a leash you cannot feel until the day somebody finds the far end of it. He was leashing me while he recruited me. The gamekeeper he made was, himself, being kept.\par

\begin{room}

--- And then he let you be photographed.

--- Amastan photographed me. At the outer folds, against a particular face of rock, with the light hard off the pale stone the way it is only for an hour after dawn. I am standing, and I am in my own clothes, my field clothes, pale, cut for the heat, and my hair is tied back, and Amastan is beside me in the clothes of the sand with his face lined and his stance loose, and beneath the border, later, in two hands, someone wrote our two names. It was taken on Ka-Xhian's authority. He could not see it made. He ordered it made because he wanted a thing kept, in Shield's own archive, that proved the Exception had happened, that a Keeper of Shield had, with his mind clear and his office intact, let an Outsider stand at the folds and had made a bargain with her. Shield does not trust the memories of the living, he told me. Shield trusts the drawer.

--- \ldots{} I have held that photograph.

--- I know you have. Sit. I will tell it and you will not have to.

--- No. You will hear it from the one who held it, because you have had everything your own way in this house and you will not have this. \ldots{} There is a place under the Mountain we are not to speak of, where Shield keeps the things too dangerous for Faith to carry. I went where I was not permitted, at the end, when I had already stopped being able to believe. I opened a drawer. And there was a woman under hard light in clothes I had no word for, and a man of the sand beside her, and I did not know the woman's face, only her name written under it, which meant nothing to me then and has meant everything to me since. Sarah Blackwood. I knelt in the dark of my own people's most guarded room and I held the proof that Shield had shaken the hand of the outside while it taught us the outside was death. That drawer is one of the reasons I am here. It is one of the reasons there is a here at all. \ldots{} And you made it. You are the woman in the drawer.

--- I am the woman in the drawer. I have been the woman in the drawer for longer than you have been alive, waiting, I suppose, for the drawer to be opened by exactly the sort of person who could not survive opening it. I am sorry. I am not often sorry, and I will not make a habit of it in the time we have left, but I am sorry it was you who opened it, because I know now what it cost, and I did not, then, care what it might.

\end{room}

I left the Mountain with a shape and no substance, a certainty and no proof beyond one photograph I was allowed to keep a copy of and a memory I was forbidden to write down, and I left, above all, wanting more, which the blind man intended, and underestimated. He gave me exactly enough to bind me to Shield's purpose and exactly too much to be content with it. I rode back out of those folds already building the thing that would become my life, and I built it, at first, in perfect good faith, as the very gamekeeper he had asked for. That is the part that no one believes and that I insist upon. Horizon began as a promise kept.\par

It began as liaison: a single quiet channel between Blackwood and Amastan, a way for Shield to hear what the outside was doing and for me to be told when I strayed too near. Then it became monitoring, because I could not help myself; I had forty years of drum-papers and a reason, now, to gather every stroke that empty quarter ever made, and I told myself, and it was even true, that Shield was safer the more completely I could hear. Then it became the management of secrecy, because to protect a thing you must know who else is looking at it, and so I began to keep a census of the world's curiosity about that patch of desert, every geologist, every satellite pass, every rumour in every ministry, and to reach out, gently, and turn each of them a few degrees aside. Then, because a channel that can misdirect a survey can also move a man, it became routes: ways in and ways out, cached and tested, in case Shield ever needed to put someone across the Threshold in a hurry, or take someone off it. Then mapping, because you cannot secure what you have not charted, and I told myself the map was for defence. Then the identification of the five, because you cannot protect Keepers you cannot name, and I began, office by office, death by death, knock by knock, to learn who they were and when they changed. Then contingency, because a prudent guardian plans for the worst. And then, one day, so gradually that I could not tell you the hour it happened, I was no longer building a wall around the Mountain. I was building a hand that could reach into it. And the wall and the hand, it turns out, are made of exactly the same stones, laid in exactly the same order, and only the last stone tells you which one you have built.\par

I will give you one stone, so it is not all bricks and sermon. There was a survey, a real one, a consortium of three companies and two governments, that came within a season of running a deep line straight across the folds under cover of hunting for water. I did not bribe them. Bribery leaves a shape, and a man who is paid to look away can be paid again to look back. I ruined them. I arranged, across two years and through eleven hands, for their funding to develop a doubt, for their lead geologist to receive a flattering offer he could not refuse in Western Australia, for their permits to tangle in a border dispute I quietly helped to warm, and for their backers to read, in a journal of unimpeachable reputation, a careful paper establishing that the whole region was geologically barren and seismically dead. Every piece of it was true, or true enough to survive a check, and none of it could be traced within a thousand miles of a woman in Switzerland, and the survey folded, and its men dispersed to other deserts, and not one of them ever knew they had been turned aside from five thousand living souls by a hand they never saw. That is what I mean by protection. I was, for a long stretch of years, extraordinarily good at it, and the Mountain was safer for my existence, and I have no doubt at all that Ka-Xhian, had he lived to watch me work, would have named me faithful. And that is the horror I have been walking you toward, so hear it in one line and then we can stop: turning a survey aside to save the Mountain, and mapping the Mountain to take it, are the same labour, done by the same hands, and the morning I passed from the one to the other was marked by nothing at all. I did not fall. I have never fallen. I walked, on a level floor, from the guardian to the thief, and there was no step under my foot to warn me.\par

\begin{room}

--- Say the reason. You have walked us all the way up to it and now you are describing bricks. Say why the gamekeeper built a hand.

--- I will say the reason I gave myself, and then, because you have earned it, the truer one underneath. The reason I gave myself was a good one, and I still cannot fault the logic of it, which is what makes it so dangerous. Your Mountain's whole terror rests on one word: unanimity. The end of the world needs all five hands, or so the doctrine runs, so that no single madman, no captured office, no one tyrant can ever bring it. But a thing that needs five hands can be stopped by removing one. If I could reach into the Mountain and take a single Keeper, quietly, at need, then the machine could never be fired, because I would have broken the five, and the world would be safe, safer than your terrified priests had ever managed to make it, safe by my hand, at last, from the thing your people had spent eleven thousand years merely praying about. That is the veto. That is what I told myself I was building. A guarantee. The one insurance policy the human race had never been allowed to hold.

--- And the truer one.

--- The truer one is that I wanted it. \ldots{} I had found the largest secret on the earth, and I could not bear that it was not mine. Not safe. Mine. My father died reaching for that emptiness and I had touched the far side of it, and every stone I laid toward the Mountain I laid because the alternative was to stand still and let the thing exist without my hand on it, and I have never in my life been able to let a thing exist without my hand on it. Call it my father's nose that would not close. Call it the appetite I found in myself at twenty-seven over a bomb. Call it what your scripture would call it, which is the oldest name there is for it and the only honest one. I wanted the whole. I built a machine forty years long to reach into the one place in the world built to make certain that no one ever could. And I told myself, the entire time, in a voice I half believed, that I was only building a wall.

\end{room}

\begin{room}

--- Then you are the thing. \ldots{} Do you understand what you have just told me. There is one crime in my whole world, one, beneath all the others, the crime the Beast itself was set to punish and the reason the five are kept apart and the reason a Keeper opens his own body rather than hold two offices at once. To reach for the whole. To be the one hand. My people drowned a city of the wise for it before there was a Mountain. And you sat in a stone room with a blind man who gave his eyes to keep that hand from ever closing, and you shook it, and you went home, and you spent your life becoming precisely the hand he was blinded to prevent. You are not the enemy at the gate. You are the thing the gate was built for.

--- Yes.

--- You do not even argue.

--- Why would I argue. I have had forty years to find the flaw in what you have just said and I have not found it. I am the one hand. I have always been the one hand. The only thing I have left to add, and it is the reason you are alive and in this house and not dead in a desert, is that being the one hand does not feel, from the inside, like wickedness. It feels like responsibility. It feels like being the only adult in a house full of frightened children who are all standing around a fire they will not admit is lit. That is the part your scripture leaves out, boy, and it is the part that should frighten you most, because it means the thing your Mountain fears above all others does not arrive looking like a devil. It arrives looking like the one person in the room who is willing to be sensible.

\end{room}

I looked at the fact-checker while she said the last of it, because I had begun, over four days, to watch him the way I watch a needle: he is the honest instrument in that house, the one that only draws what is there. And what was there, on his face, was a man doing arithmetic he did not want the answer to. I knew the sum he was working, because I had worked it myself, years before, and had chosen not to finish it. He had been hired out of a city a long way from all of this to check a single fact about a single man. He had been given a thin folder, a machine, a room, a car, a salary, an instruction. And he was sitting there, at last, understanding that every one of those things had a hand under it, and that the hand was the woman across the table, and that his whole clean small honest job was one brick, laid in order, in a wall that was also a hand, and that he had never once been outside it. He had come into this to hold a fact to the light. He was learning that he was inside the fact.\par

\begin{room}

--- I have a question, and it is not the boy's question, it is mine, and I would like it answered as plainly as you have answered him.

--- Ask it, Paul.

--- The man who engaged me. The letter, the terms, the account that pays me. The physician who tends him. The archivist who handed me the folder with the two traces already in it, dated in a hand that is not mine. \ldots{} How much of the thing that brought me to this house was chance, and how much was you.

--- \ldots{}

--- Answer it.

--- None of it was chance, Paul. I do not permit chance in my house. I have told you what I am; do you imagine I would leave the one honest man in the building to arrive by accident? You were chosen, and vetted, and brought, and given exactly the folder I wished you to have, and set in front of exactly the man I wished checked, and the two traces were in that folder because I put them there, to see what you would do when you found them. You did the thing I hoped you would do, which is why you are still in the room, and the thing I feared you would do, which is why I am no longer certain the room is mine. You have been a brick and an instrument at once, all week, exactly as I was in a stone chamber a lifetime ago. I am not mocking you. I am telling you that you have just met the wall from the inside, and that there is only one useful thing to do on the morning you learn you are inside the wall, and it is the thing I did. You stop asking whose hand built it. You start asking whose hand is on it now.

\end{room}

And it was there, on that sentence, with the boy white and silent and the fact-checker's pencil stopped against the paper, that the house stopped being the safest room any of us had ever sat in.\par

\begin{room}

--- \ldots{} Forgive me. Madame. There is a thing off the drums you will want to see.

--- Not now.

--- Now. It came in eleven minutes ago. The southern array. It is the shape. It is your shape.

--- \ldots{} Bring it here. \ldots{} Set it down. There. Under the lamp.

--- \ldots{} That is a Keeper rising. That is the knock. I have watched two of them draw themselves and I would know that hand in my sleep. Someone in the Mountain has died, and someone has risen in his place, and it happened while we sat here talking about me.

--- No one has risen.

--- You cannot know that. You have been gone from the Mountain half a year. A Keeper could sicken and fall and be replaced and you the last to hear of it.

--- I could. But the knock does not lie about what it is, and you have just told me it lies about nothing. And I am telling you that the whole of my life inside those offices, no Keeper of the five was old, or ailing, or near the chain. There was no death waiting. There was no succession due. \ldots{} A knock without a death is not a succession. It is something wearing a succession's face.

--- \ldots{} Madame.

--- What.

--- It is drawing again.

--- That is not possible. A rising is one hand, one time. Nothing rises twice in a single hour.

--- Look at the drum. It is drawing again. The same five. Now, while we are standing here.

\end{room}
