\typesetchapter{Chapter 9}{Threshold}

\begin{room}


--- The thirty-first of March; nine o'clock; the eighth of our interviews. Before we return to your account, I was wondering if you had time to study the texts of \textit{Genesis} I translated for you.

--- Yes. I spent the time after the lesson reading instead. You promised me the first chapter of Genesis and gave me four; thank you for the translation, Paul, though I do not yet know whether it was a kindness.

--- I began with the chapter I promised, saw enough resemblance to understand why you had asked for it, and could not sensibly stop before the first murder. I have translated the language; the history I have not certified, and I would ask you to keep that distinction near you while you compare them. Two stories may preserve the same shape because one descends from the other, because both descend from something older, or because human beings repeatedly give the same shapes to different events. Resemblance alone cannot tell you which account has travelled, which has altered, or whether either was ever true.

--- Your own story contains the murderer from whom ours says your people descend. You call him \textit{Cain} and make him the son of Adam, whereas our murderer is the younger son of Shield, and Shield is Adam's son, so your line has lost a generation. You have forgotten that the murdered brother was broad in the shoulder, mighty before the beasts and loved for his strength, yet you have kept the envy, the secret killing, the blood crying from the ground, the exile and the home made inaccessible behind him. I wanted that resemblance, because it seemed to prove that your people had carried away a broken memory of our beginning and kept the murderer while forgetting the Mountain. Then I read the part before \textit{Cain}, and found something I wanted less. God places the tree inside the garden, gives the fruit one meaning and forbids the woman to test it. The serpent places another meaning beside His: perhaps the boundary does not protect her from death; perhaps it protects God from a creature whose eyes have opened. That is entirely a principle of Light. The serpent puts no knowledge into the fruit, and forces nothing; it reveals that the stated purpose of a prohibition may not be the whole of it, and asks her to look at the arrangement behind the command. She looks. She crosses. Her eyes open. Your God Himself then says that the man has become like one of Us, knowing good and evil, so the serpent is right about the hidden consequence, and yet your tellers make the serpent evil and call the woman's movement toward knowledge a fall. This is how your story twists the offices: the creature who places a second account beside the authorised one belongs to Light, and the woman's passage into self-consciousness is an ascension, but because both acts cross a boundary set by authority, you name illumination corruption. So tell me, Paul, why is your serpent evil?

--- Hear what you have asked. To say whether the serpent's gift was good or evil, I must weigh good against evil --- which is the fruit itself; that story cannot be judged from outside the fruit. You have set the apple before me and asked how it tastes, as I have done to students all my life and called it a lesson. Your reading is clean, and too clean. He tells them they will not die, and they die --- slowly, through what they have done; he reveals what God withheld and keeps back the cost of it, as your own Light opens one hidden thing and lays down another in the opening. Eve's eyes come open on her nakedness, her fear, her separation. Her crossing is an ascension, yes; and an ascent may go where there is no coming back. That is why we call it a fall: a boundary crossed the one way it can be, and the door shut behind her, and the garden left standing, and closed.

--- I know. That is the part I did not want. A forbidden act; widened knowledge; the self divided into the one who lives and the one who sees himself living; exile; the entrance guarded; the former home still existing and inaccessible. I began by reading \textit{Eve} as the first of your kind to practise Light, and ended by discovering that her punishment is my life. Then the sequence happens again with \textit{Cain}. He crosses into murder, the hidden act becomes visible through blood, the knowledge of what he has done changes what he is, and he is driven away under a mark that carries the act wherever he goes. Your \textit{Genesis} separates the two expulsions: \textit{Eve} leaves through knowledge, and \textit{Cain} leaves through murder. Ours joins them. Our \textit{Cain} murders his brother and crosses out of the Oasis carrying the dead man's wife, a daughter of Faith, through whom holy speech passes into the open land. I cannot yet tell whether your story has split ours, ours has sewn together yours, or whether neither text contains what I have just said and I have made a third account in the seam between them. That uncertainty would be ordinary here. Your \textit{Cain} is the resemblance I wanted. Your \textit{Eve} is the one I did not. I have not finished being expelled from \textit{Eden}, and the next part of my own account begins with the reward I received for crossing a boundary without knowing what waited on its other side.

\end{room}

For several days after the martyrdom, I waited for the Mountain to feel different. It stayed the same. Water still came by measure. Children still recited badly in the teaching halls and were corrected by voices patient enough to become cruel. Shield still closed passages without explaining itself. Balance still marked loss and use with the same green dye. Faith still spoke over jars and wounds and dead bees and frightened mothers and old men who had begun to forget the names of their own children. Justice still sat where people wished no one were sitting.\par

The Mountain had changed my name, recorded my sequence, bound the white cloth to my wrist, and set me beneath Ka-Raedin in the chain of Light. Then it handed me the northern halls.\par

Syphiron had told me, in the feast among the immoral cups, that the northern halls would begin to touch me. They did more than touch me; they closed over my head. The candidate sequences came to my hand, and the apparatus rosters, and the cross-Kahir requests that Light must answer and file and answer again; the lamp accounts; the quarrels over who might stand where during which rite; the young men to be watched, and graded, and told in the exact measure Va-Raedin had once used on me that their best was acceptable. My days filled to their edges and past them. I rose before the First hour and lay down after the last lamp, and between the two there was no hour that was mine.\par

And the people went. Sela went first, and most quietly. She had carried the cloths and the little oil and watched me the way the young watch a thing they are working out, with the corner of a smile she could never quite put away. Then one morning she was no longer set to my chamber; and the next, when we passed in a corridor, she lowered her eyes and held to the far wall; and the smile was put away for good. No one had done it to hurt her. Rank had moved her to the far side of a wall that neither of us had built, and would not let her back across it.\par

The candidates I had trained beside stopped speaking when I came into a hall, and gave me the flat obedience a man gives an office and not a friend. Va-Sheva was Shield, and Shield had its own year, and the doors I might once have found her behind were shut for reasons that had nothing to do with me. The Mountain is very large. I had never known, until a piece of it was set in my hand, how large it was; nor how a man may stand at the centre of five thousand people, spoken to by all of them and touched by none.\par

One thing had always been mine, and that was the Mirror. I had stood before it in the dark since I was a candidate of Light --- days of it, then weeks, then the years run together --- until I knew every beam of it the way a man knows the inside of his own mouth, and had trained my eye to catch the smallest glimmer, the smallest movement, the one thread of light that came in where no thread should. It was the whole of my craft, and the best of what I was. In those first months as Veil I went to it, and stood before it, and found that I did not care what it did. The glimmers came, and I let them come. The eye that had been made to catch them looked, and reported, and felt nothing.\par

Ka-Raedin went on teaching me, because that was his office. One morning he set me at the wave wall to learn what light does when it passes an edge.\par

``It bends,'' I said, giving it back to him. ``It bends around the edge, and it spreads. Set a second edge beside the first, and the two spreadings meet, and where they meet they add in some places and cancel in others, and leave a banding of light and dark that no straight ray could make.''\par

``Interference,'' he said.\par

``Interference. There. That is the whole of it.''\par

And I meant, and let him hear that I meant, the rest of it: that I did not care. I did not care what light did around one edge. I did not care what it did around two. I had spent my life being told that the thing I knelt to was one thing, plain and single and holy, and edge by edge and lesson by lesson it had been taken from me and handed back a sum of parts I had never been asked whether I wished to know. I gave him the banding correctly and felt nothing, and he watched me feel nothing.\par

``You will learn the next thing after this,'' he said.\par

``No.''\par

He waited.\par

``I will not learn the next thing, Keeper. I have done the arithmetic of your chain. Every next thing you set before me is a single step toward one morning, and I have already seen that morning, because you showed it to me under a falling stone in front of five thousand people. To finish the chain is to be the hand that opens the Keeper above me below the ribs, while he bares himself to it --- as your hand did it, and Syphiron bared himself, and I knelt in my tier and watched. I will not walk toward the morning that hand is mine and the body under it is yours. Teach another Veil. Let him take my place, and rise, and hold the knife. I will stay where I am, and you will go on living.''\par

Under the refusal lay a colder thing, which I could not have set into words for him, because I had only begun to feel its shape. Everything I had been given as true had been taken back and returned to me changed. Light, that I had knelt to as the one single holy substance, had turned in my hands into a sum, and a wave, a thing that bent and banded and lied around an edge. The five offices I had taken for the frame of the world had a foreigner's cartoon for a twin. The gravure I had wept in front of as a child had a seam running through it that I had learned to see and could not unsee. I was of Light, and I had been wrong about the light. With that gone, there was no floor left under me firm enough to be certain of anything at all.\par

So I stopped. The offices do not stop for a man's mood --- the lamps must be trimmed whether or not the hand that trims them wishes to be alive --- and so I trimmed them, and graded the candidates, and filed the cross-Kahir requests, and did all of it correctly and emptily, by rote. I ate alone, or forgot to eat. I let the questions that had once burned behind my teeth go out, one by one, and did not miss their light. I did not go to the Mirror. At night the title lay on me like a stone and I did not sleep; by day I moved through the northern halls the way a lamp is carried through a room, giving out what I was told to give out, going where I was carried, and wanting nothing. Around me the Mountain went on exactly as it had the morning it named me.\par

Ka-Raedin watched all of it, and for a long time said nothing. I know now what he was doing, because he told me so himself, when it was over. He was waiting. He had carried the thing himself, the year Ka-Sarem was released and he could not, for all his rank, climb out from under it, and he knew there is no use reaching for a man until the man knows he is drowning. So he let me go all the way down. Six months he let me. He lightened nothing, corrected nothing, offered nothing, and let the office and the doubt and the ungrieved dead press me flat, until one morning I stood before the small black basin with the cloth in my hand and could not remember why the circle had to be clean, nor whom I was cleaning it for, nor whether I would mind if I left it.\par

That was the morning he came for me.\par

``Va-Kailan of Light. Put the cloth down. It has waited eleven thousand years; it will keep a year more.''\par

He took the stool with the uneven leg and sat, and looked at me a while, and then he told me the oldest thing in the office, the thing we pass from one mouth to one ear and never write down.\par

``What is on you now has a name, boy, though you will find it in no record. We call it the ascension amnesia. It is the grand and ancient sickness of Light, and you are not the first it has taken, nor the tenth, nor the hundredth. It came for me. It came for the one who trained me, and for the one who trained him, back and back as far as the office runs. Syphiron had it the year he could not stop seeing the face of the man he had risen over, and the Keeper before him gave him the cure for it; and Syphiron gave the cure to me; and I am giving it to you now. It has hit this Mountain over and over since the first Light knelt, and it will not stop hitting it, because the office is built out of the very thing that breaks a man: you are made to see, and then shown that everything you saw was the half of it, and then handed the knife.''\par

``I have counted your years. Three a servant. Four and a half a candidate. Six months a Veil. That is eight years of Light --- eight years of the dark and the Mirror --- and in all of them no one, not I and not Syphiron and not the Mountain, ever once told you that you were allowed to be tired. If you do not wish to pierce the mysteries of the light as a wave, then do not. Give yourself the time. Remember why you liked the light in the first place, before it was a chain, and a knife, and a stone.''\par

``I am taking the northern halls off you. All of them, for a year. You will sleep by day, and I will hear no word of complaint about it from any quarter; and if a candidate must be graded, he can wait, or be graded by a man who is not drowning.''\par

``And what am I to do with the year, Keeper?''\par

``Nothing.'' He almost smiled. ``I want you to do nothing with it. I want you to waste it, end to end, and come to me at the finish of it and tell me why you loved the light.''\par

He stood.\par

``One thing more. All those years at the Mirror in the dark, catching the one glimmer in the black --- no one has told you why we make a boy do that, nor what it is training the eye to do. It is not obvious. It was not obvious to me either. Come up with me this evening, and I will show you, and then I will leave you to it.''\par

He took me up.\par

I had not known there was an up. The way to it left the last of the Light passages by a shaft I had cleaned a hundred times and never once thought to climb, and it went up through the living rock by handholds worn smooth and a stair that gave out into a chimney a broad man could not have passed. It was a hard climb, and a cold one, and long. Shield does not come here, he said over his shoulder as we went; the view buys them nothing worth the danger of being seen against the sky, and so they leave the crown of the Mountain to us. It is the one place in the world that belongs to Light alone.\par

The Mountain is a cone. Near the very top a great hollow has been cut, or was always there --- as though a vast ball had been pressed down into the point and lifted away again, and taken the point with it, and left a cone with its tip sheared clean off and a round bowl scooped into the flat of it. And in the bowl, at the crown of the Mountain, where Shield never comes, there is an observatory.\par

``No,'' he said, before the question could reach my mouth. ``It is not the Keeper's Chamber. Do not go looking for it up here.''\par

It was a low round room open to the sky, its floor worn smooth by generations of backs that had lain on it. And set on a mounting of pivots and brass rings, its long throat pointed up into the dark, stood a thing of metal and glass I had no name for. We have metal, he said, and we have lenses; a people that has both, and a reason to look up, will build one of these sooner or later. Put your eye to it.\par

``Here,'' he said. ``There is no lesson tonight, nor on any night you do not want one. The sky is yours to explore. If a question comes to you, bring it to me and I will answer it gladly. And if you only wish to look, then look --- and find again why you liked the light, before anyone told you what it costs.''\par

Then he took a bottle from inside his robe --- hydromel, the same sweet fire Syphiron had poured into my cup the night before the ceremony, the night before I watched him open himself under the stone --- and two of the small white cups, and he filled them both, and held one out to me.\par

``We have not done this,'' he said. ``Neither of us, not once. There has been an office to hold, and a young man to carry, and a Mountain to keep from its fear, and in the middle of all of it no one has said his name aloud and grieved him. So.'' He raised the cup. ``To Syphiron of Light.''\par

``To Syphiron of Light,'' I said, and my voice broke on the second word of his name.\par

We drank. And then the two of us, the Keeper and his Veil, alone in the bowl at the top of the Mountain where Shield did not come and no living soul could tell us not to, set down the cups and wept for the man we had both loved, and both, each in his own way, helped to kill. Neither of us looked away from the other. Neither of us made the other stop. For the first time since the dome came down over the swing, I was not alone inside it.\par

When it had passed, and the cold had dried our faces, he told me what the place was for.\par

``Every Light that ever rose has come up here to do the thing you have just done. To burn the loneliness down to something a man can lift. And to remember, when everything the office has taught you has turned to weight in your hands, the one thing under all of it that stayed only beautiful. The light. What we have always loved.''\par

Then he left me there, as he had promised, with the bottle and the two cups and the whole slow turning of the sky.\par

And so the year began.\par

I slept through the daylight and climbed at dusk, and lay on my back in the cold stone bowl at the crown of the Mountain with the long glass beside me, and gave myself to the sky. I had grown up under two shafts of borrowed sun and a Hall of lamps. I had never, in any true sense, seen the sky at all. Now I had the whole of it, every clear night, and for five months I did the one thing Ka-Raedin had ordered, and gave myself to it entirely.\par

It was past anything the Mountain had ever shown me. There were more stars than there were people in the Hall, more than there were people in the whole of my world, and they did not hold still: the entire field of them turned through the night in one slow patient wheel, all but a single one low in the north, around which the rest of heaven went the way water goes around a stone. I lay and let the field be far too large for me, and did not try to make it smaller.\par

Then, because I had been made in the dark before the Mirror and could no more stop measuring than stop breathing, I began to measure.\par

The wheel was not quite true. The one low star in the north, that I had taken for the fixed pin of it, kept a small circle of its own; even it crept, and the true centre of the turning was a point in the empty dark beside it, where no star stood at all. That small wrongness held me more than all the rest. The pattern the stars made among themselves never broke --- night upon night, season upon season, the same shapes returned undeformed, the same three points held the same angle to a hair --- and so I let the old travellers keep their beasts and heroes, and named the sky the only way I trusted: by the angles it kept. Stable triangles. Distances that did not change. A handful of sure points I could swing the rest of the heavens against. I was building, without the word for it, the thing my office had trained my eye to do --- a reckoning of the sky out of lines of sight and the angles between them, the geometry of light carried upward and laid against the dark.\par

And against that fixed lattice, a few things moved.\par

Five bright points kept no faith with the pattern. Night upon night they wandered along a single slanting road across the others, now quick, now slow, one of them so bright before dawn that I mistook it once for a lamp. I set them apart at once; my whole training was the catching of the one thing in a field that behaves unlike its neighbours. The Moon kept no faith either, and kept it differently: it changed its shape and its brightness, moved against the stars faster than anything in the sky, showed itself sometimes by day and sometimes not at all, and so I set it outside the star-field, a nearer thing on business of its own. Under the long glass a single point would open into two that circled one another; a smudge would resolve into a knot of a thousand; the brightest wanderer showed a small round disc, and about the disc four lesser lights that changed their places from one night to the next, as though they were tethered to it and swinging. My trained eye caught what a lazy eye would let pass --- that this star burned faintly red and that one blue-white, that this one held steady and that one trembled, that the dark between them, which looked empty, was only too far off to read.\par

And across the whole of it, from one edge of the dark to the other, ran a pale river of light, so crowded that no eye could pull its grains apart, splitting along its course and closing again, with dark reaches braided through the bright. I turned the long glass on it, and found no river there at all --- only stars, and stars behind those stars, past all counting, a crowd my eye had taken for a mist.\par

I measured what I could reach, and my people had given me good tools to reach with: the Year-Clock's count, the pendulum's honest beat, the sighting-frames of the practice halls. I found that the stars came back to their places a little sooner each night than the sun did, by a fixed small margin that never varied. I measured the width the Moon showed, and the width the sun showed through smoked glass, and found them, to my surprise, nearly the same. I timed the Moon from full to full, and the sun from its northmost rising back to itself, and wrote the numbers small, and trusted them.\par

Some things I could not measure, and reached anyway. The Moon was a round ball, always, and its bright part always turned toward where the sun had gone down or would rise; so I took it that the Moon made no light of its own, and only caught the sun's, and its shapes were the turning of a lit ball before my eye. The wanderers stood nearer than the stars, and the stars stood unreachably far; for however I shifted along the bowl, or waited half a year and shifted with the whole Mountain, the stars never budged against each other by the width of a thought, and a thing that will not move however far you walk to the side is a thing beyond all walking. Why the five wanderers slowed and hurried and doubled back along their road I could not solve, and I set it down unsolved, and did not lie to myself that I had the answer.\par

But the thing that took me hardest was none of these. It was the dark.\par

I had been raised to know exactly what darkness was. It was the absence of light --- the thing the lamps pushed back, the thing that filled a corridor when a flame failed, the enemy my whole people spent their coal and their mirrors and their two poor shafts of stolen sun to hold at arm's length. Down in the Mountain, light was the world and dark was the lack of it. Up in the bowl, night on night, I watched that turn itself over. Out here the dark was the whole of it, the ground of everything, running past all measure in every direction; and the light was a scatter of small bright holes pricked in it, few, and far between, and mostly failing halfway across. My people had spent everything they had to make a little light, and had called the darkness its absence. The sky told me the darkness was the thing that was there, and the light the rare exception --- a handful of sparks in a hall with no walls and no end. I lay under it and felt the floor of everything I had been taught tilt very slowly beneath me, and I did not look away.\par

And in the looking, the eye that had gone dead in the northern halls came back to life --- came back for the beauty of the thing, which was the only teacher I had left that asked nothing of me and cost no man his life. I remembered why I had loved the light. And the trained eye, once it was living again, would not hold still at the beautiful. It began to catch Light at the edges of Light. The watching had changed the Mountain.\par

Small leaks. Accidental glints. Exterior light entering in wrong seasons; internal lamp-lines escaping through vents and returning from stone beyond the official paths. At first I dismissed them as maintenance defects --- a tarnished plate, a wet patch catching more than it should. Yet certain wrongnesses repeated. A faint white flash beyond a high ventilation slot in the south-east service descent, visible only as the night lamps were trimmed. A reflected thread along the upper edge of a sealed Shield route, present three nights and absent the fourth. A pale return from far outside the Mountain when internal lamps burned late and the air beyond the Oasis was dry.\par

I let the first glint pass. I recorded nothing, spoke to no one. The old Sa-Kailan would have gone after the first brightness with a stolen lamp and a heroic justification; I watched for twelve nights instead. The flash failed when high dust crossed the moon. It fed on a deeper glow from a maintenance shaft worked after evening. It showed from one position only in the old vent gallery, and only if I lowered my head to the line where the outside air entered before it became Mountain air.\par

Then memory supplied the missing shape. As Sa-Kailan, Candidate of Light, with dust in my sandals and Va-Sheva beside me saying softly: \textit{Do not point.} Va-Sheva closing possibilities before danger had a name. A flash may be nothing. A turned marker may be nothing. An old woman out of place may be nothing. Three nothings close together become a shape. Shield had built the world so that small wrongnesses could be read before they became deaths. It placed markers, watchers, angles, hidden bodies, and silent signals. Light sees what can be shown. Shield sees what must be stopped before it shows itself. What had once been said to me as a rebuke now sounded like an invitation, and I distrusted it. For several more nights I did nothing but make myself useful. I supervised younger Candidates in the practice halls. I spared them the teaching Va-Raedin had given me. I was still too young to make cruelty look like skill. When one boy rushed a false door and struck his shoulder, I waited until the exercise ended and said, privately,\par

``The room did not defeat you. Your first answer did.''\par

The boy looked at me with a resentment so familiar that I almost smiled. Former peers avoided my gaze. Older Light attendants tested my authority with small omissions --- a report handed late, a lamp plate left unpolished, a route question phrased too casually. I did not answer with anger, and I did not soften it either. But in the empty hours of the night, I returned to the glint. \par

I chose the route only after I could close my eyes and walk it in memory. From the eastern lower spine to the old vent gallery. Down the maintenance stair past the disused oil shelves. Through the narrow inspection slit that overlooked the sealed north passage. Pause for the third Shield change. Across the dark water service, where my robe would take damp if I brushed the left wall. Then the old air chamber above the cedar descent, where outside breath moved through stone before becoming Mountain air.\par

Deeper still lay the compost dark, where the Mountain took back what it had spent: kitchen scrap and night-soil and spoiled straw, turned and turned again by men working half-naked in the heat, the worms running thick under their forks. The cistern water here stood green and half-alive, and a keeper drew a measure of the weed from it each day.
So far I had only looked. On the night I chose, the Mountain was quiet after a minor mourning rite. Faith had held the lower Hall late for a child who had died of fever before naming. Such deaths were common enough to pass without shock, but they always altered its sound. The night lamps burned low. The air was dry. No dust cloud veiled the moon beyond the hidden openings. The glint appeared twice before second-night silence, faint and precise.\par

I waited until it appeared a third time. Then I went, and carried no blade; a blade would have made me a frightened Veil, pretending the desert was something metal could bargain with. The passage outward yielded to pressure where the lower stone had been shaved thin. Beyond it the Mountain became less human. Someone had cut handholds and worn them smooth, and carved a line that fingers could find and low light hid. It had none of Light's interpretive softness, none of Balance's measured signs. It existed because some men had to move through places the rest were forbidden even to picture.\par

Cold air came through cracks ahead. I paused before the final opening. The subtler fear was worse than any curse out of the Book of Blood: that what I was calling wisdom, here at the boundary, was hunger. I had an answer ready --- \textit{Light must follow what is revealed} --- and I distrusted it, because it sounded exactly like something I would say to excuse myself. Still, I opened the stone and stepped out.\par

The night desert was another country from the one I had seen by day --- larger, because it showed me almost nothing. The Mountain rose behind me as blackness with weight. I wrapped the mouth cloth against the instinct to breathe too quickly. The air tasted of stone, salt, and distance. 
I kept low in the Mountain's shadow until the ground fell away toward the outer folds. The Oasis lay out of sight below another ridge, hidden even from me. Once I found a line of small stones that might have been natural, except their flatter faces all turned the same way. I circled wider, losing time and confidence. I was Light, and the desert made that plain quickly. My sandals, made for stone, slipped on loose gravel; the cold stiffened my fingers; the water skin knocked against my side until I tied it higher. I misjudged distance again and again --- a rock that seemed close took a thousand breaths to reach. I had memorised the glint's direction from within the Mountain, but without walls my eye had too much to take in, and it kept fooling me.\par

I stopped at the first low ridge and looked back.\par

From there the Mountain looked like a sleeping beast, vast and black, with a few hidden breaths of light where the vents opened under shadow. The land folded around it. The Mountain's shoulder cut the sightlines. A man could die out here within sight of the Oasis, only because the land never gave him the angle to see it.\par

The glint appeared again while I was crossing the second wash --- once, from far ahead and slightly higher than I expected. Too clean. Too brief. A white point, then gone. I froze, crouched, and waited. Nothing. I shifted my head by a finger's width. Nothing.\par

I almost smiled. Then I remembered Va-Sheva saying: \textit{Do not start believing the outside speaks to you.}\par

The ground rose in broken shelves, each one stealing the next from sight. I marked three stones with dust rubs plain only to a trained eye, counted my steps between turns, and drank once. The water tasted of leather and guilt.\par

The outcrop, when I reached it, looked natural at first.\par

From below the glint stayed dark. I moved around the stone slowly, searching for wrongness. A surface too smooth. A crack too straight. A place where the dust failed to gather. Shield would build a place so that no one would think to look for a door at all.\par

I found the polished mineral only after kneeling.\par

It sat in a shallow hollow no wider than my hand, behind a lip of stone: a mineral plate polished by hand and angled toward the Mountain, lost among pale scratches by day, but at the right lamp or moon angle it caught and returned a single point --- a signal only one placed to read it could read.\par

Beside it, almost worn away, was a mark cut into the stone.\par

A threshold line, broken twice, with a small inward notch.\par

Shield.\par

The outcrop was Shield. A point beyond the permitted prayer ground, aligned with Mountain light, invisible to ordinary eyes, reachable only by a dangerous route.\par

I felt no triumph. Light had led me sideways, into another Kahir's secret. The proper thing was to withdraw and report, and let Keeper answer Keeper --- which was also the surest way to have Shield seal the place before I knew what I had seen. I stood in the cold until the choice stopped arguing with itself.\par

I chose to go in and know, whatever it would cost.\par

The mechanism resisted silently, then gave with a thickness that spoke of long maintenance. Air breathed out from within. Dry. Old. Mineral.\par

Full.\par

I slipped inside and pulled the stone nearly closed behind me, leaving a finger's width for air and for return.\par

The passage sloped downward. It was narrow at first, forcing me sideways, then widened into a cut corridor whose walls bore neither Light markings nor Faith text. Dust lay thin over the floor, thin enough to show where feet had disturbed it, though few had passed lately. I held my lamp low and moved slowly.\par

After twenty paces the corridor divided. One branch descended deeper into darkness. The other angled inward toward the Mountain.\par

I chose neither immediately.\par

No entry mark, no return mark, no prayer, no measure, no instruction. Only stone and the smell of preserved air.\par

\textit{Quarantine,} I thought.\par

Then another word came, less precise and more terrible.\par

\textit{Tomb.}\par

I took the descending branch.\par

It ended in a chamber whose first impression was emptiness. Then my lamp found the nearest shelf.\par

Objects.\par

At first only a few: stones, broken blades, small bowls, fragments of painted surface, a corroded shape that might once have been a tool or an idol. Then the flame widened and more shelves appeared, rising along the walls, tier after tier, receding into darkness. A second chamber opened beyond the first, then a third, each cut lower and lined with recesses. No bodies lay there. No bones arranged for burial. Yet death was in it all the same.\par

Civilisations had entered here as fragments.\par

They had stayed.\par

I lifted the lamp to the nearest shelf. Every object was tagged, and every tag was Shield's --- the broken-threshold sign at the top, and beneath it an official hand closed to public reading. Shield alone had catalogued this place; Shield alone had ever been meant to stand in it. The other four Kahirs would have had to be told the room existed before they could so much as fear it. Each tag stated what the object was believed to be, how it had entered, who had handled it, and whether it could be touched, decay, or carry doctrinal danger.\par

Archive, then. Quarantine and memory together.\par

The first object I truly saw was a small bronze figure of a man holding a weapon shaped like three prongs. The body had gone green with age, the beard set in formal curls. Around the base, faint wave marks had been scratched or cast. The Shield tag gave no name I knew, only its own hand: \textit{water-lord; corruption of the deep-water memory; metal stable, salts present, do not wash.}\par

A water god. A man-shaped water power standing proud with a weapon in his hand. Outsiders had taken the memory of deep water and made it into a lord of appetite --- the desire to command water without counting it.\par

Next came a dark figure with the head of an animal on the body of a man. Long snout, upright ears, narrow limbs. Black surface, gold traces, eyes shaped with an unsettling calm. Beneath it lay a cloth fragment marked with faded symbols and a small carving of a wrapped human shape. The Shield tag bore a warning older than the hand that had written it.\par

Death.\par

Outsiders had given death posture, office, beauty. Faith taught that the dead returned under God's keeping. This figure looked as if death itself had been made a Keeper.\par

I looked away first, then forced myself to look back.\par

The next chamber changed material. Painted stone. Carved panels. A fragment of image preserved behind cloudy glass. I held the lamp high and saw a man with a blade raised over a bound youth. The youth lay on something close to an altar. Above the man's hand, a sign of Heaven: lines descending from cloud or fire. The father's face had been damaged, but the blade remained clear.\par

For several breaths the horror escaped me.\par

Then the Book of Blood did it for me. Brother against brother. Father against son. Hunger made law. A broken piece of Gospel called whole.\par

Outsiders had carved a holy story in which a father bound his own son and prepared to kill him while the animal stood spared beside them. They had made obedience into a willingness to murder the child before the goat --- authority coming down on someone helpless, a blade raised by nothing but the right of being the father.\par

The Shield tag below it carried more than the others: \textit{threshold danger; doctrinal contamination; retained for comparative doctrine; image damaged, blade line clear.}\par

Comparative doctrine. A phrase too calm for what it held.\par

The next image was worse, because the people in it looked tender, and what they were gathered around was cruel. A small metal cross, then a larger one of wood darkened by age. Then a painted fragment of people kneeling before a tortured man fixed upon crossed beams. His head drooped. His ribs showed. Wounds marked hands, feet, side. Around him the worshippers lifted their faces in reverence. Some wept. Some reached toward him.\par

I stared.\par

The Mountain had martyrdom --- I granted that now. I had seen Syphiron die. But this was different, or seemed so. Syphiron had cut himself when the link was complete. This man had been fixed, displayed, adored in his suffering. The Outsiders, I thought, had turned pain itself into an altar and then knelt to it.\par

I slowed myself. \textit{Veil, not boy. Interpret after seeing.}\par

I looked again. The worshippers' faces were grieving. Something lived in the tortured man's face that the artifact failed to carry across time, because I lacked the language for it. To the Outsiders, holiness seemed to go in through torture and come out wearing gold.\par

Beyond it, on a lower shelf, stood a figure of a woman unlike any gravure in the Mountain. Many arms spread from her body. Many breasts, or ornaments like breasts, covered her chest. Her face was serene and excessive at once. Around her were small animal shapes, fruits, weapons, and open hands.\par

Fertility, perhaps. Abundance. Or greed dressed up as a goddess.\par

I thought of the Mountain's half measure, its injury allowance, its waste counted, its honey given in amounts so small that sweetness became an event --- and I looked back at the many-breasted figure and saw Outsider appetite given a mother's shape.\par

A false water lord. A death keeper. A father's blade over a son. Worship before torture. Abundance without count. Each might have meant something else to the people who made it; whatever reached me came damaged.\par

The shelves went on past these, older and stranger, more than one lamp and one night could hold, and I let most of them stay dark.\par

Each shelf had its chain: object, source, risk, witness, preservation. \textit{Object recovered beyond Threshold. Object acquired by exchange. Object removed from a dead caravan. Object seized after breach attempt. Object received under exception. Object retained by Shield only. Object forbidden to Faith novices. Object harmless unless taught.}\par

Harmless unless taught.\par

Time became material. Stone became bronze, bronze became iron, iron became glass, glass became paper, paper became photograph.\par

The chamber widened at the modern section. Here the shelves became cabinets, some locked, some open. The air was drier, kept by hidden vents. The labels were newer and more dangerous, and all of them Shield's --- classification, preservation, restriction, the angle from which a thing might be safely looked at. The handwriting varied from tenure to tenure. The system held.\par

That was where I felt the danger.\par

Old corrupted gods could be explained --- Shield guarded the threshold, and a threshold needs a guard who has seen what waits beyond it. I could accept that, uneasily.\par

Then I saw the photographs.\par

The first showed a woman under hard light. Her blonde hair was tied back. Her clothes were unlike anything in the Mountain --- practical, pale, shaped for heat, marked by pockets and seams whose purpose escaped me. Beside her stood a man in desert clothing, his face lined, his posture loose in the way Shield posture loosened only after readiness had entered the bones. Behind them, partly visible, was a rock face I recognised with a sudden bodily certainty.\par

Another place, near the Mountain's outer folds.\par

The lower white border carried marks in an outsider hand. Beneath them, in careful Shield script, someone had copied two names.\par

Sarah Blackwood.\par

Amastan.\par

I stared at the woman. Recent enough that the colours still held, that the Mountain's current elders might have been breathing when she was. I had never heard the name, and finding it here, in a Shield drawer, was worse than if I had.\par

--- Sarah Blackwook.

--- Sarah Blackwood indeed.
\end{room}

The second photograph showed no face. Only desert, distance, and a terrible flower of light rising from the horizon, too large to be flame and too ordered to be storm. The sky around it had been bleached almost empty. On the lower border, in the same unfamiliar hand, were marks I had never learned to read. One sequence had been copied beneath in Shield script:\par

Reggane, 1960.\par

The word and the numbers meant nothing to me --- a place, a date, a wound the Outsiders had set down because even they had been frightened by what their own hands had made. What that flower was lay beyond me. All I knew was that it was too large to leave.\par

I set the two photographs down as if they might accuse me of touching them.\par

The cabinet below held maps, some in outsider lines, some in Shield marks: Mountain folds, dry washes, glint points, hidden sightlines, the Oasis shown by the blank space the routes bent around. And a route leading from the outcrop inward, splitting toward the Labyrinth and, impossibly, toward service routes near the Hall of Ceremony.\par

I unfolded another map. This one bore names in scripts beyond my reading, but three had been copied in Mountain hand.\par

Amastan. Sarah. Ka-Xhian.\par

Ka. A Keeper.\par

Shield had recorded the contact with a title.\par

Lines connected the names to dates, routes, and small boxed terms. \textit{Breach. Containment. Exchange. Loss. Sealed. Limited disclosure. Threshold Exception.}\par

Exchange.\par

I read the word three times.\par

At the back of the map cabinet lay a Shield record tablet.\par

I lifted it with both hands. The script was compressed, official, closed to public reading. It preserved a sequence, and that was more dangerous.\par

\textit{Contact maintained under Threshold Exception.}\par

\textit{Outsider asset contained beyond full breach.}\par

\textit{Amastan intermediary confirmed.}\par

\textit{Sarah Blackwood retained under observation.}\par

\textit{Ka-Xhian authorised limited disclosure.}\par

\textit{Horizon note sealed.}\par

The last line was a word no lesson had ever given me, and the tablet let it stay sealed.\par


I set the tablet down carefully, because my hands had begun to shake.\par

I arranged the facts. The chamber ran past old proof into recent contact: records of Sarah Blackwood and Amastan; a Keeper, Ka-Xhian, who had authorised disclosure under something called \textit{Threshold Exception}; maps of routes and stations no hidden man was ever taught. Shield had kept it all in sequence, in its own closed hand, and none of it had ever been spoken in Genesis, Blood, Beast, Cataclysm, Covenant, ceremony, lesson, or public record.\par

It proved less than treason. Divided knowledge was law; an exception could be mercy. Yet even correcting for every doctrine I had been trained to correct for, the central fact stood and would not fall: Shield had stood before the people as the threshold against Outsiders while keeping its own channels to them, and had watched children kneel before Blood and Beast while holding photographs of the murderer's children in a secret drawer.\par

And Va-Sheva belonged to Shield.\par

This lay beyond Va-Sheva. She was Veil, not Keeper --- young for the office she held. She might know nothing of this chamber. Or she might know everything, and have looked at me in the Oasis while standing inside a lie so large it had become her natural air.\par

I closed the drawer. The sound was soft, and even so it seemed too final.\par

Beyond the modern cabinets a narrow passage led back toward the Mountain, and I followed it with the lamp held low.\par

The smell reached me before the room did. The archive had been dry mineral; this was older air gone wrong --- piss, and sweat, and under both of them the flat iron of blood. The passage opened into a low cell cut from raw stone. A single fixed seat. Rings set into the rock at the height of a man's wrists and his ankles. A shallow gutter worked into the floor and sloped to a drain. The walls were thick, and the stone drank sound; I clapped once, softly, and the room gave me back nothing. A man could be held here and worked on for as long as it took, off every map and beyond every permitted route, and no living soul in the Mountain would hear him call.\par

Someone had called. The stains had gone down into the seams of the floor and dried there black.\par

Near the foot of the seat, pressed into the stone where the blood had set around it and held it fast, lay a narrow band of woven cloth. I knew it before I let myself know it. I had spent too many hours in the Oasis with nothing to do but look at her, and a man remembers what he has looked at too long. It was a ribbon from Va-Sheva's hair. There was no mistaking the weave of it, nor the colour, though half the colour had gone down into the dark stain that fixed it to the floor. It was hers. It was in the stone, and it was in the blood.\par

I knelt with the lamp, and I did not touch it.\par

Past the cell the passage ended at a sealed door, and the door bore one mark only, cut deep and cut old: Shield, and beneath it Shield again, and beneath that a Shield seal I had never been taught, older than Ka-Raedin's tenure, perhaps older than Syphiron's. Whatever lay behind it, Shield had sealed for Shield, and had never once meant a Veil of Light to be standing in this passage to read the sign at all.\par

I left the door sealed.\par

The old Sa-Kailan would have pressed, searched, scratched, risked the alarms, and told himself that holding back was cowardice and forcing the door was his duty. The proof I already held was more dangerous than anything I could force before dawn.\par

I had spent enough for one night.\par

To take anything tagged was breach, and would leave a gap in the count. I needed something slight enough that its absence would pass, and found it in a loose copy fragment at the back of the map bundle --- a scrap bearing four entries in Mountain hand:\par

Sarah Blackwood. Amastan. Ka-Xhian Of Shield. Threshold Exception.\par

I folded it once and placed it inside the inner seam of my robe. Then I stood in the Tomb --- or the Chamber of Thresholds, or whatever Shield named this place when it dared name it at all --- and let the full weight of it enter me.\par

It was the name that undid me. Chamber of Thresholds. I had crossed one to stand in it, and I understood at last what the whole Mountain was, above in the light and down here in the dark alike: an engine for the governing of crossings, and this room its hidden heart. My people had let all of it in --- the water-lord, the death-keeper, the father with the blade, the adored and tortured man --- and had kept each one by forbidding it to mean anything. The statue was admitted; its god was held at the door. Take in the body, and quarantine the meaning. Let a beam pass a wall, so long as no man passed with it. Let a boy walk down into the Oasis, if the water could wash the desert off him at the gate.\par

And kneeling there I saw why the art must fail, and had perhaps already failed in me. A threshold runs one way. A man may cross it; he cannot cross back into the one who had not. The dust washes off. The flash does not. They had been right to fear every object on these shelves, because the one thing no gate holds at the door is what has already gone in through an eye. I had come to learn what this room kept, and in the learning I had become the thing it was built to hold out --- a place inside the Mountain where the outside had entered and could never again be made to mean nothing. The scrap folded in my seam was a courtesy to my own memory. I was already the breach.\par

The Outsiders had remembered broken pieces; Blood had drawn its warnings from something real. Had the doctrine been merely false I could have thrown it out, but it was true, and being used to cover an exception, and there was no clean place to stand in that.\par

I lifted the lamp and looked once more at Sarah Blackwood's face. The Outsider woman stood under desert light, half squinting. She looked ordinary. I had been taught that the dangerous ones rarely looked dangerous at first.\par

I lowered the lamp.\par

I would hold my rage. I would keep away from Va-Sheva --- whose ribbon was set in blood in a room I had no name for, and who was either the hand that had drawn blood there or the girl who had left it, and I lacked the strength that night to learn which. I would keep the evidence in my hands. I would not spill it in front of Ka-Raedin like a child demanding the Mountain rearrange itself around his outrage. I would think. Sequence mattered. Witness mattered. A word in the wrong place could let them call it all a breach before the truth in it had a chance to hold.\par

I left as carefully as I had entered.\par

The return through the passage felt longer. The sky had paled at its lowest edge --- a warning before sunrise. I moved faster than I should have and slower than fear wanted. The marks I had made on the stones brought me back across the washes. Twice I stopped to restore disturbed dust with the side of my sandal. Nothing came. Or something came and stayed hidden.\par

When I reached the hidden vent above the old air chamber, my hands were numb, my mouth dry, and the water skin nearly empty. The Mountain received me without welcome. Stone closed behind me. Warmth returned gradually --- first as smell, then as pressure, then as accusation.\par

I replaced the grille, checked the seams, brushed grit from the lower ledge, and stood in the darkness until my breathing no longer sounded like a confession.\par

Inside my robe, the scrap touched my skin.\par

Sarah Blackwood. Amastan. Ka-Xhian. Threshold Exception.\par

I left it folded. Every word of it was already in me.\par

By the First hour I stood in the eastern chamber before Ka-Raedin arrived --- washed, robed, composed, no dust on my sandals and no sign that the world beneath the Mountain had opened sideways in the night. When the Keeper entered, his eyes moved once over my face, then down to my hands, then to the floor around my feet.\par

``You slept poorly,'' he said.\par

``Yes, Keeper.''\par

``Why?''\par

I looked at him. There had been a time when the question would have frightened me into cleverness. Now I let the silence hold.\par

``Because the title is heavier in the dark,'' I said.\par

He studied me.\par

``Good.''\par

He turned toward the basins.\par

``Today we learn a new interference pattern.''\par

I followed.\par

I had found something badly wrong, and I had not yet earned the standing to say so aloud.\par
