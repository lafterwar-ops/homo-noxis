\typesetchapter{Chapter 22}{Noxius Zero: The Loss}

We read him together, in the Archive, in the equinox light, taking the sheets in turn: Paul, who could hear a voice in a text the way I could see a hand in a stone; Sarah, who had hunted this man across forty years without knowing his name; and I, who was about to learn that I had been reading my own possible life all along and calling it another man's confession.\par

I will set his account down as he wrote it, in his own first person, because that is how it must be met, the way I made Paul meet mine. Where I break in, I will say so. The rest is his. I ask you to notice, as you read him, that he never once says the woman's name and his own project in the same breath, and that this is the whole of him, and that he did not know it.\par

What follows is Zero's.\par

\ornament

Let it be recorded, by the one who will not be recorded elsewhere, that what follows was chosen and not received; that a mind stood behind it; and that the mind was mine.\par

I was born into the last generation of my people that still believed itself the first of the world, and I will not pretend to you, whoever you are, whatever you are, that we were a small people, or a rude one, or a people you would have pitied had you passed us on a road. We were the accomplishment of the world. We had held the cold lands and the warm for longer than any count then kept; we had made tools that other tools could not improve upon for an age; we knew the movements of the lights in the sky and had names for them and songs that fixed the names; we buried our dead with intention and marked the places, and we did not think ourselves clever for any of it, because a people at the summit of the world does not think itself clever any more than a mountain thinks itself tall. We simply were the shape the world had been climbing toward. Everyone knew it. It was the one thing that did not need to be said, which is always the most dangerous thing a people can possess, because a thing that does not need to be said is a thing no one has examined in a hundred generations.\par

I will give you one image of us, so that you do not do what the scripture I later built did, and picture us as brutes with clubs, because the ones who win always draw the ones they replaced as beasts; it makes the replacing easier to live with. Picture, then, a hall of my people deep in the warm rock at the height of a winter, lit by lamps that burned a rendered fat we had spent an age learning to make burn clean and tall; and in that hall, old men reciting the descent of a line for eleven generations without a single error, because to lose a generation of the line was to lose a piece of the world, and the reciting was checked, name against name, by others who had learned it apart, so that no one failing memory could corrupt the whole; and the young sitting to learn it, not because they were made to, but because there was nowhere in that world they would rather be than at the centre of the thing that knew itself completely. We did not write, or we wrote little and buried it. We kept the world in trained heads, cross-checked, redundant, a library of persons. It was the most beautiful engine for keeping a truth that has ever existed, and it had one flaw, and the flaw is what killed us: a library of persons is only as loyal as the persons, and a person can walk away. A written thing cannot come to prefer the neighbours. A person can. We had built our whole immortality out of the one material in the world that is able to choose to leave, and we had never once had to notice, because for an age no one wished to.\par

And we were losing, and I was the first of us, I think, to say the word, because I was the first of us positioned to see the whole of it and cursed with the kind of mind that cannot leave a whole unlooked-at.\par

We were not losing a war. I want that understood before anything else, because the ones who came after me made a scripture of the losing and turned it into a war, into murder, into a brother slain in a field, and that is a lie, or rather it is the truth a wounded people tells itself so that it can go on standing. There was no field. There was no single murder. What was killing us could not be met with a blade, because it did not come as an enemy. It came as a preference.\par

I saw it first in my own cousins' children. A girl of our line, raised to the completeness, who began to go down to the new people's fires in the evenings; at first to trade, then to listen, then for no reason she could give when we asked her, which was itself the sign, because a person of our people could always give a reason, we were made of reasons. She could not say why she went. She said only that it was warmer there, and she did not mean the fires. Within two winters she had gone, not fled, not taken, simply gone, married into their easy mess and carrying children who would hold a quarter of us and none of our knowing; and when her mother went down to bring her home, the girl wept, and would not come, and could not say why. Her mother came back up into our clean cross-checked hall and sat among the reciting old men, and I watched a thing go out of her that never returned, which was the belief that being right was a defence. That is how we died. Not in a battle we could have trained a generation to fight. One beloved daughter at a time, walking downhill toward the warmth, unable to say why, and leaving us standing in the cold at the summit of a world that had quietly stopped wishing to be saved.\par

The other people, the new people, the ones who had come up out of the warm south in my grandfather's time and whom we had at first barely troubled to notice, the way you do not trouble to notice weather that is not yet a storm, were fewer than us, and weaker than us in the body, and knew less than us of nearly everything that we had measured our worth by for an age. And they were winning, and they were winning in the one arena against which our whole accomplishment was no defence at all: they were winning our children. Not by taking them. By being, to them, more pleasant to be near.\par

\ornament

Sarah laid the sheet down here and did not speak for a while, and then she said, ``He is describing demographic collapse by assimilation, and he is describing it correctly, and it is the one form of a people's ending that no wall and no weapon has ever been able to stop, because you cannot fortify against your own children preferring the neighbours. My whole century argued about it and never said it as cleanly as a man a hundred thousand years dead.'' Paul said, ``He is also describing it as a man who has lost one specific person and has not admitted it yet. Listen to how he writes \emph{our children} when the sheets under this one are going to be about one woman. Keep reading. Watch him not say her name.'' And I said nothing, because I had begun, without permission, to be afraid of this man, and I did not yet know why.\par

\ornament

Her name was Ithe, and I will say it here once, against my own rule, because a record that hides the one true thing is the same lie I built the Mountain to tell, and I have decided, in my old age, at the bottom of my own machine, to tell the truth in at least one place, and let it be here, and let it be her.\par

Ithe was of my own people and of my own rank, which in our world meant that she had been raised, as I had, to a certain completeness: to know the songs and the star-names and the long descent of our line, to carry herself as a person carries herself who has never once been made to feel small, to think in the wide unhurried way that only the secure can think. She was the most finished person I have ever known. And she left me for a man who was none of those things, and I have spent a hundred and thirty-five thousand years understanding it, and I understand it now, and I will set the understanding down, and you will see that the understanding did not save me, because understanding a wound is not the same as closing it, and I would give the whole of what I built to have learned that lesson before I built it rather than after.\par

The man was of the new people. I will not give him a name because I never learned one I trusted; his people gave names the way they gave everything, easily, and changed them, and I found this, at the time, further proof of their shallowness, and I found later that it was proof of a freedom I did not have the courage to want. He was a singer. That is the plainest true thing I can say of him. Where we made songs to fix a name so that it would survive, he made songs to spend an evening, songs that fixed nothing and preserved nothing and were gone by morning, and people wept and laughed and touched one another under them in a way my people had forgotten was permitted, or had never learned, or had traded, somewhere in our long climb, for the dignity of the summit.\par

He was not a good man by any measure my people kept, and I say this without the comfort of it being a lie: he was violent, in the sudden bright way of his people, quick to a blow and quick past it; he was faithless by our lights, and boastful, and he took what he wanted and was forgiven for it because the taking had a kind of joy in it that our virtue had never learned to counterfeit. I have watched him do a cruel thing in the morning and a tender one at dusk and be, to the people around him, the same beloved man in both, because his people did not require a person to be one thing, and mine required it above all else. That was the whole of the difference, in the end. We had made a civilisation in which a person had to be one consistent, finished, defensible thing, forever, on pain of ceasing to be respectable. And they had made a shabby, violent, glorious mess in which a person was permitted to be many things in one day and loved in all of them.\par

I will give you the evening I understood I had lost her, though I did not admit the understanding for a year, because a man of my completeness does not admit a thing until he has first defeated it in argument, and I could not defeat this. There was a gathering, one of theirs, that some of ours had begun to drift to, and I went, to observe, I told myself, to know the enemy; and Ithe was there before me, which I had not known, and she did not see me come in. The singer was singing. It was nothing, the song, a small foolish thing about a boat and a girl and a fish that got away, the kind of thing my people would have found beneath the dignity of memory. And Ithe was laughing at it. Not the measured laugh she gave at our own gatherings, the laugh that was itself an accomplishment, performed for people watching it be performed. She was laughing the way water laughs, helplessly, at a foolish song about a fish, her head back and her whole finished dignity forgotten on the ground beside her like a coat she had taken off because the room was warm. I had never once made her laugh like that. I had never tried. It had never occurred to me that it was a thing a person of our rank was permitted to want. And I stood in the doorway of the enemy's warm and shabby hall and understood that the summit of the world had never learned to do the one thing that fish-song did without effort, and that we were going to lose everything to people who could make a woman forget her dignity and be glad that she had.\par

Ithe went to him because he let her be more than one thing. That is the sentence I could not write for a hundred thousand years and can write now. With me she was the finished person she had been raised to be, and I loved that person, and I required her, without ever once saying it, to keep being only that person, because her completeness was the mirror of my own and I could not bear a crack in it. With him she was allowed to be silly, and frightened, and greedy, and wrong, and changeable, and to sing a song that fixed nothing, and to weep at it, and to be, the next morning, ashamed of nothing. He did not treat her as the last hope of a declining people. He treated her as a woman he wanted on a particular evening. And it turned out, to my endless instruction, that a person would rather be wanted for an evening than revered for an age.\par

I went to her once, near the end, when it was already decided in everything but the words, and I did the thing I had never once done in all our years, which was to ask her for something plainly, without first arranging to be certain of the answer. I asked her to stay. And she did me the honour, because she was to the last a person of our people, and our people did not lie to spare a feeling, of telling me the truth. She said that with me she had always known exactly who she was, and that it had been like living inside a beautiful and perfectly made room that had no door, and that she had not known the room had no door until she had stood in the singer's shabby field and felt the wind. \emph{You never did anything wrong,} she said. \emph{That is the worst of it. If you had wronged me I could have stayed and been angry. You only ever revered me, and I cannot spend the rest of my life being revered, because a reverence is a thing you must keep still to receive, and I am so tired of keeping still.} And then she said the thing I built the whole Mountain in order to unhear, and I will write it once, and seal it here, and it is the last I will say of her. \emph{You will make something terrible out of this. I know you. You cannot lose a small thing small. Please try. Please, for once in your life, let a thing be only the size that it is.} I did not try. You are standing in what I made instead.\par

\ornament

Here I stopped reading, because I could not go on, and Paul saw it and took the sheet from my hands without a word, and it was Va-Sheva, who had come down to the Archive and stood at the edge of the light where I had not seen her, who said the thing none of the rest of them would say to me.\par

``You have gone the colour of the stone,'' she said. ``Say why, or do not, but do not pretend it is the cold.'' And I could not say why, in front of her, of all the people who have ever lived, because the why was that I had just read, in the hand of a man a hundred thousand years dead, the exact shape of my own worst night, the night in a country I could not find when a woman I had spent years standing in front of and requiring to be one finished thing had stepped, for the length of one breath, into a man who bought sweets for no reason, and been, in that breath, more than one thing, and glad. I had told myself, that night and every night since, that it was a wound about her, and about him, and about me. I had just been shown that it was a wound about my whole species, ninety thousand generations deep, and that a man had once felt it exactly as I felt it and had answered it by building the thing that made me, and I did not yet know what I was going to do with the fact that his answer was the Mountain, and that I was standing in it, and that some cold ancestral part of me understood the answer completely and was not sure it was wrong.\par

\ornament

I did not, at first, decide to build anything. I want the record to be exact about the order of it, because the order is the whole indictment. First there was the wound. Then there was the thinking, which dressed the wound in the language of a people and a crisis, because a man of my rank does not permit himself a private grief; he translates it upward, into a matter of state, where it can be dignified and funded and made to look like duty. And only then, third, last, dressed and armoured and no longer recognisable even to me as the thing it had grown from, came the plan.\par

And here is the thing I most need a reader to understand, because it is the thing that made what I did possible, and the thing that makes it unforgivable, and they are the same thing. The true parts were not a disguise for the wounded part. A simple man would think that: that I dressed a heartbreak up as a cause, and the cause was a costume, and under the costume was only a jilted man. It was worse than that. The true parts were true. My people really were ending. The loss really was vast. Had I been the coldest and most whole man who ever lived, with no woman anywhere in it, the argument would still have stood, every step of it, and might have led that cold whole man to build the very thing I built. That is why I could not see the wound at its work: because the wound was not lying. The wound had simply chosen, out of all the true things in the world, the one true thing large enough to grow to the size of the Earth, and had pointed me at it, and said, truthfully, \emph{this matters}, and I had followed the true thing all the way down into the dark, and never once had to feel whose hand was on my back. A lie, a man can catch. A grief that has taught itself to speak only true sentences, you will follow to the end of the world.\par

The thinking went as follows, and I will give it to you in its own cold voice, because you should see how good it was, how nearly right, how a true thing and a wounded thing can be braided so tightly that not even the man who braids them can afterward find the seam. My people were ending. That was true. We were ending not by defeat but by preference, our blood walking willingly into theirs, generation on generation, until in some near century there would be no one left who carried the whole of what we had been. That was true. And a thing of great value was therefore about to vanish from the world, not with a war worth remembering but with a shrug, a slow glad shrug across a thousand years, and no one would mark its passing because the people best equipped to mark it would by then be them. That was true. And it was intolerable that the summit of the world should end in a shrug. All true. Every step of it true. And under every step, unspeakable, unspoken, the other thing, the small thing, the only thing: that she had shrugged, and gone, and been glad.\par

\ornament

Paul stopped reading and said, quietly, to the room, ``This is the most dangerous document I have ever held, and I have held a plan to end the world. Because everything he says is true. The demographics are true. The loss is true. The value of what was being lost is true. And it is all a lie, because none of it is why. He is a heartbroken man building a monument to being left, and he has draped it in a species so that no one, including him, will ever have to see a heartbroken man.'' And Sarah, who knew more than any of us about draping a private appetite in a public reason, said only, ``Yes. I wrote the same document. Mine was shorter. Keep reading.''\par

I did not say what I was thinking, which was that I had written it too, or very nearly, on the night my council cast me out, when I had stood in the desert with my whole life taken from me and begun, already, to compose the doctrine of my grievance, the account in which I was the wronged whole and my people the shrugging betrayers who had earned whatever came to them. I had been saved from finishing it by nothing grander than a fact-checker with a machine, who made me say the small true things, one at a time, until the large false one had no room left in me to grow. I had been one night's kindness away from becoming the man on the sheet in Paul's hand. And I looked at the cold beautiful reasoning of him, and I felt it call to me, the way a low door calls to a man who has spent his whole life being told to stoop, and I was afraid, in the equinox light, of how much of me still wanted to agree with him.\par

\ornament

My father held more of the world than any man of our people then living. I will not pretend to you that I came to my thinking as a poor man comes to a dream, harmlessly, for a poor man's dreams die in him and harm no one. I came to it as the son of the one man who could make a dream cost the world, and that is the difference between a grief and a catastrophe: not the size of the wound, but the size of the hand the wounded man can reach.\par

He heard me out. I had expected to have to persuade him, to marshal the demographics and the star-count of the generations, to make him see the ending as I saw it. I did not have to. He had seen it before me; he had simply, in the way of his generation, decided that a thing you cannot stop is a thing you do not speak of, and had gone on holding the world and saying nothing, and grieving privately, as we all grieved privately, because privacy of grief was the last dignity our completeness allowed us. And when his son came to him with a plan that promised that the summit of the world need not end in a shrug, that something of us, the true whole of us, our knowledge and our order and our long climb, could be taken somewhere the preference could not reach it and kept, kept alive, kept whole, against the slow glad drowning of our blood, he did not weigh the cost, because I had offered him the one thing a proud man losing everything cannot refuse, which is a way to not surrender.\par

He asked me only one question, at the very end, and I have carried it a hundred thousand years, because it was the one moment he came near to stopping me, and did not. He asked: \emph{And the people we save. Will they know what they are?} I did not understand him at first. He meant: will the ones we seal away, the kept seed, the salvaged summit, will they know that they are the last of us, carrying the whole on purpose; or will you have to make them forget in order to make them carry it. He had seen, in a single question, the whole rot at the centre of the plan that I had not yet let myself see, that a truth kept by persons is only kept if the persons stay, and persons stay only if they cannot imagine leaving, and you cannot make a free person unable to imagine leaving except by taking the freedom, generation upon generation, until they no longer know that it was ever theirs. I did not answer him honestly, because I did not yet have the honest answer, or because I did and could not bear to hold it. I said, \emph{They will know what they need to know.} And he looked at me a long moment, my father, who could have ended it with a word, who commanded everything I was about to spend, and he chose, as I would choose after him, and as five judges after me would choose in their turn, the comfort of the unasked question. He nodded. He looked away. He gave me the world. It is the family failing, and it is older than my family, and I have watched it work in every hand that ever built a terrible thing: we would all of us, every one, rather hand over everything we have than ask the one question whose answer we already know.\par

He gave me everything. I want the enormity of that set down plainly, because it is the hinge of the whole history that followed, and because it is the answer to the question every reader of this will eventually ask, which is how one man could possibly have built the impossible thing I built. He could not have. A man could not have. But a man with a father who would rather fund the impossible than admit the inevitable, and who commanded the labour and the metal and the knowledge and the armed strength of the greatest of our peoples, and who loved his son, and who was too proud to grieve out loud and so grieved by giving, without limit, without audit, without ever once asking the one question that would have stopped it, which was \emph{how large will this have to be}, because he did not want to know, because knowing would have made it a decision instead of a gift: such a man could hand his heartbroken boy the means to change the shape of the Earth. And he did. And I took it. And I never told him, and he never asked, what it was really for, and we understood each other perfectly, in the way of a father and a son who are both grieving the same loss and have agreed to call it something else.\par

\ornament

Sarah put the sheet down at that and sat with it, and I saw her go somewhere far off, into a cellar, among a dead father's papers. ``My father asked me the same question,'' she said, ``in his way, near the end, and I gave him the same answer, which is no answer, and he did the same thing this one's father did, which is look away and let me have the money. They always look away. That is the whole engine of every terrible thing that was ever well funded: a proud man who would rather look away than ask his child the one question, and a child who has already composed the lie that lets him. I have been on both ends of that look. I was the one who would not ask, with the men I sent into that desert to their deaths. And I was the child with the lie ready, forty years ago, in a stone room, in front of a blind man who let me in. This man is not describing his family, boy. He is describing mine. He is describing everyone's.'' She picked the sheet back up. ``Keep reading. I want to know whether he ever asks it. I never did.''\par

\ornament

I left in the spring, with more than I will count here, because the counting shames me and because a later sheet holds it: engineers, and those who commanded them; the ones who knew stone and the ones who knew water and the ones who knew the sky; metal, and the men to work it; and soldiers, because a thing this valuable must be guarded, and because a plan born from a wound always, always makes a place in itself for men with weapons, long before it can say aloud what they are for. I had my father's authority to go, and to take, and to seek a final place, a place defensible past defeating, hidden past finding, provisioned past any siege, where the whole of what we were could be set down and sealed and kept until the preference had run its course above and the world could be begun again, correctly, by us, from the kept seed.\par

I took also, and I set it down here because it is the seed of the cruellest thing I did, the young. Not only engineers and soldiers but children: some of ours, some bought, some gathered from the failing edges of our people, because a place that must outlive its own understanding has to be peopled by those still young enough to be made to believe it was always so. I told myself they were being saved. They were being saved. And they were also being made into the first generation of a civilisation that would be taught, from the cradle, a past that never happened, so that they would tend a machine they must never comprehend, and kneel to a god I was about to invent, and I looked at them on the road, the children, asleep in the carts among the metal, and I did not turn back, because by then the wound had finished its work in me and I was no longer a man who could turn back. I was a plan that had learned to walk.\par

The road south took the better part of a year, and I lost people on it, to the heat and the distance and to the slow leaking-away that is how a great column dies in a dead country, and each loss I entered in a reckoning and did not mourn, because a man who has decided to save the whole learns very quickly how to spend the parts. We passed the last of the green, and then the last of the water that anyone but my water-men could find, and then the last of the marks of any people at all, and went on into a country that had never wanted anyone and said so every hour of the day. My engineers began to tell me, carefully, in the way men speak to a lord they have begun to fear, that there might be no place in such a country able to hold what I meant to hold; that I was hunting a thing which did not exist. I did not turn back. I had stopped, somewhere on that road, being able to imagine an outcome in which I turned back, and I called that strength at the time. It was not strength. It was the wound, grown so large by then that it had eaten the horizon, so that there was no longer any direction anywhere that led to anything but forward, into the stone, toward the thing I would build to make certain the world still held one room that she had not walked out of.\par

I did not yet know where. I knew only the shape of what I needed, which was a place the new people would never want, because they wanted warmth and company and the easy south, and so I turned away from everything my people had ever found good, and went toward the one country that offered none of it, the dead country, the country of thirst and distance and stone, because a thing you wish never to be found must be hidden inside the one place no one who loves life would ever look. I went into the desert. I was young. I believed I was saving the world. I have had a hundred and thirty-five thousand years to notice that I never once, in all my thinking, in all my father's giving, in all the long road south into the killing ground, said her name, and that I was doing the whole of it, the engineers and the metal and the soldiers and the sealed and salvaged summit of the world, so that somewhere, in some form, in some kept and frozen shape, there would go on existing a world in which I had not been left.\par

\ornament

Paul set down the last sheet of that day very gently, the way you set down a sleeping child, and no one spoke, and the equinox beam had moved off the machine and onto the wall while we read, the way it moves, a year at a time, patient, indifferent. And I found that they were all looking at me, Paul and Sarah and Va-Sheva, not unkindly, and waiting, and I understood that they had all three arrived, by their different roads, at the thing I had been refusing since the first sheet, and were waiting to see whether I would say it before they had to.\par

So I said it. ``He sounds like me,'' I said. ``He reasons the way I reason. He turns a hurt into a doctrine the way I was about to turn mine. He builds a wall against a woman and calls it a wall against the world.'' It cost me more than I have said in this whole account to say it, and Va-Sheva, who was the woman in my own version of his story, watched me say it and did not spare me and did not comfort me, which was the truest thing she could have done, and only said, ``Yes. He does. Now read the rest, and find out whether that is a warning or a permission. Because you have not yet decided which, and I can see you have not, and that is the only thing in this Mountain that still frightens me.''\par

I did not answer her, because she was right, and because the honest answer would have been to say that I did not know; that I had read a hundred thousand years down into the root of my own kind and found a man built to exactly my shape, holding exactly my wound; and that the thing I felt looking at him was not horror, or not only horror, but a terrible companionship, the relief of the badly alone at learning that someone, once, anyone, had felt the precise thing and been unable to bear it either. I had come down that stair believing I was about to read the confession of a monster, and be set free by the distance between us. There was no distance. That was the whole of what the Archive had to teach me, and it taught it on the first day, and everything that came after, the Mountain and the drill and the children and the god, I read across that one closed gap, the way a man reads the history of a country while standing in the middle of it.\par
