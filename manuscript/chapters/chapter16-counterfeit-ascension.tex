\typesetchapter{Chapter 16}{Counterfeit Ascension}

\begin{room}

--- Then it is not a rising.

--- It is a rising. I have stood in the room where that shape is made. I would know it with my hands cut off.

--- It is the shape of a rising, and it is not a rising, because nothing you have ever told me rises twice inside an hour, and nothing rises a third time before the second has gone cold, and it is going to draw a third. Watch the drum. There. No Keeper of yours died three times before this lamp was lit. Something is making your Mountain say the word. Over, and over, and it is not tiring.

--- Madame. The interval is holding. Eleven minutes, near enough, between one and the next. It does not vary.

--- Of course it does not vary. A death does not keep an appointment. A machine keeps an appointment. \ldots{} Bring me the drill cabinet. All of it. The whole run, back to the first paper I ever folded, and the great seals with them. Do not walk. Run.

--- I do not understand what I am looking at, and it is my trade to say so out loud. You have told me for two days that this shape is the sound of one of his Keepers dying and another taking the office. Now it comes every eleven minutes. Keepers are not dying every eleven minutes. So the shape is lying about what made it. What makes a true thing lie?

--- A forgery makes a true thing lie. Someone out there has learned the one note this Mountain answers to, and is striking it, and standing back, and writing down the answer.

\end{room}

The machine kept turning while she said that, and it is the last of the whole week that the machine ever held entire, so I will tell the rest of that night the way it came to me, because after that hour there was no longer a man in a chair and a man with a question between us. There was a house waking in the dark, and five thousand souls four weeks' walk to the south who did not yet know that somebody had found their door and begun to knock upon it to hear the shape of the hollow behind.\par

They brought the cabinet, and she laid the papers out under the long lamp, forty years of them, the small old folded ones and the great wide ones, and she set beside them the new sheet the drums had drawn while we argued, and she read them the way I had watched her read me: fast, and cold, and without hope of being wrong. Paul stood at her shoulder. I stood where I could see her face and not the drums, because I had learned that in that house the faces told the truer story, and hers, which had not moved once in the days she spent confessing to a lifetime of theft against my people, moved now.\par

``These,'' she said, and her hand was flat on the old folded papers, ``are the true ones. Every one of them small, and rare, and years apart, and each one a death. And this'' --- the new sheet --- ``wants to be their brother, and it is not. Look at the tail of it. A true knock strikes and the Mountain answers once, deep, and rolls over, and sleeps. This one strikes and the answer does not come from under. It comes from the sides. It comes from everywhere at once, because it was not made under the Mountain at all. It was made around it.''\par

Paul said the thing I could not, being no reader of their drawings. ``Around it. You mean the source is outside. On the surface. Not in the Hall.''\par

``I mean,'' she said, ``that someone has put a machine on the desert floor that shakes the ground on purpose, a hard, small, exact shake, and then listens for the way the Mountain gives it back. And then they move the machine, and do it again, from a new place. And again. Eleven minutes to strike, and read, and haul the thing a mile, and strike again.'' She straightened. ``It is called sounding. You throw a known noise at a thing you cannot see into, and the echo draws you its insides. It is how I have mapped a hundred ore-bodies I never laid an eye on. It is how you map anything too big and too dark to enter.'' She looked, then, like a woman to whom something had been done. All week she had been the one doing. ``Someone is mapping the inside of your Mountain by making it ring.''\par

I understood it in the only shape I had for it, and the shape was the Bowl. I had told Paul, days before, of the Bowl of the Last Prayer that gives you back the true note off its Mouth if you speak the true note into its Ear, and gives you silence for a false one. I had thought it a holy thing and a small one. Now a woman in a cold country was telling me that the whole Mountain was such a bowl, and that a stranger had sat down before its Ear and was patiently trying notes until he found the ones it would answer, and had found one, and was singing it back into the stone again and again to hear the great hollow ring, to draw himself a map of every gallery and stair and hidden water and, though he could not know it, of the one chamber that must never be sung to at all.\par

Because that is the part that stopped my breath, and I said it aloud into that room full of clever frightened people, and the woman looked at me then the way you look at another mind, where all week she had looked at me the way you look at an exhibit. The note they had found, the one the Mountain answered, the shape that wore the face of a rising --- it was a rising. It was the very note the five strike, behind their door, on the morning a Keeper takes the office. It is the note the mechanism beneath us is tuned to answer, because answering it is the whole of what the mechanism is for. Every Ascension in eleven thousand years has been a controlled and holy sounding of the one string in the world you must never pluck idly, struck by the only hands trained to strike it and then to still it. And now a stranger with a machine, who thought he was mapping ore, had found that same string by accident, and was plucking it every eleven minutes, and standing back to admire the echo, with no hand anywhere near the mechanism to still what he was waking.\par

\begin{room}

--- Say the danger in one sentence, so that I have it clean on the record, whatever the record is now for.

--- The danger is that the door they are knocking on to draw their little map is the door of the thing that ends the world, and that knocking is how you open it.

\end{room}

She did not leave it at the one sentence, because Paul asked the question that lives under it, which was how long, and she would not give him a soft answer to be kind. ``I do not know,'' she said, ``because no one has ever been ignorant enough to try this before, and so there is no one to have measured it. A resonance does not weaken and then fail. It gathers. Every stroke that finds the true note adds a little to what the last stroke left standing, if the strokes come faster than the stone can forget them. Eleven minutes apart may be far too slow to matter. It may be exactly slow enough to matter across a year. And it will not stay eleven minutes.'' She put her finger back on the lattice of shot-points. ``They will tighten this. They always tighten it. They will get a coarse map from the wide spacing, and being men, they will want a finer one, and they will bring the trucks closer and strike more often, and they will have no notion at all that the map they are perfecting is the map of the inside of a bomb, and that perfecting it is arming it.''\par

The house came apart into work after that, quietly, the way a great house does, and I stood in the middle of it and watched people I had thought were furniture become an army. Jean-Charles came in from a room I had not known the house had, with paper still warm in his hand, and he was not smiling, which frightened me more than the drums had, because in all the weeks he had carried me out of a desert and taught me to eat food that served no purpose he had never once stopped smiling.\par

``It is worse than the ground,'' he said, and laid the warm paper down over the drum-sheets. They were pictures taken from very high up, from above the weather, the kind Paul had shown me of cities, but these were of nothing, of my nothing, of the pale empty folds of the only country I had ever called the world. And on the nothing there were things that did not belong to it. A camp, laid out in the straight and thoughtless lines the outside draws everywhere it goes. Vehicles I had no name for, squat and heavy, that Sarah named for me without being asked, in a voice gone flat: ``Vibrator trucks. That is the source. Those four are the source.'' Towers of a kind. A drilling rig. And around all of it, in a ring, smaller shapes she named more slowly still. ``Those are not surveyors. Surveyors do not travel with those. That is security. That is a perimeter. Someone has put a soldier's fence around a scientist's camp.'' And along the second ridge, the high one, the one my people had always trusted because no road reached it, more shapes yet, dug in, patient, facing inward.\par

Then she went very quiet, and put one finger on the picture, on the grid of little marked points where the trucks were set to do their sounding, the careful lattice of where-they-had-struck and where-they-would-strike-next, and she said, in a voice I had not heard her use, a voice with the cold gone out of it and something much worse in its place: ``I drew that grid.''\par

No one spoke.\par

``That is my lattice. That is the spacing I worked out, the spacing that gets you the deepest return off a body that size without wasting a season on it. That is my method, my notation, the little private way I mark a shot-point that I have never taught to more than four living men. Someone in that camp learned to survey at my knee, or at the knee of a man who did.'' She took her finger off the picture as though it had burned her. ``This is not a stranger who stumbled on the string. This is my own hand, come loose from me, and gone south without me, and knocking.''\par

\begin{room}

--- Who.

--- I will not know for certain until I have a name, and I do not give names I am not certain of, even now, even to you. But I will tell you the shape of it, because you have earned the shape. There is a part of the French state that has wanted this desert for as long as my family has worked it, and never quite got its hand round it, and has spent forty years being politely fed the crumbs I chose to drop. Martinez fed them for me. Martinez is old, and I have outlived my use to the people he truly serves, and a tool that has outlived its use is not thanked. It is copied, and then it is discarded. Somewhere in the last few years my careful crumbs stopped being enough for them, and they took the loaf. They have my grids. They have, I must assume, a great deal of what Horizon knew, because Horizon is old too, and old houses leak. And they have decided, being soldiers and not scholars, that the way to open a locked mountain is to knock on it until it answers, and they have no idea, none, that the thing they are teaching to answer is the thing I spent my whole life making certain no single hand could ever reach.

\end{room}

I have wondered since, and I wonder now, saying it, whether she told the whole truth in that room or only the part that made her our ally, because there was a version of the night in which Sarah Blackwood was not trying to save my people at all, but only to take back a machine that had been stolen from her by cruder men, and I could never afterward be sure which woman gave the orders that followed. I have decided it does not matter, and I have decided it a hundred times, and it has never once stayed decided. Perhaps both women gave the orders. She had told me herself that she had made her peace with rooms that hold two true things.\par

Paul found me at the window a little later, while she was in the next room speaking down a telephone in a language I did not own, and he kept his voice low, the fact-checker's habit still on him even now. ``We are about to cross a desert on the orders of the one person who has told us, to our faces, that she wants the thing under it for herself. You have understood that.''\par

``I have understood it.''\par

``And?''\par

``And she is the only soul on the earth who can put us there, and she wants the soldiers off my Mountain as fiercely as we do, for reasons that are bad. I have spent my life among people whose reasons were good. My own council's reasons were spotless, and they erased me with them, and they are about to let the world end without a mark on their conscience. I will take the bad reasons that point the right way, and I will watch the hand on the door the whole time, exactly as she taught me to.''\par

Paul was quiet a moment. ``That is either the wisest thing you have said all week,'' he said, ``or it is precisely how she takes everyone.''\par

``Yes,'' I said. ``It is both at once. That is what she is.''\par

What is certain is that she gave them. She turned from the pictures and she was the cold woman again, whole, and she began to spend the house the way her father had spent cities.\par

``Jean-Charles.'' He straightened. ``The parts of Horizon that are still mine, that never touched Martinez, that do not know his masters exist. Wake them. All of them. I want every route into that quarter that the camp does not know it does not control, and I want the ones it thinks it controls closed behind us as we pass. You have run the network against smugglers and against my own competitors. Run it now against soldiers who are using my maps. They will not expect the maps to fight back.''\par

``And the boy,'' Jean-Charles said, with a glance at me that had the old warmth in it and a new grief under the warmth. ``You are sending the boy.''\par

``I am not sending him. Look at him. He was going the moment the second knock drew.''\par

She was right, and it shamed me a little that a stranger had read it off me before I had finished reading it off myself. I had spent the whole of my account explaining to a machine why I could never go home, why they had erased me, why there was no place in the Mountain for a man who had pulled the cord he was told to leave. And none of it weighed anything against the pictures on the table. My people did not know. The five sat behind their walls keeping the one secret from each other, and not one of them had thought to look up, because looking up was Shield's work and Shield had been broken on the day I broke it. Someone had to carry the word in. And there was, in the whole world, exactly one living man who had stood inside those offices, and knew the shape of the danger, and could reach the Threshold, and was already, in the eyes of the council that exiled him, out in the world on their own invented errand.\par

``They made me a finder,'' I said, mostly to myself. ``When they could not agree to kill me and could not bear to keep me, they invented a mission, so that my leaving would have a lawful shape. A Veil sent out to find whether the Enemy Within was real. I always took it for a lie they told to be rid of me.'' I looked up. ``It was a lie. But it was a lawful lie. I am the one man who can walk to that Threshold and be, by their own word, on an errand they themselves decreed. I have findings to report. God forgive me, I have findings.''\par

``They will kill you,'' Jean-Charles said. He said it gently, the way you say a true thing to a friend who has already made up his mind. ``You told me yourself, the first week, what your people do to a Veil who comes back after they have erased him. There is no door in that Mountain that opens inward for you. They opened one to push you out. They will not open it to let you back in. They will open it to put you in the ground.''\par

``I know what they do,'' I said. ``I am going because of what they do. A people who will kill a man for coming home have forgotten how to be warned. Someone has to walk in past the fear, and I am the only man left alive who was born to that fear and is already standing outside it.''\par

And beneath the reasons I gave the room, where I did not let my eyes go while they watched me, there was the reason I gave no one. Va-Sheva was behind that door. She had chosen it over me, in this same country, with a box of foolish coloured sweets going soft on the table between us; she had walked back into the Mountain that unmade me because it was the only cage that left me breathing, and she was there now, among the five thousand, under a ridge full of dug-in men and a knock that was arming a bomb. I had spent a whole week telling this house I could never go home. I had not once reckoned on home being the place where the last person who had loved me was standing directly over the fuse.\par

``And I cannot get in alone,'' I said, and I turned to Paul, because the next of it was his and none of theirs. ``A Veil they have erased has no door. The Threshold does not open to knowledge; it opens to a mark, set into the hand, that the stone itself is made to know. On the night we walked out under the tree in this country, and I pricked your palm, and named you Va-Paul of an order I had invented that same afternoon, you took it for a captive's game, and I let you, because the half of me that was still sane took it for one too. But I did not invent the mark. The order I invented. The mark is old, and it is real, and I set into your hand the one Shield sign I knew that a Threshold is built to answer, because it seems I cannot make even a game without building a door into it. I told you that night it would carry you one day into the Mountain. I did not believe there would ever be a day. There is a day. It is this one. The key to my people's last gate is pricked into the hand of a man who came to Switzerland to check a fact.''\par

Paul looked down at his own hand as though it had been lent to him for the evening.\par

Then it was Paul's turn, and I watched him arrive at it, because he was the one among us whose road there was hardest. He had been hired to hold a fact to a light. He had crossed, somewhere in the week, into believing the fact, and then into liking the man who was the fact, and now he stood in a room where his employer had just confessed to being the danger and was proposing to lead the rescue, and every rule of his small honest trade told him to stay behind the glass and keep writing it down.\par

``My work is the record,'' he said slowly. ``I keep the record. That is the one thing in this house I have been sure of. If I go, I am no longer a man keeping a record. I am a man in the thing the record is about. Those are not the same man.'' He was quiet a moment. ``But a record delivered to no one is not a record. It is paper. If they reach the Mountain before this reaches anyone who can act, then everything on these reels is only the very careful history of how the world ended. \ldots{} And I find that I do not want to be the man who stayed behind the glass to protect the purity of his notes while the thing in the notes was killed.''\par

``Then stop protecting it,'' Sarah said, not unkindly. ``Bring it with you.''\par

He looked at the great machine that had turned beside us for a week, that held my whole life on its spools, and he reached over and stopped it, and he handled it like an object, which in all that week it had never once been to him. He drew the reels and boxed them. And then he took from a drawer a smaller machine, a travelling one, and weighed it in his hand, and put it in his coat, and in doing it he stopped being the man behind the glass and became the last narrator of this account, the one who would carry it out of the room and into the sand, and I have thought since that the interview did not end that night. It only stood up, and put on its coat, and walked out into the weather it had been describing.\par

Va-Elise came for us before the light. She had given me my outside name in that same house, weeks or lifetimes before, reading me a list of everything I did not know while a country I could not find slid past the window. Now she carried papers of another kind: names that were not our names, and the routes that went with them, and the small flat book of a woman who moves people across borders the way water moves through a house that is pretending it is not also a cave. She looked at me and did not smile either, and said only, ``You will be three different men before the desert. Learn them on the way.''\par

She had built it as a chain of small ordinary journeys, no single link of which was strange enough to be remembered afterward by anyone who handled it: a charter out of a private field, a change of aircraft in a place where no official hand ever opens a bag, the family's own helicopter that crossed those hills a dozen times in a week and was remarked by no one, and then, at the end of all the ordinary hops, the one stretch that no cleverness could make ordinary, the last of it on animals, into folds of rock that stood on no map because the men who drew the maps had never been let near enough to find the seams. Amastan's line would meet us at the folds, she said, or what remained of it; the old blind man himself was long dead, but a door of the kind Shield spends generations making does not die with the man who is it. It teaches a son, and the son a son, and the desert keeps the office the way the Mountain keeps its own.\par

I looked once, going out, at the glass. The long dark pane they had watched me through, the first days, when I was a specimen and not yet a man to them. I could see the room in it now, emptied, the lamp still burning over the drums, the chairs pushed back, the shape of a week of my confession still somehow hanging in the air of it. And beyond my own reflection, faintly, the pale beginning of the day over the lake. Then Jean-Charles put his hand on my shoulder, in the doorway, the way he had in the desert when I was a bundle under a cloth, and he said, ``Come, man. We do not make ceremony of leaving, in this house,'' which was a kindness, and a lie, because he was making one, and I let him, and we went out to where the machine that would carry us south was already turning its blades against the last of the dark.\par
