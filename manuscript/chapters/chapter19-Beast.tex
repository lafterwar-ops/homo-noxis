\typesetchapter{Chapter 19}{Beast}

The Hall of Cataclysm is not the Hall of Ceremony. I had lived my whole life in the Mountain and never known there was a second hall, lower, older, cut before the doctrine that hid it, and I came to it with my hands unbound, because a man cannot do what they needed me to do with his hands tied, and being unbound felt worse than the rope, because it meant they had decided to use me.\par

It lay beneath the deep reserve, past a Shield gate that Ka-Dhavar opened with a thing he wore against his own skin and a word he said to no one, into a chamber shaped like the inside of a bell and colder than any place I have stood, because the heat of the Mountain, all of it, the fire that cooked the bread and warmed the wards and drove the whole slow life of the place, was gathered and sent elsewhere here, down, into the floor, so that the floor of the Hall of Cataclysm was warm as a living thing while the air above it froze. The four surviving Keepers stood at four stations cut into the four sides, and where the fifth should have stood, at Faith's place, there was only a low stone bowl on a plinth and a fitting for an instrument that no longer had an operator, because the operator was dead under a fallen roof and her Veil was breathing in a cot somewhere with his skull broken and his ears gone.\par

We had been sealed nine days when they brought me down. Nine days of the Mountain learning to be a tomb: the wards rationed, the air turned and turned again, the wounded from the Faith passage laid in rows, and above us, muffled by the whole thickness of the Seal, the patient thud of an enemy who did not yet understand that the door they were drilling through had a worse thing behind it than any treasure. Nine days for the four surviving Keepers to arrive, one argument at a time, at the place they now stood: that the soldiers would reach the mechanism; that a mechanism reached was a mechanism taken; that a whole taken by one hand was the single thing the Beast had been made to prevent; and that the only defence left against the crime of the whole was to commit the greater crime first, on purpose, by law, tonight. They had come to it exhausted. That is the thing no one tells you about the ending of a world: that the people who bring it are tired, and cold, and have been awake too long, and would like, by the end, more than they would like to be spared, simply to have it decided.\par

``We cannot fire it,'' Ka-Leth of Balance said, ``and we cannot fail to fire it, and both of those are true, and I have been standing here for an hour unable to move for the truth of both.'' He turned his heavy face to me. ``The Beast needs five. It has always needed five. That is not custom. It is the machine. It cannot be worked by four, and the fifth office is the one that listens, and the one that listens is dead.''\par

\ornament

They told me what the Beast was by making me help build the firing of it, which is the only way anyone was ever taught it, because there is no book of it; a book could be taken. I will set down what I came to understand, in the order I came to understand it, and I will keep to what my hands did, because the rest is beyond me still.\par

Balance is the water and the fire. Far below the Hall, deeper than the reserve of the living water, there is the other reserve, the great one, the one Ka-Leth had called ballast in the hearing and would not let anyone drink, and below even that there is the heat, the deep heat the whole Mountain steals its warmth from, rock hot enough to cook stone. Balance's work, the first work, is to let the great water down onto the deep heat, in amounts it meters to the drop, and the water where it touches the heat does not boil, it flashes, it becomes steam in an instant and with a violence that has to be felt through a floor to be believed, and the steam has nowhere to go but where Balance sends it, down a throat cut for it, a hammer-blow of pressure driven into the roots of the world. That is one stroke. A drop of water, metered onto fire, become a blow that shakes a mountain. Ka-Leth stood at a bank of stone valves worn smooth by eleven thousand years of hands and he could open them a hair or a hand, drops or a river, and the difference between the two was the difference between a drill and the end.\par

He made me learn the first valve with my own hand before he would trust my word at the eye, because, he said, a man who has never felt the water fight the stone has no business timing it. He set my palm on the great worn wheel and had me crack it a hair, and the thing bucked under my hand like a live animal, the deep water wanting up and out, and it took the whole of my weight to hold it to a hair. Ka-Leth watched me shake against it. ``Now picture holding that steady, to the drop, for an hour, while the floor tries to throw you off it,'' he said, ``and you will understand why Balance is the office we give to the patient and the strong, and never once in eleven thousand years to the clever. Clever men open valves too far. They want to see what happens. You will not touch these again. But now you know what your one word is going to cost the man who obeys it.''\par

\ornament

Light is where the blow goes.\par

I had thought, being Light's own once, that I knew my office. I knew a lamp of it. Ka-Raedin took me to the Light station of that Hall and showed me the rest, and I understood that everything I had ever been taught in the Veil's Chamber, the red glass, the token-hole, the way a beam bends and spreads and comes back together at a place you can choose if you know the arithmetic of its bending, all of it, the whole of my discipline, had been the children's version of this.\par

Because a blow driven into rock does not stay a single blow. It spreads, the way any wave spreads, out through the body of the Earth in every direction, weakening as it goes. Left alone it would arrive everywhere on the far side of the world as nothing, a tremor no instrument could pick from the ordinary breathing of the ground. Light's work is to see that it is not left alone. Deep in the rock below the Hall there are surfaces, cut and placed across ages, the way I had cut the red glass into a beam, that take the spreading wave and turn it, and bend it, and above all bring it back together, so that instead of arriving everywhere as nothing it arrives at one place as everything. Apertures. Alignments. The whole patient geometry of making a spreading thing converge. And the place they had aligned it to converge, across eleven thousand years, through the whole thickness of the world and its molten heart, was the one place on the Earth exactly opposite the Mountain, the far pole of the shot, where the crust is already broken in the middle of an ocean, in a middle of a ring of fire the size of half our world and needs only a hand to help it fail, and only moves a thousand steps per century.\par

``You bent light in a chamber to teach a boy his office,'' Ka-Raedin said, and he looked at me directly, which he had not once done since I came back. ``We bend the Earth's own shaking through the Earth's own core, to a single point on the far skin of the world. It is the same art. You were taught the toy of it, and you were the best with the toy any of us had seen, which is why they feared you, and why I am about to need you.''\par

And he needed me sooner than either of us wished. The enemy's charges had been at their patient work for nine days, and the deep surfaces that Light keeps true, the mirrors of stone that bend the shot, are trued to a hair across eleven thousand years, and a hair is all it takes to throw the convergence wide. Ka-Raedin took me down a stair behind the station to where one of the great surfaces stood, a slab of something dark and polished the size of a house-wall, and showed me the marks along its edge, the alignment sigils I had been taught as a boy to read as prayer and now read as a protractor. One of them had gone out of true, shifted by the long shaking, so that the shot would have bent a whole degree wide and come home not at the far pole of the world but somewhere in the empty sea beside it, a miss the size of a country. We could not move the slab; it weighed as much as a hill. But we could move where the shot met it, by a hand's breadth, with the smaller surfaces above, the way you correct a beam by tilting the second glass and never the first. It was my art. It was my exact and only art, the thing I had been the best of my generation at and been broken for, and I did it in the cold with Ka-Raedin holding the lamp and reading me the sigils, and I bent the shot back onto the pole of the world with my two hands. When it came true a sign along the far edge of the slab caught the lamp and threw it back green, which is the Mountain's oldest way of saying yes, and my Keeper let out a breath I think he had been holding for nine days.\par

\ornament

And then there was Faith's place, empty, and the whole thing stopped there, because I had learned what Balance did and what Light did and I could see, even I could see, that neither of them was any use without the thing the dead woman had done.\par

For the blow, once bent and aimed, comes back. It goes down into the world and part of it returns, an echo, up through the rock to the Hall, and the returning echo is the whole secret of the weapon, because a single blow, however well aimed, is not enough to break the far side of the world. It takes many, one after another, each one added to the last. And they can only be added if each new blow is struck at the exact instant the echo of the blow before it comes home, so that the two agree, and push together, and build, the way a child on a rope is sent higher not by one great shove but by many small ones timed to the swing. Strike too early or too late and the new blow fights the old echo and cancels it and the whole labour of an hour is thrown away. Faith's work, the dead woman's work, was to listen to the returning echo through a stone tuned to hear it, and to know, in her body, in her trained and holy patience, the one instant to tell Balance to let the next drop fall. She was the metronome of the end of the world. And she was dead.\par

``It is called the prayer,'' Ka-Raedin said, ``because that is how it was taught, as a listening and an answering, a call into the stone and a waiting for the stone to call back, and a Faith is trained from a child to feel the phase of it the way you and I feel a held breath. There is no one left who can feel it. And without it we have four offices and a bank of valves and no way on Earth to time the strokes, and an untimed Beast is a mountain shaking itself to rubble over its own children for no purpose at all.''\par

Sarah had been silent since we came down into the cold. She spoke now, from the dark at the edge of the lamplight, where she had been standing with Paul, the two Outsiders, in the one place in the world no Outsider was ever meant to stand.\par

``Play me the echo,'' she said. ``On your stone. Let me hear one stroke and its return. I have spent forty years listening to exactly this and calling it my life's work, and I never once knew it had a name, or a hall, or a dead priestess. Play it for me.''\par

\ornament

They did not want to. I watched four Keepers, in the worst hour their office had ever known, balk at the blasphemy of letting an Outsider woman lay her ear against the listening stone of Faith, and I watched Justice overrule them, because Justice had already ruled, in the hearing, that the world's ending was lawful, and a man who has permitted the greater blasphemy cannot in conscience forbid the lesser one that might make it work.\par

So Ka-Leth let a single drop fall, the smallest stroke the machine could make, and the floor jumped once under us, and a sound went down and away into the world, and Sarah stood with her hand and her ear against the Faith stone and waited, and the echo came home, and I watched the whole of her life arrive in her face at once.\par

``That is it,'' she said, and her voice had gone somewhere I had not heard it go. ``That is the five strokes. That is the thing under the bomb. That is what I heard at Reggane at twenty-seven and have chased across forty years and two continents and a marriage and a great many crimes. It was this. It was always this. It was a mountain full of frightened priests, testing that the end of the world still answered when they knocked, and stopping, always, always, after five, before the swing could build.'' She turned her face toward the four Keepers, and there were no tears in it, which was worse. ``Your drills. Your holy little drills, every time a Keeper died and a new one had to be shown the machine still worked. Five strokes and stop. I built my whole self out of the echo of your caution. And now you need someone to do the thing your caution was invented to prevent, which is to not stop after five.'' She took her hand off the stone. ``I can hear the phase. God forgive me, I have the best ear for it left alive, because I am the only one who was ever curious about it instead of afraid of it. I can time your strokes. I can be your dead woman.''\par

\ornament

But she could not, alone, and this is the thing the Mountain had built its whole law to prevent, and the thing we did anyway, in the cold, because there was no other door left.\par

Sarah could hear the phase, the living seismic return, better than any of them; that was true. But she could not read the stone the way a Faith read it, could not tell the true return from the Mountain's own noise, from the enemy's charges still landing above us, from the structure's groaning, because a Faith spent a lifetime learning the one voice of this one Mountain and Sarah had spent hers learning the general grammar of all ground everywhere. What Sarah heard as a return might be the enemy. What she discarded as noise might be the true echo. She had the ear and not the tongue.\par

Paul had the tongue, and he had it because Ka-Elun had put it into him with her own mouth before the roof took her. In the sealed hearing, when Justice opened the Threshold and had each office depose its one forbidden thing into the outsider who could do nothing with it, the fragment Faith gave him was this: the timing itself, the sequence, the how-long-to-wait and the how-to-know-the-true-call, which had never once been written, because a book can be taken, but had lived as the liturgy, the prayer of Faith, the words a Faith is taught as a child and says over every drill of a life without ever being told that the psalm is an operating manual for the end of the world. She had recited it into Paul's turning machine plainly, an old woman handing a heavy thing across before her arms failed; and an hour later the roof came down on her, and the last living tongue that knew what the words meant was a fact-checker's. So it fell to Paul, whose whole trade was reading what a text says under what it claims to say, to hold Faith's deposed prayer against the half-knowledge of the low under-priests and turn each holy verse back into the instruction it had been before someone hid it inside a hymn. \emph{When the stone answers in the third measure, and not before, let fall.} He translated the end of the world back out of scripture into arithmetic, in the cold, by lamplight, carrying a dead woman's last confession as the one thing that could work her office now that she was gone.\par

It did not work the first time, or the third. We built the composite the way you build anything in a hurry out of parts that were never meant to fit: badly, and with blood. The first true stroke and return we tried, Sarah called the phase off the living wave and Paul called it off the liturgy and the two calls did not agree, a whole measure apart, and I had to choose between them in the half-second the machine allowed, and I chose the ear over the tongue, and told Ka-Leth to let fall, and the stroke arrived against the return instead of with it and cancelled it, and an hour of built swing bled out of the Mountain in a long sick groan. Ka-Leth looked at me with no anger at all and said only, ``That is why we stop at five,'' and we began again. The second time I chose the tongue over the ear and was wrong the other way. It was Sarah who found the fix, because Sarah had spent forty years learning to tell a true signal from noise, and that, in the end, was the missing office: not the ear, and not the tongue, but the judgement standing between them, the thing a Faith did without ever knowing she did it. ``Give me the stone and give me the words both,'' she said, ``and stop trying to choose. The words tell me which returns to listen for. The stone tells me when. You keep asking one of them to be right on its own. They are only right together.'' And after that the three of us were one instrument, an ear and a tongue and an eye, and it was, I think, the most complete violation of my people's founding law that has ever been committed, and it was beautiful, and I hated how beautiful it was, and I have never stopped hating it, and I have never stopped being proud of it, and I have made my peace, like a woman I knew, with holding the two at once.\par

And neither of them could see the whole. Sarah had the ear. Paul had the tongue. Neither could hold Balance and Light and the return and the sequence all at once, in one mind, and see how the pieces moved against each other, and that, it turned out, was the thing they needed me for: not a hand, but an eye.\par

Because I could see it. All of it. I had been Light and knew the bending; I had watched Balance and understood the strokes; I could hold Paul's translated sequence and Sarah's living ear and Ka-Raedin's geometry and Ka-Leth's valves in the one place at the one time, and see where they had to meet, and say the word that made them meet. I became, in that cold hall, the eye that saw the whole machine.\par

And I want the next thing set down with more care than anything else in this account, because my own story would like to hurry past it, and it is the one thing the Mountain got exactly right. I saw the whole. I did not hold it. Look at where the hands were. Shield kept the token against my palm, so that I could not have fired with one hand if I had gone mad on the spot. Justice kept the word before every single stroke, and gave it or withheld it. Balance kept the valves, and no drop fell but by Ka-Leth's own hand. The five kept their custody, each of his own, to the very last. The thing that eleven thousand years of walls and terror and murdered heretics had been built to prevent --- one hand that sees the whole, and holds the key, and gives the word, and turns the valve, all at once and alone --- did not come to exist that night, not even then, not even at the end of the world. What came to exist was four Keepers and two Outsiders and one heretic's eye, and no single one of us the whole of it. They had built the wall so well that even the firing of the Beast could not put the whole into a single hand. I was the eye of it. I was never, for one breath, its owner. That distinction is the only thing that has ever let me live with having been in that room.\par

``Do you understand what you are asking me to become,'' I said to Ka-Raedin, my own Keeper, the man whose office I had broken.\par

``Yes,'' he said. ``I have understood it since the roof fell on Faith. There is no one else. It has to be one mind, and it has to be a mind that knows all five offices, and there is exactly one such mind in the history of the Mountain, and we exiled it into the desert, and it has come home on the one night we cannot do without it.'' He did not look away this time. ``You were right, is the thing I have not been able to say. All those years ago. You pulled the cord because you saw that a wall of ignorance is only strong until the day it is tested, and I helped to break you for seeing it, and tonight the wall is being tested, and the only thing that can save the people behind it is the sight we punished you for having. Be the eye. God and the Beast forgive us both. Be the eye.''\par

I have wondered since what I truly felt in the moment I agreed, and the honest answer is the one I am least proud of: that under the horror there was a small, clean, terrible relief. All my life my sight had been named a sickness, the flaw that damned me, the thing that made me pull cords I was told to leave; I had been broken for seeing wholes where I was ordered to see only my one part. And here, at the end of everything, the sickness had become the last hope of five thousand souls, and the men who had punished me for it stood begging me to use it, and some low ungovernable creature in me, in the very middle of the end of the world, was glad. I set it down because I promised Paul's machine the truth, and because a man who hides the one shameful thing has told you nothing worth the keeping. I said yes to save my people. I also said yes because it felt, once and once only in my whole life, like being given leave to be the thing I am.\par

\ornament

Justice set the terms, because nothing irreversible happens in that Mountain without Justice standing over it counting.\par

Ka-Orun took his place at the Justice station, and Sa-Tavan beside him with the wet clay, and Ka-Dhavar of Shield opened the two custodies, the two keys that must turn together, his own and the token that had been Light's since before the Mountain had a name, so that even now, even here, no single hand, not even mine, could fire the thing without a second hand agreeing. And Justice laid down the law of the firing, which was the last mercy left in the machine.\par

``Every stroke is permitted separately,'' Ka-Orun said. ``Not the firing. Each stroke. Balance does not let a single drop fall until I say the word, and I do not say the word until Shield confirms the last stroke was contained, and Sa-Tavan writes each one as it is committed, so that there is a record of exactly where the point of no return was crossed and by whose word, because a thing done by law is done in the light even when the light is going out. And there is an abort. Until a stroke I will name, Shield can shut the throat and vent the pressure and we walk away from it, shaken, alive, a Mountain that came to the edge and stepped back. After that stroke there is no walking away, because after that stroke enough of the swing is built that it will complete itself through the far side of the world whether we strike again or not. I will tell you when we reach that stroke. I will tell all of you. And then I will ask, once, whether we go on. And then we will not be able to unask it.''\par

Shield's work through all of it was the work no one watches, which is the keeping of the thing from killing the wrong people first. Every stroke we sent down recoiled up; a hammer that drives a nail is also driven back into the hand, and the hand, here, was a mountain full of five thousand souls. Ka-Dhavar had spent his nine days moving the wards, ward by ward, off the lines where the recoil would run, into the deep shielded galleries, and Va-Sheva stood at the throat of the Hall with the abort under her palm and a living map in her head of where each stroke's recoil would land and whether anyone was still standing on it. Twice in the early strokes she raised her hand, and Ka-Orun stayed the word, and we waited in the ringing cold while a warden cleared a gallery that someone had crept back into, an old man after a forgotten thing, a child who had run the wrong way in the dark, and only when the line was empty did she lower her hand and let us go on ending the world with no one hurt in the small local way of it. It was the most Mountain thing I ever saw, and I have carried it since as the truest single fact about my people: that in the committing of the largest murder our kind has ever devised, they stopped, twice, and made certain no one was harmed by accident.\par

\ornament

We began with drops.\par

It is the thing no one who imagines the end of the world imagines: that it starts so small, so patiently, drop and wait, drop and wait, a old man at a bank of valves and a woman with her ear to a stone and a heretic in the middle saying \emph{now}, and \emph{now}, and \emph{not yet}, while the enemy's charges thudded down from above at their own blind rhythm and the two rhythms had nothing to do with each other, theirs trying to break in and ours trying to break the world, and the Hall shook for reasons no one could always tell apart.\par

The first strokes did nothing that I could feel beyond the jump of the floor. Sarah listened, and told me the phase, and I held it against Paul's sequence, and told Ka-Leth the instant, and Ka-Leth let the next drop fall, and Ka-Orun said the word before each one, and Sa-Tavan wrote each one down, and Shield stood ready at the throat to abort. Drop. Wait. Listen. \emph{Now.} Drop. And somewhere far below and far away the swing began, very slightly, to build.\par

I could feel it before any instrument showed it, because I was the eye and the eye is the thing that feels the whole. The returns began to come home a little stronger than the strokes that made them, which is the sign that the world had begun to help us, that the swing was catching, that each blow was arriving to a rock already moving the right way and adding to it. Sarah felt it in her stone and looked at me, and I nodded, and she said, low, ``It is building. It is actually building. I spent my life proving this was real and I never once let myself believe I would feel it,'' and there was in her voice the thing that had ruined her at twenty-seven, the terrible joy of being right, and I understood at last, watching her, the exact size of the appetite that had eaten her life, because I felt it move in myself, the awful pleasure of the whole machine answering to my one word, and I have never been so afraid of anything as I was of that pleasure, in that hour, in myself.\par

\ornament

Past the fifth stroke the drills had always stopped. We went past the fifth stroke.\par

I felt the Mountain change under us when we crossed the line no drill had ever crossed. The whole body of the place took on a low sustained note, a ringing that did not die between the strokes any more but hung, and grew, because now the returns were strong enough to keep the rock moving on their own between one blow and the next, and our strokes were only feeding a thing that had begun to swing by itself. Balance opened the valves from drops to a thread, from a thread to a measured flow, metering the water onto the fire faster now, because a swing that is building will take more, and Ka-Leth's face ran with sweat in the cold and his hands never once shook. Dust came down from the bell of the roof in a steady fall. A Shield warden was sick against the wall and went back to his post. Va-Sheva stood at Shield's throat with her hand on the abort and her eyes on Ka-Orun and did not move.\par

Once, past the twelfth stroke, the swing tried to run away from us. A return came home larger than it had any right to be, fed by something we had not put into it, and for three strokes the thing built faster than Paul's sequence said it could, the water metering out one measure and the swing taking three, an animal that had begun to eat on its own. I felt it through the whole machine as a wrongness, a horse taking the bit, and I called a hold, and Ka-Orun stayed the word, and we let three returns come home with no stroke to feed them, to watch whether the swing would fall back the way an unfed swing must, and it did not fall back the way it should have. And I understood what was feeding it. The enemy. Their charges, still landing above us at their own blind rhythm, had by pure evil chance begun to arrive in phase with our work, adding their small violence to our great one, so that the two rhythms which had nothing to do with each other had started, at the worst possible hour, to agree. The men drilling down to take the mechanism whole were, without knowing it, without ever being told, helping us to fire it. I said so aloud, and Sa-Tavan wrote it into the record in his good unhurried hand, that the enemy had hastened the very end they came to prevent, and Ka-Orun did the cold arithmetic again and moved the point of no return three strokes nearer than the old reckoning had set it, and no one in that room argued, because every one of us had felt the horse take the bit.\par

And all the while, above the sealed Hall, the Mountain we were doing it for felt every stroke and did not know what it felt. I learned afterward how the dark had gone for the five thousand: that they had felt the floor jump, over and over, in fear at first, and then, because people cannot stay afraid forever and must make some shape of a terror in order to live inside it, in a kind of dreadful worship. Someone in the deep wards had begun to count the strokes aloud, the way Faith had counted every drill in living memory, and the count had spread, ward to ward, until the whole buried city was marking each jump of the floor with a single number said together in the dark, not knowing that this time the count would not stop at five, not knowing they had become the choir of their own ending, knowing only that the stone was speaking and that their people had always, always answered the stone. Va-Sheva heard it come down faint through the Seal, the whole Mountain saying a number into the black, and I watched it reach her at her post, and I watched her decide not to let it reach her, and go on holding the abort with a level hand, because that was her office, and the office was the last floor any of us had left to stand on.\par

And the enemy, above us, chose that hour to break through into a high gallery, because of course they did, because the two rhythms had nothing to do with each other; there was a new sound, nearer, human, the sound of the outside actually arriving inside, and a runner came down white-faced to Ka-Dhavar and said the words \emph{they are in the upper Faith wards}, and I saw Jean-Charles, who had been standing at the Hall's own door with a Shield blade he had no traditional right to hold, look at Va-Sheva once, and she gave him a warden and a nod, and he went up into the Mountain to hold a stair against soldiers so that the four offices and their heretic could go on ending the world in peace. I did not see him again for a long time. I did not know, striking the world in that cold bell of a room, whether he was alive above me or dead.\par

The fighting came closer while we worked. A second runner, and then no more runners, which was worse, and then a sound down the Hall's own approach that was boots and not stone, and Ka-Dhavar sent his last two wardens up from the Hall door to meet it, so that the door of the end of the world stood guarded by nothing but an old blind arithmetic and a woman's raised hand. I learned afterward what it had cost above us. Jean-Charles held the third stair of the Faith wards for the better part of an hour, with a handful of wardens and a boy who ran messages and a barricade built of the Mountain's own furniture, against soldiers who had trained for this and had not expected a sealed civilisation to fight like one. He held it not because holding it could save anything now, for the world was already committed, but because if the enemy reached the Hall before the last stroke they might, in their greed to take the thing whole, do the one thing that could still ruin it: seize a Keeper, stay a hand, break the sequence at the final minute and leave the Beast half-fired, a wound in the world that killed the Mountain and spared the men who came for it. He did not know that was why the stair mattered. He only knew it was his to hold. And I was three hundred feet beneath him telling a tired old man when to let water fall, and neither of us could reach the other, and that is the truest shape of that whole night: every one of us alone at his own valve, doing the one thing set in front of him, trusting the dark to be full of others doing theirs.\par

\ornament

``The next stroke is the one,'' Ka-Orun said.\par

Everything stopped, except the ringing of the Mountain, which could not stop now, which hung in the air and the stone and the teeth.\par

``Hear me, because Sa-Tavan is writing it and it must be true on the tablet. The stroke I now permit is the last stroke we can call back. If Balance lets this drop fall, the swing that returns from it will be large enough to complete itself through the core of the world without our help. We will have committed it. The far side will break whether we strike again or not, whether we live or die in the next hour or not, whether the enemy takes this Hall in the next minute or not. Everything after this drop happens on the far skin of the world by itself, and cannot be stopped by any hand, mine or theirs or God's.'' He looked, not at me, but at the four offices, one by one, the living ones, and last and longest at the empty place where Faith should have stood. ``I will ask once. We are permitted this. The law is met; the whole is threatened with capture; there is no lesser answer that restores the secret. But permitted is not commanded. I am Justice, and I can find a thing lawful and still stay my own hand. So I ask the five of us, who are four, and the three of the outside who have become our fifth: do we let it fall.''\par

I have been asked, since, in the dark, by the only people left who could ask it, whether there was a debate. Whether anyone argued. Whether some voice cried out to stop, to find another way, to trust that the soldiers might not, after all, reach the mechanism, that the world might be spared by luck what it could not be spared by wisdom.\par

There was no debate. That is the thing I most need to set down and the thing I least know how to make bearable. We had done all our debating in a warm room and a sealed hall and a lifetime each of coming to this. There was nothing left to say that had not been said. Ka-Leth of Balance said, ``Let it fall.'' Ka-Raedin of Light said, ``Let it fall.'' Ka-Dhavar of Shield, who would have to live in the sealed dark with all of us afterward and answer for it, said, ``Let it fall.'' Va-Sheva took her hand off the abort, which was its own vote, and folded it into her other hand. Sarah, who had chased the echo of this across her whole ruined life, said nothing at all, and looked at me, and I understood that she was leaving the last of it to the one of us whose Mountain it truly was. And Paul, the witness, the honest instrument, held his little machine up a fraction, so that it would catch the words clearly, and that too was a kind of vote, the vote of a man who has decided that the least he can do, if a thing must be done, is make certain it is remembered exactly.\par

And I, who had been made the eye that saw the whole and owned no part of it, gave the last of the answers Justice had asked for --- not a hand firing the Beast, for no single hand ever did that, but the one among us whose Mountain it truly was, saying at last the word the seeing had brought all our hands to.\par

``Let it fall,'' I said.\par

Ka-Orun said the word. Ka-Leth opened the valve. The drop fell.\par

I have tried many times to say what the last stroke was like and I have not managed it and I will not manage it now. The floor did not jump. The floor \emph{left}. For a moment that had no length the whole Hall of Cataclysm was in the air, all of us, the lamps, the wet tablet, the cold, and then the world caught us again, and the sound was not a sound, it was the absence of every other sound, a silence so total and so vast that I understood the strokes before it had been loud only so that this could be heard through them, and into that silence the Mountain poured its whole gathered heat and water and patience in one long exhalation down into the roots of the Earth, and the swing we had built let go, and went down through the core of the world, and there was nothing any hand could do any more, ours or theirs, forever.\par

For a while after the last stroke no one in the Hall moved or spoke. The ringing that had hung in the stone through the whole firing was gone, spent, sent down into the world, and its absence was the loudest thing I have ever failed to hear. The lamps had guttered in the great exhalation, and someone trimmed them, slowly, and the small ordinary sound of a wick being trued was almost past bearing in that silence, because it was the sound of a person doing a small careful living thing in the first minute of the end of everything. Ka-Orun set down the last of the reckoning. Sa-Tavan pressed the final mark into the wet clay and laid the stylus beside it, square, the way you set down a tool you know you will not need again. And I stood in the middle of the four offices whose every work I had held in one sight at once, the eye that had been made to see the whole and to own no part of it, and felt the seeing drain out of me and leave me a single tired man in a cold room; and I have never been so grateful to be small again, nor so certain that I would never be entirely small again, because a man who has once seen the whole carries the shape of it in him after, the way a bell keeps the shape of the note long after the note has gone.\par

Then we waited, and this is the part the Mountain's own children were never told, because the Mountain's own children were never meant to be alive to wait it: that after you fire the Beast, nothing happens, for a long time, because the far side of the world is very far away, even straight through, and the thing we had sent had to cross the whole thickness of a planet and its molten heart before it arrived, and rebuilt itself, and did what it had been aimed across eleven thousand years to do.\par

Sarah stood at the Faith stone with her hand against it and did not take it away, and she was the one who would know first, because she was the ear, and the rest of us watched her, four Keepers and two Veils and a witness and a heretic, watched an old Outsider woman with her palm on a stone, waiting for the far side of the world to answer.\par

It is a strange thing to have fired the end of the world and then to stand in a cold room and wait to learn whether it took. We had perhaps an hour, Sarah said, maybe less, for the thing to cross the core and rebuild itself and arrive, and there was nothing to do in the hour but breathe, and no one could think of anything the right size to say, so mostly no one said anything at all. Ka-Leth closed his valves one by one, slowly, the way you put a great animal to sleep, and then sat down on the floor of the Hall of Cataclysm, an old man at the end of his office, and put his face into his two hands, not weeping, only resting the whole weight of his head in his palms as though the neck could no longer be asked to hold it. Ka-Raedin went and stood in the empty place where Faith should have been, which no Keeper of Light had ever done, because someone should stand in Faith's place at the ending and there was no one else to do it. Sarah did not move from the stone. And Paul was beside me in the dark, and he had started the little machine again, quietly, and I asked him why, when there was nothing to hear. ``There is always something to hear,'' he said. ``And if anyone is ever going to know what an hour like this was, they will need to know that mostly it was quiet, and that a tired old man sat down on the floor. That is the true thing. Not the fire. The old man sitting down.'' I have thought since that he was the only one of us, in that whole hour, still doing his office perfectly.\par

Once in that hour Sarah spoke to me, without turning from the stone. ``You felt it,'' she said. ``When it was building. When it answered your word. You felt what I felt at the bomb, at twenty-seven.'' I said that I had. ``Now you know,'' she said. ``Now you know it was never wickedness in me. It was the best feeling there is: the whole of a great thing moving because you understood it and it did not. That is the thing your Mountain should have taught, instead of teaching fear. That the reaching for the whole feels exactly like being alive; and that is why no wall of terror was ever going to hold it; and why, in the end, they were right to build a Beast instead of a wall, because you cannot frighten a person out of the one pleasure that feels to them like the meaning of their life. You can only make certain that the day a hand closes on the whole is the day the whole is taken away from everyone.'' She took her palm off the stone a moment, and laid it back. ``I have wanted, before I died, to say that to one living person who would understand it. I did not think it would turn out to be a boy from the inside of the very thing I spent my life trying to steal.''\par

It came as a line. Faith's instrument, the one with no operator, drew it by itself off the returning wave, a single long stroke climbing the drum higher than any mark any living Keeper had ever seen or been told of, a stroke that did not come from below us or around us but from the exact far pole of the shot, from the place on the Earth precisely opposite the Mountain, arriving home after its long crossing to tell us that it had worked, that the far crust had broken along its ring of fire, that the thing we aimed had struck the roots of the Earth where the Earth was already weakest and set the whole slow catastrophe of a made winter walking across the world above us.\par

``It is done,'' Sarah said, and took her hand off the stone at last, and looked at it, her own hand, as though it belonged to someone she had spent her life hunting and had, in the end, caught, and become. ``It is done. We have struck the far side of the world.''\par

And then, a long moment after, we felt our own shot come all the way home, up through the floor of the Hall, arriving not as a blow but as a slow swell, the whole ground lifting under us the way the deck of a ship lifts on a sea-swell, and settling, once. I understood that we were feeling the Earth itself ring, the entire planet struck like the bell it is and answering in a note so low and so long that no ear could hold it, only a body, only the soles of the feet and the water in the eye and the marrow of the bone. Somewhere on the far skin of the world, at the one point exactly opposite us, the note had already become fire, and wave, and a darkness climbing into the sky that would not come down for a year. Here, at the struck pole, it was only this: a great slow swell in the stone, and the sense of standing upon something that had been made, this once, to move on purpose.\par

The Mountain moved beneath us, once, settling into the new shape of a planet that had just been rung like the bell it is, and then was still. And Paul, whose machine had heard all of it, reached over and, for the second time in my knowing of him, stopped it, because there was nothing left, that hour, worth the reel; and in the silence after the machine there was no sound at all from the world outside, no charge, no drill, no soldier, no sky, nothing, because the outside had, a little while ago, without knowing it, without ever being told, begun to end.\par
