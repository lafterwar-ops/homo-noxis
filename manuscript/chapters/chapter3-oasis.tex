\typesetchapter{Chapter 3}{Oasis}

\begin{room}

--- The nineteenth of November, nine o'clock; the second of our interviews. You look as though you did not sleep.

--- Not very well if at all. They sat me before a moving light yesterday, three half days of it, and I have not stopped seeing it since.

--- Ah. The instruction. Yesterday was \textit{Life On Earth}, I am told: the whole ladder of it, from the first speck to your own hand. This afternoon they turn to the heavens. I am given the shape of the week, you understand, not the sessions themselves; I do not sit in that room. But you may speak of yesterday freely, if you wish, before we begin.

--- I wish. It began with time. A man's voice, in your tongue, with my own words set beneath it in writing; someone had troubled to do that. He said \textit{these immense periods of time baffle the imagination}. You don't say. My people count the years of the world in their thousands and think themselves ancient. He counts them in their millions, and had the rock to prove it. He took me down into a great red canyon, wall standing over wall, and taught that each layer beneath was older than the one above it; that a rock lying low is a rock lying old; and that the floor of the thing was older than the whole of my scripture, and older again than that. I did not know a rock could be young or old. A rock is just a rock. And in those walls there were beasts, Paul: creatures caught in the stone where they had died an age ago, turned to stone themselves, held in the rock as a fly is held in sap. The deeper he went, the further back he was asking; the descent itself was the question. Millions of years. It is hard to picture, even for me; and I had thought myself a hard man to astonish.

--- Geology. My own first study.

--- You said you read geology. Is it a book? I should like to read it. And I should like to hold the instrument: the microscope, in my own hands. I should like to see this DNA he spoke of with my own eyes, and not take it on a voice's word. 

--- That is a translation mistake of mine. We use this word, \textit{to read}, only to pompously mean study. As it turns out, we have quite a powerful one downstairs; but whether you would be granted access to it is not mine to say, nor the tutor's: that is a door that opens from a good deal higher up than either of us. I will ask all the same, and press the case. I should like you to have it, and I will do my best. The book you shall have, though slowly: it is written in no tongue you read, and so few living men can carry it into yours that the translating alone is the work of months, not days; you are right that these things take time. What I may not do is teach you the world myself. I am here only to take down the one you came from.

--- There it is again: the wall you will not walk past. Every house I have been kept in has had one. But the rock was not what kept me from sleep. It was that your kind is permitted to ask. Where I was raised there is scripture, and scripture is the answer to a question, not the start of one. That voice did not speak in truths. He spoke in questions, and in partial answers, the best found so far, and called the rest a mystery still to be pierced. That spoke to me. I was cut from Light, at fifteen, for saying that what is hidden still has shape, and if it has shape it may be found. And here is a whole people who have built their temples out of precisely that sin.

--- We would not call it a sin.

--- No. You would call it a virtue, and give a child a prize for it. And the light itself, the moving picture. I have thought of little else. It is light, Paul. Only light, placed and turned by a hand I was not shown. My whole office rests on one lesson: a lamp set in the wrong place will make a man swear to a thing that is not there. You have built a lamp that can set any thing before a man, in the dark, and he believes it because he wishes to. We would have named it the most dangerous object ever made and given it to Shield to bury. And I sat in the dark and could not look away. Nor could I stop asking the two questions my whole life trained into me: who chose what I was shown, and what did they leave in the dark.

--- \ldots{}

--- And you will have been watching me watch it; I assumed it all day. I was raised under the finest eyes my world ever made, and I can feel a trained watcher through a wall. So tell me: who stood behind the glass?

--- No one. That I know of. I was not there; for I spend my afternoons replaying the tape, taking notes and doing research. I am kept from your instruction as you are kept from every account but your own, for that is the whole method of this house. Only the tutor sat with you, and he attends the lesson, not the man. You were alone with it, though I know how little you will believe it. And the rest of what you said, of the lamp: you are not wrong, and I find I would rather you were. The instrument you sat before yesterday, we have had the better part of two generations; and in that time it has been turned to make whole nations swear to things that were never in the room: to love a war before a single reason was given for it, to hate a people before one of them was met, to want a thing they had no want of an hour before. A hand chooses the frame; a hand chooses what is left in the dark; and the man in the chair believes it the more warmly for wishing to. You named in one evening the danger it took my world fifty years to half admit. So yes: you are not wrong. I would rather you were, for I have no cure to hand you, and this house, and my whole world with it, runs on the lamp.

--- \ldots{} A room with no eye in it, and a man, you Paul, who grants the point when he might have kept his silence. That is the most honest thing you have said to me, and I hardly know what to do with it. I had forgotten there were such rooms, and such men. Then let me test the honesty while it lasts: that man above your fire, with the face of one who was obeyed, him at least you may tell me of. Or is he walled away with the rest, on the far side of the tutor's line?

--- That one I may answer, and gladly. He is Sir Edward Blackwood, this time it is a rank. He built the house you are sitting in, and a good deal more than the house. His business ran the whole way round the world: oil, metals, the desert, the things men went south and east for in his day. Toward the end he believed there was something out there worth more than all of it. He never found it, but his daughter Sarah did. And, speaking of around the world: I should not want to spoil your afternoon, which I am told is to be the heavens, and the shape of the earth among them; but I find I cannot leave the phrase unasked. Your people know the earth for a sphere. Do they not?

--- Curious, that you should ask me that very question, of all the questions you might ask. Yes, we know that it is a sphere. And also we do not. It is complicated.

--- That is not an answer.

--- And now, you sound like me. No. It is the true shape of one. Ask me again later this week, in its proper place, and I will give you the long form. This morning it is only complicated.

--- Then I shall keep it for later. But first, the small errand I was sent with. You kept your hours with the tutor yesterday, and then sent the physician away. The exercise. You would not do it.

--- Paul. I mean you no disrespect; you have been courteous, and courtesy is not nothing. But you should understand what you are asking, and of whom. I am not a nobody. I sat among the ten who mattered most in a world ten thousand years old. Men knelt when I passed them. Lives were kept, or were not, upon my word. And you would set me in coloured cloth to chase a ball across a lawn, for no stake but the running, while grown men clap, and call it improvement. I did not come this far, through all that has been taken from me, to be entertained by silly games.

--- It is a game, and it is not. It is complicated, to borrow your word. Let me put the thing in a way you will take. I would not ask you to caper for anyone's amusement, and I have no say over the regimen in any case; the physician holds it, and I am only his messenger. But hear it plainly: it is not play, and it is not for pleasure. The body is an instrument, and yours has come to us badly out of tune, underfed, overwalked, wrongly mended. You are to be brought to full working order. Function. Not fun. And where he would begin is swimming; I imagine there was little call for it where you were raised. A pool beneath the east wing: indoors, private, no eye upon it but the physician's, and he reads numbers, not men. It asks everything of the body and shows nothing to the world. I thought it might suit a man who has had his fill of being watched.

--- The swimming I will try; you may tell the physician he has won that much from me, and I will keep to his pool. 

--- \ldots{}

--- No. — No: yes. I will do the swimming. The man whose voice it was yesterday did more than cross the top of the water; he went down under it, in a red tunic, and a large yellow bottle behind his back, and moved among the creatures there as though he belonged with them, and named each one for me. You have a pond out in your garden; I have watched it all the morning from the window. Train me to swim, and then train me to dive, and give me the small glass I asked you for at the start, the microscope, and I can go down into that pond and learn for myself whether the man on the light told me the truth or only a good story. I have taken a great deal on a voice's word yesterday. I should like to check one thing of it with my own eyes.

--- That is no small thing you are asking, and it is more than the physician had in mind; but I will try for it. A man who will not cross a lawn in the sun, and in the same breath asks to be taught to go down under the water and check the truth of a thing with his own eyes, is at least a consistent one. Now: yesterday you left me at the Mirror, the wall where all of it falls. What did Light do with you the next morning?

--- It gave me a beam of my own to learn, and then would not let me learn it. I had been granted permission to go outside, for the second time in my life.

\end{room}

The first had been at my blessing, when I was too new to hold it, and the Mountain had folded the sky away again before I could learn it was ever mine to lose. The permission came to me on a strip of pale bark, folded twice and sealed with green wax. The seal was Balance green: the cup and the sealed jar pressed deep into the wax, crossed by a narrow black witness line and the small hard stamp of Shield. I broke it with my thumb. The old cut pulled under the bandage.\par

The script was plain, meant to be read quickly and nothing more.\par

\begin{scripture}
Sa-Kailan, Candidate of Light, is granted one controlled prayer access before the Oasis at the Third Measure. Duration: one silence before and one silence after the water prayer. Route: east lower passage, cedar gate, second threshold, shaded descent. Measured by Balance. Witnessed by Justice. Held by Shield.\par
\end{scripture}

Below, in a different hand, someone had added: 

\begin{scripture}
Do not be late. Prayer does not lengthen because boys do.
\end{scripture}

There was no signature. None was needed. It could have come from any number of hands on the Mountain. Plenty of them were that cruel, and knew the prayer schedule that well.\par

I read the strip once for permission, then again for hierarchy. Va-Raedin had taught me to read the second thing in a document, the order of the offices, more carefully than the first. Balance had measured. Justice had witnessed. Shield held. Faith would conduct the prayer. Light had no authority out there except through me, as a body to be admitted, washed, watched, counted, and returned. \par

Outside. Guarded, and barely outside at all, if my father's corrections were to be believed. But outside enough for sky and the Oasis. Outside enough for the Mountain's shadow to fall across my body out in the open air.\par

I dressed more carefully than the rite required. I tied the bandage on my thumb, untied it, and tied it again so the linen sat cleanly over the cut. 

My mother watched from the wall recess where she had been folding cloth.\par

``You will keep your eyes lowered through the water prayer.''\par

``I know.''\par

``You will not ask Shield why a path is closed.''\par

``I know.''\par

My father looked up from the tablet he had been marking. ``Outside is not a reward.''\par

``No.''\par

``It is not a private revelation.''\par

``No.''\par

``It is a place where the Mountain becomes harder to protect.''\par

I folded the permission bark and placed it inside my robe. ``Yes.''\par

He studied me for a moment, perhaps deciding whether the word had entered or merely passed through. ``Stand in the shadow when instructed,'' he said. ``Do not look long at the desert. It teaches appetite.''\par

I left before I answered. \par

The familiar passages to the cedar gate felt strange that morning, because I was walking them in order, outward, and I could feel the difference in my own feet. Checkpoint, gate, count, threshold, descent: each had stood there yesterday, and yesterday I had walked past it blind. Today I passed through each one wondering what it was for.\par

At the first checkpoint a Justice clerk checked the green wax against a slate. He examined the time mark, my name, my bandaged thumb, and the absence of anything hanging from my belt.\par

``Permission strip.''\par

I gave it to him. He opened it, read it though he must already have known what it said, and pressed a clay counter into my palm.\par

``Return this counter at the second threshold on re-entry. If you lose it, the route remains open in the record, and Shield searches until it is closed.''\par

``I will not lose it.''\par

``That is the usual intention.''\par

The counter was small, rough, and stamped with a number. I placed it in the inner fold of my robe, away from the Light token, as if the two objects might quarrel if they touched.\par

At the cedar gate, Faith washed my hands and my mouth; the route out was not Faith's to touch. No one passed outward for prayer without being washed into the proper condition. Two attendants stood beside a shallow basin. Every leaving body was given water, enough for rite and no more.\par

There were seven of us in the access group: myself, three older women from the western family chambers --- one of them leading a boy with a single cloudy eye --- and two men who looked too tired to be anything but workers granted prayer because grief had entered their household. One of the men carried no offering, which meant his request had been accepted under sorrow. I was counting the group the way a boy counts, for company. Shield was counting it the way Shield counts, for return.\par

The Faith attendant poured water over my hands. It was colder than I expected. I lowered my eyes before the attendant could see where they had gone.\par

``Speak only what is appointed before the Oasis,'' she said.\par

``I will.''\par

``Take nothing from the ground.''\par

``I will not.''\par

``If the wind rises, turn with Shield, not with fear.''\par

I looked up. She held my eyes. ``Fear often faces the wrong direction,'' she said.\par

The boy with the cloudy eye began to cry when the water touched his mouth. His aunt bent close and whispered too softly for me to hear. The two grieving men kept silent.\par

\ornament

Beyond the cedar gate waited Shield. Behind the wood were black iron bands and a locking bar thick enough to stop a cart. Two wardens stood before it. A third watched from a side niche, visible only because I had begun, that week, to notice where watching could be hidden.\par

The senior warden read our marks one by one. ``Prayer access. The Measure. Seven bodies out. Seven bodies due.'' He looked at me last. ``Candidate of Light.''\par

``Yes.''\par

``Eyes where Faith places them. Feet where Balance marks them. If Shield speaks, you obey before understanding.''\par

``I understand.''\par

``No,'' the warden said. ``You agree. Understanding is not required.''\par

\textit{Understanding is not required.} At fifteen it sounded like an insult to my intelligence.\par

The iron bar was lifted by two hands and a lever set into the wall. A narrow bell sounded somewhere beyond the gate, answered by another farther down the stone. The Mountain informed itself that it had opened. We passed through in single file.\par

The descent beyond the cedar gate was cut between natural rock and worked stone. At first it felt like any lower passage: dim, damp, controlled, with lamps set in protective recesses and the smell of water in the cracks. Then the lamps stopped.\par

Light lay on the floor in a pale wedge. No lamp-light. No measured reflection. No slit calculated by Light. It came from somewhere ahead, full of dust and height, careless in its angle, making each stair deeper and then shallower as I moved. \par

``Eyes down until the mark,'' the Shield warden said.\par

We obeyed. I lowered my gaze to the steps. The outside light was brighter, and undisciplined, and it took some minutes for my eyes to adapt. My training recoiled from it, and my body wanted it anyway.\par

At the last turn, the Mountain opened. The sky struck me first as sheer height, and after that as blue. The Mountain had ceilings; even its highest halls ended somewhere, and had taught my eye to expect an end. The open sky had none. I kept my eyes low, and failed, and above the dark shoulder of the Mountain the open brightness went on and on, with no vault to end it and no hand to have placed it.\par

Faith led us to the marked prayer stones. Only there were we permitted to look.\par

The Oasis lay under the Mountain's shadow, and for the space of a breath, before my training woke, it was only beautiful --- green, alive, breathing, in a world I had been raised to believe was stone to its heart. The Genesis gravure had shown me a spring inside a ring of trees, and I had knelt to that carving my whole life. The living place was quieter than I had expected from the carving, and far cleverer than the carving had ever let on, though it took me a while to see how much.\par

For nothing in it grew where it pleased. The palms leaned inward, every crown tilted to spend its shade on the water and waste none of it on the sand. Reeds kept to the slow reaches, so where they stood the water idled, and where they thinned it ran; I could read the speed of the current off the plants that were willing to stand in it. Small white flowers held the cracks that would otherwise have opened and let the hill crumble. Against the warm rock the hives were stacked in clay, and a keeper moved among them in a slow haze of smoke. And along the Mountain's foot, where the night's chill still slept in the stone, the hardiest plants wore a fur of dew --- I watched one bead of it gather on a leaf, and slide, and fall \emph{inward}, into a seam in the rock, so that even the morning's water was caught and swallowed back into the hill before the sun could take it. The Oasis was drinking through its own wall.\par

The Oasis was an engine, one that had fed a hidden people for longer than my scripture dared to count, and had kept the same slow patient rhythm the whole time. It was holy as well as clever; I felt that under everything, the way you feel a held breath in a crowded room before you have found the face that is holding it. Every leaf, every shell, every animal in that hollow had a use and wore the face of a living thing, and the work of staying alive there had been dressed up as a garden and given to people to kneel before.\par

\begin{room}

--- How large was it, in the shadow of the Mountain?

--- It is hard to say, I do not know how to count it.

--- The silly games pitch. Bigger or smaller?

--- Hard to say, because it was circular and irregular. Five, six, maybe ten of those.

--- Circular. How many steps from one end to the opposite end, going through the middle.

--- Maybe six hundred, or a thousand.

\end{room}

Beyond it all the desert expanded, heat moving over the ground in pale shimmers, and outside the Mountain's shadow the light came down hard enough to kill anything caught in it. The Oasis was hidden from every eye. The Mountain rose behind it in a great dark crescent, wrapping the hollow from the north and holding it in shade. In front, to the south, a second and far smaller height stood out of the sand --- a third the Mountain's size and no more, but set so close that the Oasis lay caught between the two of them like a seed between a thumb and a finger. What the Mountain and that near rock left open, a farther ridge away to the south-east all but closed. And the green ran on past the point where the prayer-party was let to see it. Beyond the spring and its shade the cultivation climbed, terrace above terrace along the Mountain's cooler flank: palms, then lower trees, then the low crops in their shade, more of it by far than the holy hollow held, folded into the rock's shadow so that a man out on the sand would swear the whole hill was barren stone. To reach the high terraces the water had to be sent back up against the pull of the ground, and that took a machine: a wheel of clay pots turning on a shaft that two goats walked in a slow circle, each pot filling low and spilling high, carrying the spring up the flank one cup at a time. Light had been made to protect it, or the Mountain had grown so that Faith could say God had set it there and Light would have had trouble disagreeing.\par

The old women knelt. I knelt with them. The Faith attendant unrolled a strip of pale bark and held it with both hands before the water. This was the second book of the Covenant: The Book of Blood.\par

\begin{scripture}
And after the fifth day, God spoke unto Adam and unto Eve,
\end{scripture}

--- Adam and Eve?

--- Paul, please do not interrupt me when I recite scripture. God would not approve.

--- Of course. My apologies.
\end{room}

\begin{scripture}
And after the fifth day, God spoke unto Adam and unto Eve, and unto the five children whom He had set in the Oasis beneath the shadow of the Mountain; and God said, Let there be Keepers among you, that the Mountain be not without order, nor the Oasis without care, nor the people without peace. And God called the woman of water, and said unto her, Keep thou the Balance, and gather the fruits, and count the waters, and keep the resources of survival; for what is wasted in peace shall be missing in hunger. And God called the woman of holy speech, and said unto her, Keep thou the Faith, and teach My Gospel unto the children, and keep prayer, breath, rhythm, and memory beneath the Mountain; for I shall provide.
\end{scripture}

The Balance workers moved in the distance as the words were spoken. One woman checked the level in a narrow cut along the main channel. A boy carried wet leaves toward a shaded rack. On the high terraces the barley stood pale in the sun, and a line of women worked along it with short hooks. A clerk marked something on a hanging board and moved a shell counter from one hook to another. \textit{Count the waters,} the scripture said. And there they were, counting. They counted it because there was little enough to count: no rain fell here worth the name, and the spring drew on a buried, ancient water that gave a little less each year and had none coming to replace it, so anything they spent was gone for good. That morning the words pointed at the work being done ten paces off, and the work obeyed them.\par

\begin{scripture}
And God called the man of strength, and said unto him, Keep thou the Shield, and stand before the beasts of the Earth, and before the threshold of men; suffer not the children to be devoured, nor danger to enter unnamed.
And God called the woman of brightness, and said unto her, Keep thou the Light, and illuminate the Mountain, and teach the eye where to stand, that the people stumble not in darkness.
And God called the man of peace, and said unto him, Keep thou Justice, and stand between brother and brother, and keep the record of sequence, that blood answer not blood without measure.
And the Keepers received their charges, and each kept what had been given, and the people multiplied in the Oasis.
\end{scripture}

Out here the five charges were tools laid out on stone. Balance kept the water moving. Faith transmitted the reading. Shield watched the line where shade became exposure. Light revealed enough to move without stumbling. Justice stayed out of sight, but the clay counter inside my robe said Justice had already entered the route, and had a number for me.\par

\begin{scripture}
And the Keepers had sons and daughters, and their children had children after them; and the Oasis was filled with voices, and the Mountain gave shadow, and the waters were counted, and the Gospel was taught, and the beasts were driven back, and the lamps were lit, and peace was kept among the people.
And the Keeper of Shield had two sons; and the elder was great in strength, and broad in the shoulder, and mighty before the beasts; and the younger was small in his body, and cunning in his heart.
And the younger looked upon his brother, and saw that the people loved his strength, and envy entered him as a serpent entereth the grass.
\end{scripture}

I had heard the beginning many times. Children loved the elder brother because he was easy to love: strong, brave, broad before the beasts. They loved the younger too, until they learned better. Smallness and cunning had sounded like virtues to me, back when I had neither size nor strength. But now, kneeling under the open sky, the younger brother sounded like a warning, and it had been set too close to my own ribs. I was small. I was clever. I had already learned to do the forbidden thing only when unwatched.\par

\begin{scripture}
And the younger spoke softly unto his brother, and smiled with his mouth while murder grew within him; and he rose against his brother in secret, and slew him where no beast had entered.
And the first blood of man cried from the dust.
And fear came upon the people, for Shield had defended the Oasis from the beasts without, but had not seen the beast within.
And the younger fled from the Oasis, and took with him the wife of his brother, a daughter of Faith, and went out from the shadow of the Mountain into the open land.
\end{scripture}

\textit{The first blood of man cried from the dust.} I looked toward Shield before I meant to. The perimeter wardens held their places. They were watching, and had arranged themselves to do it. Shield had failed once in scripture, watching the beasts outside while the murder happened within, and every warden since had inherited the shame of that single line.\par

\begin{scripture}
And God saw that blood had entered the world of men, and that holy speech had been carried away by the murderer; 
and God cursed them, saying, Thou shalt wander under the eye of Heaven, and the Earth shall not hide thee.
Thy children shall thirst for what they cannot keep, and conquer what they cannot love.
Thou shalt remember the Mountain, but thou shalt not find it; and thou shalt hear the Gospel, but twist it into hunger.
And from the murderer and the stolen daughter came the Outsiders.
\end{scripture}

The boy with the cloudy eye stopped crying. His aunt held him so tightly that her knuckles whitened. \textit{The Outsiders.} Kin deformed by blood, if the reading was true. Descendants of murder and stolen Faith, children of a hunger that remembered the Mountain without finding it.\par

I believed enough. I believed that brothers could envy brothers. I believed blood could change a people's story. I believed holy words could be carried away and broken by those who needed them to excuse a desire. The Book of Blood worked on me because it sounded possible, and I was already afraid enough for it to sound possible.\par

\begin{room}

--- There. It is finished, and you may have your question now: the one you have held since Adam and Eve.

--- You have those two names. A garden; a first man; a first woman; a brother who kills his brother in a field. We have that book, or one very near it. I did not think to meet it coming the other way, out of a mountain the world has never heard of.

\begin{scripture}
And the Outsiders multiplied in the open land, and their kings became many, and their cruelty became without number; and they remembered the Mountain in dreams, and hated the shade they could not find. And they gathered armies, and forged blades, and raised engines of war, and came with banners and drums and false prayers upon their tongues.
\end{scripture}

--- false prayers upon their tongues, Paul.

--- In our gospel, God forbids Eve from eating the fruit from the tree of knowledge. She does, and God is angry.

--- You said you would not teach me the world. And here you are, handing me your own Genesis. Are women not allowed instruction in your world?

--- They are, and they are not. It is complicated.

--- Do you know who wrote your gospel, and when?

--- The priests of Babylon, perhaps twenty-five to forty centuries back, out of older stories still. But we are not sure, it is a very debated subject, to this day. Some naturally argue that it was written from their god, or their prophet, or their kind.

--- Four thousand years, and it is complicated. Listen to yourself. 

--- Listen to myself, yes. I told you I am not sure who wrote it, nor when, and that learned men have quarrelled over it for centuries; I said it to your face, and I shall say it again tomorrow, and keep every finger. That is the difference you step over. Our two books may be of an age, but I may stand in the doubt and speak from it, and you were cut from Light at fifteen for reaching less far than Eve did.

--- We are two peoples telling one quarrel, each from his own end of it. Mine follows the murderer out of the shade, and the woman he took with him, and what became of the two of them in the open land.

--- The Outsiders.

--- The Outsiders. Cursed to wander under the eye of Heaven; to thirst for what they cannot keep; to conquer what they cannot love; to remember the Mountain and never find it. I learned it at five. I knelt and heard it read at fifteen, under the open sky, the morning I am giving you now.

--- And you have just recited it to me.

--- I have.

--- \ldots{}

--- You are wondering whether I know to whom I am speaking. I know exactly, Paul. You are of the open land. By the book I have set before you, you are the murderer's child, and I have been reading you your own beginning. I do not tell you that I believe every word of it. I tell you that in all the years since, I have not found the line in it that was wrong about your people. You remembered the Mountain. And, against the curse, you found it. I am the sitting proof.

--- That is a large accusation to lay on a man across his own fire. We are teaching you your beginning, and you are trying to teach us ours: the tutor sets you before the first cell and the long climb up to your own hand, and you kneel in the sand and hand me a garden, a murder, and a cursed line walking out into the country I was born in. Two beginnings, traded across one fire. I am a man of science and I will not argue a man's scripture while he recites it from memory. Let us continue.

\end{room}

The attendant lowered the bark. For a while no one moved. The silence after scripture was meant to be empty. This one had too much in it: the whole hollow at once, the kneeling women and the boy with the ruined eye, and under all of it the old story of a brother who smiled while murder grew behind his mouth.\par

Then the practical world returned. A child sprinkled water over the snail trays with the seriousness of a priest; a woman scraped fibre from soaked stalks and laid it to dry; and the bees worked the warm rock. Every living thing in that hollow was quietly being turned into something that would keep a person alive through a lean year, and nothing at all was let go to waste --- not even the water, which left its salt behind as it dried on the channel pans, for Balance to lift before it soured the beds and carry to the crocks and the tables where it kept the winter's food.\par

Faith made the refusal bearable. The attendants kept us in rhythm --- stand, silence, kneel, touch no channel, face the spring, lower the eyes, answer --- and the words synchronised the work around them.\par

\begin{room}

--- The singing that set the time of the hands. How old is it? Who made it?

--- No one made it. I asked, once, as a boy, and the answer was that it had always been so; and that is the answer to every such question inside the Mountain. The song that timed the hands in the Oasis had timed them for as long as there had been hands to time. Nothing there was new. Nothing there was suffered to become new. I was allowed to stand after the first silence, and to step to the third marker, and look. The spring and the channel stayed closed to me. I stepped to the marker.\par

Then I saw Va-Sheva of Shield.\par

She stood near the southern perimeter beyond the herb beds, where the Oasis began to turn back toward bare rock. Three Shield wardens were placed around her, though none had announced command, and none needed to. She wore dark Shield leather over her robe, a cloth wrapped round her throat against sun and grit, hair bound high, a short blade at her hip and a longer one across her back.\par

She was looking away from me, toward the outer path. She remained as beautiful --- that was the trouble.\par

One of the wardens spoke to her. She kept her eyes on the path, and lifted two fingers. The warden moved left. Another shifted near the rocks. The perimeter changed shape around a thought. Then she saw me. Her expression altered by almost nothing --- recognition, surprise perhaps, something close to amusement contained before it could become warmth --- and she crossed toward me while the Faith attendant moved away to help the old women.\par

I became suddenly aware of my robe, my bandaged thumb, the sand on my sandals, and the fact that I had been looking too long at everything.\par

``Sa-Kailan,'' she said. The prefix in her voice stung, and I was glad of it anyway.\par

``Va-Sheva.''\par

Her eyes went to my thumb. ``Light has already started cutting pieces off you.''\par

``Only the useless ones.''\par

``That cannot be true. You are still standing.''\par

I almost laughed. She stayed grave. Her mouth moved enough to show she had intended the cruelty kindly.\par

``You were at the Hall,'' I said, because I had to say something and chose badly.\par

``So were most of the people.''\par

``I meant---''\par

``I know what you meant.''\par

Wind moved between us, smelling of goat and wet stone and smoke, and under it something dry from beyond the shade. ``You have grown,'' she said. The words should have pleased me. They did not, because they came from someone who had last measured me as a child.\par

``So have you,'' I said, and wished the words back before they had cleared my teeth; it seemed that every time I opened my mouth in front of her, I picked the worst possible words to say. This time she smiled, and it was a real one.\par

``I had four years' advantage.''\par

``Five, almost.''\par

``You counted?''\par

I looked away toward the channels. ``Everyone knows.''\par

My eyes came back from the channels, and went where they had no leave to go: to a bead of sweat that had started at her throat and was sliding down into the neck of her leathers. She saw them land before I did.\par

``No,'' she said. ``Boys know such things when they should be learning where to put their eyes.'' I felt myself stay fifteen and out of reach in front of her, intolerably and completely.\par

``I am learning,'' I said.\par

``So I heard.''\par

``From whom?''\par

``Shield hears many things.''\par

``That is not an answer.''\par

``It is one of the few answers Shield enjoys giving.''\par

I looked at her again. The girl I had known remained somewhere in the face, but duty had been worked over it like leather, and the severity was real --- even now, while speaking, some part of her watched the perimeter.\par

``You are on duty.''\par

``Yes.''\par

``Then should you be speaking to me?''\par

``No.'' The answer startled me. ``But I am,'' she said.\par

The blood came up into my face, and a smile that got away from me, because for the length of that one answer the old ease between us seemed to be back in the room --- the child's conspiracy she and I had once had, thick as thieves --- very far off, buried too deep for me to reach. Then it was gone. Some door had shut between the girl she had been and the Shield she was, and I had no idea when it had closed, nor how it might be opened again.\par

It made me careful. Whatever Va-Sheva gave me caused the same trouble every time, because I wanted it to mean more than she had put into it.\par

``Why?''\par

``Because you looked as if the sky had insulted you.''\par

``It is too large.''\par

``That is one of its lesser faults.''\par

``You do not like it?''\par

``I respect it, and fear it. As should you.''\par

``That is not the same.''\par

``No.''\par

For a moment we stood next to each other and said nothing, both looking outward. I wanted to ask whether she had ever gone past the second ridge, whether the desert felt different without Faith nearby, whether Shield feared the Outsiders as scripture feared them or as wardens feared tracks in dust. The questions were too hungry, and she would hear the hunger in them before she heard the words. So I asked something safer.\par

``The reading said the Outsiders came from the murderer.''\par

``That is what the Book of Blood says.''\par

``You say it as if there is another way to say it.''\par

``I say it as one on duty during prayer access.''\par

The warning was cleanly given. I should have stopped. But the reading had opened something in me, and the desert was still there before me, and Va-Sheva was Shield. ``Do you believe it?''\par

She looked toward the outer path before answering. ``I believe the Mountain is hidden for a reason.''\par

``That is not an answer.''\par

``Do you believe the Book of the Beast?''\par

She had placed another book between us. The Beast belonged to older recitations, to gravures shown by low flame, to prayers spoken when the gates were sealed. It belonged to the idea that the Mountain hid from the Outsiders and held something against them.\par

``You mean,'' I said carefully, ``that the five Keepers hold together the Beast of Hell, in case the Outsiders come back.''\par

``That is what children say.''\par

``It is what Faith teaches children.''\par

``Faith teaches children the shape of things before they can bear the weight of them.''\par

I looked toward the dark line of the outer path. Hell belonged below, beneath stone, beneath water, beneath the red pigment in the carved warnings. Yet the Book of the Beast had always joined two things that unsettled me: the thing held beneath the Mountain, and the people out beyond the desert. One was a danger kept down in the dark; the other moved about in the open daylight.\par

``And what do you say?'' I asked her. ``Not Faith, not Ka-Dhavar of Shield. You.''\par

``I say you have been Candidate for days, and stood outside once, and heard one reading, and already you would have the Beast weighed and answered for you by afternoon.''\par

``Then tell me only whether it is real.''\par

She was silent long enough that I gave up on an answer. Then one of the wardens shifted at the outer markers, and her eyes went to him and came back. ``A gate is not made holy by what is beautiful behind it,'' she said. ``Sometimes it is holy because the thing behind it is terrifying, and to open it wrongly is to answer for every death that follows.''\par

``The Beast is real, then.'' and she kept her silence.\par

The word had thrilled me since childhood --- Beast, Hell, Chains, the five Keepers --- and it had only ever been a word to me.\par

Retreat had become necessary. ``The Oasis is smaller than the gravures made it,'' I said.\par

``Most holy things are. The desert is larger.''\par

I looked toward it. The outer path ran out toward the south-east, where a farther ridge stood between us and the deep desert. On that ridge, for less than a breath, something caught the sun and threw it back, then went dark. I narrowed my eyes.\par

Va-Sheva's gaze moved instantly. ``Where?''\par

``There. On the second ridge, the dark stone.''\par

``What was it?''\par

There was no shout. No drawn blade. No rush of bodies. Va-Sheva made three gestures in succession: two fingers low, the palm closed, the hand cut toward the outer path. And the Oasis rearranged itself.\par

One warden moved behind the goat pens and became invisible in shade. Another knelt near the dust at the outer marker. A third shifted between the prayer group and the spring. A hidden figure answered from the rock above the descent, where I had seen only stone. Then another signal came from higher on the slope. Shield had been around us the whole time --- visible wardens, hidden bodies, lines of sight, markers arranged so that a disturbance would show itself before it grew into a real danger.\par

The Faith attendant looked up, alarmed, but kept silent. One of the old women tried to rise and was pressed gently back by her companion. A goat stopped chewing. The bees continued.\par

Va-Sheva crouched beside the kneeling warden and touched the ground. She lifted her fingers, rubbed something between them, then looked toward the outer path, the Balance terraces, the prayer group, and the ridge where I had seen the light. A Balance worker had gone still beside the snail trays, water ladle in hand. Va-Sheva pointed to him, then to the shaded wall. He moved at once.\par

A flat side faced inward where the others faced skyward. Va-Sheva kept her hands off it. ``No one returns inside yet.''\par

The Faith attendant's eyes widened. ``The access period---''\par

``By whose order?''\par

I heard the words enter the Oasis like a bar lowered across a gate. Faith governed prayer. Balance governed water and work. But here, at the edge of the world, Shield could interrupt sacred timing with two words and no explanation, and everyone bent.\par

A young Balance boy near the herb beds began to cry, old enough to be ashamed of it and too young to stop.\par

Va-Sheva walked the marker line without stepping beyond it. At the turned stone she knelt, took a thin bone pin from her sleeve, and lifted a single strand from the ground; it caught the light once, pale against her glove, before she sealed it in a covered dish. ``Second gate,'' she said, and a hidden watcher answered from the slope. Then she turned to the workers. ``Count.''\par

Balance counted the way Balance counts: every tool to its tally, every beast to its pen, every worker to his name, the whole hollow reckoned in the time it takes to tell it. Nothing was missing from the work.\par

``Prayer group?'' The Faith attendant named seven. The warden corrected her. ``Six.'' The attendant froze. My stomach tightened before my mind understood.\par

Then one of the old women said sharply, ``Mara.'' The woman beside her looked round. ``She has gone out to the south-west markers.'' No one moved.\par

She had crouched against a dry stone, one hand flat on it, breathing hard, unable to rise. Whether she had gone out there for a reason of her own or simply lost her way, I never learned. Va-Sheva took the sprig gently, gave it to the worker, then helped the old woman stand. Va-Sheva leaned close and said something that made the woman close her eyes and nod.\par

The Faith attendant called us back to the prayer stones for the return blessing. The access period had been shortened and lengthened both, made irregular by the containment. Then the washing: the dust of the open rinsed from the hands and the face in a shallow bowl, the water darkening with it. And over each of us the Faith attendant spoke.\par

``What has touched the open returns under shadow.''\par

We answered. ``Under shadow, we return.''\par

We re-entered in single file, and the ceiling came back like a hand over the skull. Relief came with it, shameful and immediate.\par

The Oasis was proof, and I granted it: no spring could hold against that heat without the Mountain standing over it, and who set the Mountain there but God?
