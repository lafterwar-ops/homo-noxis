\typesetchapter{Chapter 20}{Beast}

The Hall of Cataclysm is not the Hall of Ceremony. I had lived my whole life in the Mountain and never known there was a second hall, lower, older. I came to it with my hands unbound, because a man cannot do what they needed me to do with his hands tied. Being unbound felt worse than the rope, because it meant they had decided to use me.\par

It lay beneath the deep reserve, past a Shield gate that Ka-Dhavar opened with a thing he wore against his own skin and a word he said to no one. It opened into a chamber shaped like the inside of a bell and colder than any place I have stood, because the heat of the Mountain, all of it, the fire that cooked the bread and warmed the wards, was gathered and sent elsewhere here, down, into the floor. The floor of the Hall of Cataclysm was warm as a living thing while the air above it froze. And down the middle of it fell a single great shaft, a throat cut straight through the floor of the world. Around the throat, at the five points of a star, stood five stations, each raised a little and set into a shielded recess behind a window of polished stone, so that the five could see the throat, and the water when it came, and one another across it, and which hand hesitated and which withdrew. The offices had come to the Hall apart, each by a separate route and door, and stood now together, in one room, in plain sight of one another, which I had been raised to believe the five never were. The four surviving Keepers stood at four of the five stations. Where the fifth should have stood, at Faith's place, there was only a low stone bowl on a plinth and a fitting for an instrument that no longer had an operator. Ka-Elun was dead under a fallen roof, and Va-Maera, her Veil, lay breathing in a cot in the deep wards with her hearing gone out of her for good and her mind sunk too far down to be reached.\par

We had been sealed nine days when they brought me down. Nine days of the Mountain learning to be a tomb: the wards rationed, the air turned and turned again, the wounded from the Faith passage laid in rows. And above us, muffled by the whole thickness of the Seal, the patient thud of an enemy who did not yet understand what waited behind the door they were drilling through. Nine days for the four surviving Keepers to arrive, one argument at a time, at the place they now stood.\par

``We cannot fire it,'' Ka-Cheryn of Balance said, ``and we cannot fail to fire it, and I have been standing here for an hour unable to move for the truth of both.'' She turned her heavy face to me. ``The Beast needs five. It has always needed five. The machine is cut that way in the stone, deeper than any custom. The fifth office is the one that listens, and the one that listens is dead.''\par

I said, foolishly, that the law had already ruled it.\par

``The law does not permit this; it commands it,'' she said. ``Justice found the condition met nine days ago, and a finding of that kind is not a leave we may take or decline; it is an order, and there is no unfinding it and no second asking. The four of us who are left cannot reconsider a stroke of it. What the law cannot do is put the missing hand on the machine. It can command the Beast to be fired. It cannot make us able to fire it without Faith.''\par

They told me what the Beast was by making me help build the firing of it, which is the only way anyone was ever taught it, because there is no book of it; a book could be taken.\par

Balance is the water and the fire. I had thought, when they said \emph{the deep water}, of a single black lake in a single black cavern, and I said so, and Ka-Cheryn looked at me the way she looked at a clever man. ``A cavern that size would fall in on itself in a season,'' she said. ``It is not a lake. It is a body.'' What the Mountain had made, across eleven thousand years, was not a hole full of water but a whole second mountain of water threaded through the first: flooded faults, natural cracks opened and lined and joined, cisterns cut into the deep rock, pressure vaults, and great mains running from all of it down toward the Hall, so that the weight of the entire distributed reserve stood always behind whatever Balance chose to let fall. ``Five hundred measures,'' she said, and let me understand that a measure was itself a thing past picturing --- a million tonnes of water, the weight of a hundred-pace cube of the sea, one load of the Beast. ``Five hundred of those, held through the whole body of the Mountain. That is what stands above this throat. That is the ballast I would not let anyone drink.''\par

Above the Hall, close over our heads, the five header cells were the near end of it: five separate stone chambers, dome-headed, each near fifty paces across and a hundred high, each holding a fifth of a full load and no more, filled from the great mains and shut behind their own gates. ``Kept apart because water kept apart is water you can govern,'' Ka-Cheryn said. ``Loose one and the Mountain shrugs. Loose all five together and'' --- she did not finish it. When the gates of the five opened as one, their waters did not fall as five things; they fell into a common plenum below the cells, a collecting throat where the five became one before they ever reached the great shaft, so that what went past the five windows was a single body, a whole measure, a hundred paces of water falling as one hammer onto a fire miles down. ``The windows are five because the cells are five,'' she said, seeing me count them. ``What falls is one thing: a hundred-pace cube of water, a city block built of the sea, a million tonnes let down the throat of the world a little past every twenty minutes for three days. There is nothing in your outside world to set beside it.''\par

The fire is nowhere near the floor we were standing on. She took me to the rail at the lip of the throat and made me look down until looking stopped meaning anything. The place where the water meets the heat lies miles below the Hall, at the foot of a shaft that runs past the last worked stone into rock no eye has ever seen. ``Were it near,'' she said, ``we should be having no conversation at all, and this Mountain would be a bowl in the sand with a story attached to it.'' The water where it touches the heat has no time to boil; it flashes to steam in the instant, and the steam is the whole engine of the thing. Part of it Balance drives up a second throat, a colossal gallery glazed against steam and set far from any ward, where it turns the wheels and pumps that lift the next measure into the cells and haul the gates shut behind it; and farther along that gallery, where the steam has spent its heat, it gathers cold again in flooded passages and runs back down into the body of the deep water, so that better than half of every measure the Beast throws comes home to it within a day and is used again. ``It is not a miracle and it is not for ever,'' she said. ``The water works and the water mostly returns. What does not return is the heat. The heat is a bank, laid up down there since before there was a people to want it, and every measure we flash spends a piece of it that will not be warm again in any span I have a number for. Picture a debt. We will fire two hundred and eleven of our five hundred measures, and get back more than half of what we spend, so there is a reserve left at the end. But there will be no more fire. One firing. After it, a cold hole, and a broken throat, and ruined cells, and a bank of heat gone down into the world; and it will be warm again when it is warm again, which is a length of time I have no use for, and neither will my grandchildren's grandchildren.'' She said it the way a quartermaster says a thing, and I took it down as arithmetic. It was eighty days before I learned what I had been told.\par

Two hundred and eleven, I said, because it was the one number in all of it I could hold, and I asked why that, when she had five hundred to spend. ``Because two hundred and eleven is the measure,'' she said, ``and five hundred is the store. You do not empty a well to fill a cup. Strike fewer and the deep place does not stay open long enough to gather; strike more, and the far coupling has already torn past use and the extra water only shakes rubble. The store is there so the head never fails and the Mountain is not left dry. It was never the number we fire.''\par

She made me learn the first valve with my own hand before she would trust my word at the eye, because, she said, a hand that has never felt the water fight the stone has no business timing it. She set my palm on the great worn wheel and had me crack it a hair. It bucked under my hand like a live animal, the deep water wanting up and out, and it took all my weight to hold it to a hair. Ka-Cheryn watched me shake against it. ``Now picture holding that steady, to the drop, for an hour, while the floor tries to throw you off it,'' she said, ``and you will understand why Balance is the office we give to the patient and the strong, and never once in eleven thousand years to the clever. You will not touch these again. But now you know what your one word is going to cost.''\par

Light is where the blow goes.\par

Ka-Raedin took me to the Light station of that Hall and showed me the rest: everything I had been taught in the Veil's Chamber --- the red glass, the token-hole, the way a beam bends and spreads and comes back together at a place you can choose --- had been the children's version of this.\par

Because a blow driven into rock does not stay a single blow. It spreads out through the body of the Earth in every direction, weakening as it goes; and then the world does the thing no hand could do for it: a part of that spreading, having gone its separate ways through the whole thickness of the world and its molten heart, finds its way back together, of its own accord, toward the one place exactly opposite where it began. It is a smeared and partial gathering; what reaches the far pole reaches it weakened, and that it reaches strong enough to matter at all is a thing of the standing-place. The Earth does the aiming. No glass I ever cut could bend a thing the size of a continent. What a hand can do is choose where to stand when it lets go.\par

``And when,'' Ka-Raedin said, ``though the when is not mine but the world's.'' For the world has a note of its own, he told me, a period at which it will answer if it is asked: strike it, and strike it again a little past every twenty minutes, and each blow finds the whole ball of the Earth already moving the way the last blow set it moving, and adds to it, and the swell grows and holds its beat. The proving strokes wake that note and find its phase; after that the world keeps the time itself. ``That is the clock,'' he said. ``It is not the aim. The world's breath tells us \emph{when}; my geometry, and the paths I bend the shaking along, tell us \emph{where}; and the two must be made to keep the same appointment for three days. Miss the beat and you are only breaking your own house. Keep it, and the far skin of the world gathers what you send.''\par

That standing-place was chosen once, before a single gallery was cut, so that the far pole of the blow would fall where the crust was already broken. Light's office is to keep that choosing true, because the ground travels --- a few centimetres in a year, a few paces in a century, and over eleven thousand years far enough that the far end of a blow aimed from the founding-mark would come down a great way from where it must. And so Light measures the number and carries it down eleven thousand years, so that the Mountain knows to the pace where it stands and what lies under the far side of the world from it. In the Veil's Chamber they had let me call that a devotion.\par

``You bent light in a chamber to teach a boy his office,'' Ka-Raedin said, and he looked at me directly, which he had not once done since I came back. ``We bend the Earth's own shaking through the Earth's own core, to a single point on the far skin of the world. You were taught the toy of it, and you were the best with the toy any of us had seen, which is why they feared you, and why I am about to need you.'' He held the lamp between us a moment. ``I told your trial that light put out by a narrow door does not stay a line --- that your path, thrown so far, would come back and interfere with the Mountain --- and I would not say in an open room what I meant by it. I meant this hour. Here you are, come the whole way round, standing where the interference gathers, the one hand left that can read it: the Mountain put you out to keep you off the machine, and now\ldots{} the machine is yours.''\par

And he needed me sooner than either of us wished. Light's count is only ever as good as the marks it was taken from, and nine days of the enemy's patient hammering had shifted or buried half the sunk marks of the upper galleries, the fixed points eleven thousand years of measuring had been carried forward from without a break. Ka-Raedin took me down a stair behind the station to the master mark, a plug of dark stone set into the living rock with sight-lines cut away from it in five directions, and showed me that two of the five no longer agreed with the tables. From those marks alone we could no longer say whether the Mountain stood where the record said it stood; and if it did not, then everything Light had ever certified about the far side of the world was a number somebody had once believed.\par

We could not re-cut eleven thousand years in a night. What we could do was take the position again, from the beginning, by another road: the three sight-lines that had survived, and the equinox mark down the Archive shaft that the sun itself trues once a year and no hammering can move, and the old tables to check us as we went. It was my art, the exact and only art I had been the best of my generation at and been broken for. I did it in the cold with Ka-Raedin holding the lamp and reading me the figures. And at the end of it I could tell him, with my own two hands' work standing behind it, that the Mountain sat where the record said it sat, within a pace. When the last line closed, a sign at the foot of the master mark caught the lamp and threw it back green, which is the Mountain's oldest way of saying yes, and my Keeper let out a breath I think he had been holding for nine days.\par

And then there was Faith's place, empty, and everything stopped there. I had learned Balance and Light, and I could see, even I could see, that neither was any use without what the dead woman had done.\par

For the blow, once let go, comes back: it goes down into the world and part of it returns, an echo up through the rock to the Hall, and that returning echo is the whole secret of the weapon. A single blow, however well placed, does nothing whatever to the far side of the world. What the blows do at first, they do to the deep place directly beneath us: the early loads break the hot rock across, drive water into the cracks, and hold the deep floor shattered and flooded and under pressure, so that each load has more to work with than the one before. And each can be added to the last only if the next load falls while the world is still swung the right way and the deep place still open --- the one short instant, a little past every twenty minutes, when the world's breath and the returning echo agree. Faith's work, the dead woman's work, was to listen to the returning echo through a stone tuned to hear it, and to know from the shape of what came back whether the moment was true, and to name it. She was the metronome of the end of the world. And she was dead.\par

``It is called the prayer,'' Ka-Raedin said, ``because that is how it was taught, as a listening and an answering, and a Faith is trained from a child to feel the phase of it the way you and I feel a held breath. There is no one left who can feel it. And without it we have four offices and a bank of valves and no way on Earth to time the strokes, and an untimed Beast is a mountain shaking itself to rubble over its own children for no purpose at all.''\par

Sarah had been silent since we came down into the cold. She spoke now, from the dark at the edge of the lamplight, where she had been standing with Paul, the two Outsiders, in the one place in the world no Outsider was ever meant to stand.\par

``Play me the echo,'' she said. ``On your stone. Let me hear one stroke and its return. I have spent sixty-six years listening to exactly this and calling it my life's work, and I never once knew it had a name, or a hall, or a dead priestess. Play it for me.''\par

\ornament

They did not want to. Three of the four surviving Keepers balked at the blasphemy of letting an Outsider woman lay her ear against the listening stone of Faith, and Ka-Leth ended the objection without a word, because Justice had already found the greater act lawful, and a man under an order to end the world cannot forbid the one means left of carrying it out.\par

So Ka-Cheryn let the smallest calibration drop the machine could make, a hundredth part of a load, the merest allotment against a wet stone. The floor jumped once under us, and a sound went down and away into the world. Sarah stood with her hand and her ear against the Faith stone and waited. The echo came home, and I watched the whole of her life arrive in her face at once.\par

``That is it,'' she said, and her voice had gone somewhere I had not heard it go. ``That is the five strokes. That is the thing under the bomb. That is what I heard at Reggane at twenty-seven and have chased across sixty-six years. It was this. It was always this. It was a mountain full of frightened priests, testing that the end of the world still answered when they knocked, and stopping, always, always, after five, before the swing could build.'' She turned her face toward the four Keepers, and there were no tears in it, which was worse. ``Your drills. Your holy little drills, every time a Keeper died and a new one had to be shown the machine still worked. I built my whole self out of the echo of your caution. And now you need someone to do the thing your caution was invented to prevent, which is to not stop after five.'' She took her hand off the stone. ``I can hear the phase. God forgive me, I have the best ear for it left alive. I can time your strokes. I can be your dead woman.''\par

\ornament

She could hear the phase, the living seismic return, better than any of them. But she could not read the stone the way a Faith read it, could not always tell the true return from the Mountain's own noise, from the enemy's charges still landing above us, from the structure's groaning, because a Faith had spent a lifetime learning the one voice of this one Mountain and Sarah had spent hers learning the general grammar of all ground everywhere. She had the ear and not the tongue.\par

Paul had the tongue, and he had it because Ka-Elun had put it into him with her own mouth before the roof took her. In the sealed hearing, the fragment Faith deposed into him was this: the timing itself, the sequence, the how-long-to-wait and the how-to-know-the-true-call, which had never once been written, because a book can be taken. It had lived as the liturgy, the words a Faith is taught as a child and says over every drill of a life without ever being told that the psalm is an operating manual for the end of the world. She had recited it into Paul's turning machine plainly, an old woman handing a heavy thing across before her arms failed; and an hour later the roof came down on her. So it fell to Paul, whose whole trade was reading what a text says under what it claims to say, to hold Faith's deposed prayer against the half-knowledge of the low under-priests and turn each holy verse back into the instruction it had been. \emph{When the stone answers in the third measure, and not before, let fall.} He translated the end of the world out of scripture into arithmetic, in the cold, by lamplight.\par

It was not that I alone, of all the souls who ever lived, knew the five offices; the Mountain's whole terror had been built on fearing exactly that, and it was already wrong. Paul knew them, all five, laid into him across a night under the Threshold's own law; the wall of ignorance my people had murdered heretics to keep had already come down, quietly, in a scholar with a machine, and the world had not ended of it. What I had that no book and no deposition could give was not the knowing. It was the holding. I could take Paul's translated sequence and Sarah's living ear and Ka-Raedin's geometry and Ka-Cheryn's valves and hold them all in one sight at one instant, and feel where they had to meet, and say the word that made them meet. The knowing could be shared, and had been, and no single hand was the stronger for it, for the water was still Balance's and the token still Shield's. The seeing-all-at-once could not be shared, and that, and only that, was what they had come the whole way round the world to fetch me back for.\par

It did not work the first time, or the third. We built the composite the way you build anything in a hurry out of parts that were never meant to fit: badly, and with blood. Once, deep in it, Paul said through his teeth, to no one, that he thought his leg was broken. Sarah did not take her hand from the stone. ``There are two hundred and six bones in the body, Mr Morgan,'' she said. ``That is one of them.'' He went back to the words. The first true stroke and return we tried, Sarah called the phase off the living wave and Paul called it off the liturgy, and the two calls did not agree, a whole measure apart. It was Sarah who found the fix, because Sarah had spent sixty-six years learning to tell a true signal from noise. ``Give me the stone and give me the words both,'' she said, ``and stop trying to choose. The words tell me which returns to listen for. The stone tells me when. They are only right together.'' And after that the three of us were one instrument, an ear and a tongue and an eye. It broke the wall my people had built around the founding law without breaking the law itself: between us the knowing was whole, and in no one of us the power --- the water still Balance's, the seal still Shield's. The Mountain had mistaken the enforced ignorance for the law. It was beautiful.\par

``Do you understand what you are asking me to become,'' I said to Ka-Raedin, my own Keeper, the man whose office I had broken.\par

``Yes,'' he said. ``I have understood it since the roof fell on Faith. It has to be one mind holding the five at once, and there is exactly one such mind, and we exiled it into the desert, and it has come home on the one night we cannot do without it.'' He did not look away this time. ``You were right, is the thing I have not been able to say. All those years ago. You pulled the cord because you saw it. That a wall of ignorance holds only until it is tested. I helped to break you for it. And tonight the wall is tested, and the only thing that can save the people behind it is the sight we punished you for having. Be the eye. God and the Beast forgive us both. Be the eye.''\par

I have wondered since what I truly felt in the moment I agreed, and the honest answer is the one I am least proud of: that under the horror there was a small, clean, terrible relief. All my life my sight had been named a sickness, the flaw I was broken for. And here, at the end of everything, the sickness had become the last hope of five thousand souls, and the men who had punished me for it stood begging me to use it, and I was glad, and ashamed of it. I said yes to save my people. I also said yes because it felt, once and once only in my whole life, like being given leave to be the thing I am.\par

\ornament

Ka-Leth took his place at the Justice station, and Sa-Tavan beside him with the wet clay, there to attend the firing, not to permit it, and to see that what the order commanded was truthfully written down, so that whoever survived the world would know it was done in the light even as the light went out. There was no per-stroke word to give and none to withhold. And Shield kept no abort, because there was no stop to keep; Shield's work that night was the work it had been given nine days gone --- to seal the Mountain, defend the mechanism, guard the Seal, tend the local containment while the machine did what the law had loosed it to do. Once the phase and count and aim and water and Seal were set, no hand in that Hall could recall the sequence, nor was any meant to. It would run until it was spent, or until it was physically broken, and breaking it was the one thing none of us had come to do.\par

What Balance still governed was the calibration, and only that --- its quantity and its procedure, not whether it happened. ``I have allotted the proving water,'' Ka-Cheryn said. ``Nine small strokes, less than a tenth of one load in all of them, and I will account for every hundredth part to the record. If the proving fails, the order over us does not change; only the machine will have been shown unable to obey it, and we will die commanded to do a thing we cannot. If it holds, I set the machine to the true measure and commit it, and after that my hand governs nothing --- the water governs the water.''\par

We began with the five that have always been allowed.\par

Drop, and wait, and listen; drop, and wait, and listen. Ka-Cheryn let the small proving strokes fall one at a time, a hundredth of a load in each, and Sarah stood with her ear to the Faith stone and read the return, and Paul held the liturgy against it and named the measure, and I held the two of them together and watched the phase. The first five were the drill my people had worked more than a thousand times through eleven thousand years, the sacred figure, the ordered signature that had gone out under the sand and been mistaken by the whole listening world for the settling of a hill. Five strokes, and stop. Every child in the Mountain three hundred feet above us knew that you stop at five.\par

The sixth stroke was the worst thing I did that night, and I did far worse things before the sun we could not see came up. It was smaller than the first great load by an amount past reckoning; at the scale of the world it was nothing, a pin set against a bell, powerless to wake or tear anything on the far side of the world. And it was the first time in eleven thousand years that a hand of the Mountain had, on purpose, struck a sixth. I felt the wrongness of it in my teeth before Ka-Cheryn's wheel had come to rest. Somewhere far above us a count that had begun in fear reached five and waited for the silence that always came, and the silence did not come, and a sixth jump went up through the floor of the wards, and I have been told that a sound went through the whole buried city in that moment that was not a scream and was not a prayer and had a little of both in it. We had gone past five. After that there was no number that could not be struck.\par

And almost with the sixth blow the enemy came through into the upper Faith wards. There was a new sound, nearer, human, and a runner came down white-faced to Ka-Dhavar with the words \emph{they are in the upper Faith wards}. The outer seal around the whole Beast-quarter was not yet closed --- it wanted the better part of an hour more --- and if they reached the Hall inside that hour they might, groping in the dark for the machine, break a pressure line, foul the phase governor, or wreck a gate. I saw Jean-Charles, who had stood all this while at the Hall's own door with a Shield blade he had no traditional right to hold, look at Va-Sheva once, and she gave him a warden and a nod, and he went up into the Mountain to buy Shield the one thing it still needed, which was time. He held a stair of the Faith wards, with a handful of wardens and a boy who ran messages and a barricade of the Mountain's own furniture, against soldiers trained for exactly this. He did not know that the three strokes still to come were the very hour Shield needed to close the last seal. He only knew it was his to hold.\par

Between the sixth and the ninth the three of us became, at last, the instrument. Sarah learned to throw away the enemy's charges and the structure's groan and hear only the one true voice coming home; Paul's translated verses and her living ear stopped disagreeing and began to name the same instant in two languages; Ka-Raedin read me his geometry and I felt the far focus settle into true; and Ka-Cheryn, watching the returns stack against her small proving strokes, worked the measure at her bank of wheels and gave it to the record. Two hundred and eleven. The whole and the right of it, for the state of the fire tonight and the answer the Earth was giving back and the far mark we meant to reach. On the ninth stroke she engaged the count wheel --- a toothed thing of iron and stone, one tooth to a load, set to two hundred and eleven and no more --- and locked the phase governor to the world's own breath, so that the returning wave itself would trip each release; and the five header cells stood filled and their refill engines charged with the proving steam. And in the same minutes, as the ninth stroke locked, the hour Jean-Charles had bought ran out: Ka-Dhavar sang the last outer seal of the Beast-quarter shut with a sound like a door closing in the root of a tooth, and the machine that had waited on human hands ceased to wait on them. After that the enemy could hammer at the outside of the Seal for ever and never reach the machine behind it.\par

Jean-Charles came through ahead of the closing seal, filthy and torn and alive, and stood again at the Hall's own door; and I did not learn until it was long over what the hour had cost the boy who ran the messages.\par

After the ninth stroke no person released another load. The returning phase itself would trip each release; the steam of the load just spent would lift the next measure into the cells and haul the gates to; the count wheel would turn one tooth; and when it had turned two hundred and eleven teeth it would close the feed for ever, with no hand near it. We were not going to fire the Beast. We had built a thing that was going to fire itself, a little past once every twenty minutes, for very nearly three days. All that remained to the living was to tend the seals, pressures, condensers and listening apparatus, and watch the governor follow a phase no hand could hurry or hold --- to supervise the ending of the world as a man supervises a river he cannot stop.\par

\ornament

When it was ready, each office said its part to the record and to me. Ka-Cheryn of Balance said, ``The measure is whole.'' Ka-Raedin of Light said, ``The path is true.'' Ka-Dhavar of Shield said, ``The Mountain is held.'' And Ka-Leth of Justice said, ``The law stands, and the record is open.'' It was not a vote; the vote was nine days behind them; it was four offices reporting that their parts of the commanded act stood ready. Va-Sheva, who under Shield had spent nine days moving five thousand souls off the lines where the recoil would run, into the deep shielded galleries, reported that the wards and the recoil-lines were clear, and folded her hands. And Paul, the witness, did the one thing his office allowed him, and he did it without rising, in the flat voice he kept for true things. ``Let the reel show,'' he said, ``that an outsider sat in this room and said it aloud: that the far side of the world is full of people who never heard the word Mountain --- harbours, wards of the sick, children being put to bed under a sky you are about to aim at them --- and that not one of them ever came looking for you. I cannot stop this, and I was not brought here to stop it, and there is no longer any hand in this Hall that could. I will only not have it written, after, that the cost went into the ground unnamed.'' No office was bound to answer him, and none did. Then he held his little machine up a fraction, to catch the rest clearly.\par

And I, who had been made the eye that saw the whole and owned no part of it, did the one thing left that was mine to do. I looked across the five live systems at once --- the ear, the tongue, the geometry, the valves, the world's own breath coming round --- and felt them, for the first time, all true together in the same instant, the governor about to pass from the last proving stroke into the first whole measure; and I named it, because naming it was the eye's whole office, and because the machine was already moving under the word as I said it.\par

``Let them fall,'' I said.\par

The first whole load was the moment the difference in scale stopped being a number and became a thing my body knew. The five gates opened together, and the five cells emptied into the common plenum, and their waters married in the dark and came past the five windows as one, a hundred paces of water falling as a single body, a million tonnes let down the throat of the world in one breath. I had thought the proving strokes had shown me the thing; they had shown me nothing. This was to those what the sea is to a cup. The floor did not jump; the floor \emph{leaned}, the whole Hall tilting on the recoil of a million tonnes going down, and caught itself, and a sound came up the shaft afterward that I felt in the wet of my eyes. Ka-Cheryn's hand was nowhere near the wheel. The wheel had turned itself. The count read one.\par

And then it read two, a little past twenty minutes later, with no hand having done a thing but watch a stone answer and a phase hold true; and three; and I understood, standing there, that we had reached the far edge of what human beings do and stepped over it, that the horror of the thing was no longer that we were ending the world but that we had finished choosing to, and now only attended it, the way you attend a fever, the way you attend a birth, present and useless and unable to leave.\par

I will not give you two hundred and eleven of them. No one living bore the whole of the doing awake, and the mind will not hold a thing that large by looking at all of it, only by looking at pieces; so I will give the pieces I have.\par

The early loads, through that first long night, we watched like men at a deathbed who cannot yet believe the breathing will not simply stop. Each time the count advanced I half expected some ancient obedience in the stone to reach up and refuse what we had done; and each time nothing rose, and the number climbed, always nine behind the number the wards were calling in the dark. Sarah read the early returns herself, because the phase had to be proved true stroke on stroke until the swing was surely caught; but she was ninety-three, and near the fifteenth load her knees went under her, and Ka-Raedin caught her, and she cursed him, and slept some hours on the warm floor of the Hall of Cataclysm with her coat under her head while the machine went on without her, and woke to a number near thirty and was still a moment taking it in, and then went back to the stone.\par

No body could attend the end of the world for three days without rest, and Sarah least of all. So we learned to keep a watch on a thing that needed no keeping, only witnessing --- Sarah at the stone for the readings that mattered, the turns of the phase, the changes in the fire's answer, and resting between them while a low under-priest held the ear and called her the instant anything drifted. Paul could not lay three days onto a single reel; he changed them, and marked the times on each in his small hand, and recorded selected intervals, and slept badly across two chairs on a leg a warden had bound and that had, after all, only been wrenched, and woke to record again. The Keepers and their attendants took the supervision in shifts, watching pressures and seals and the trueness of the returning phase, none of them able to do the one thing no one could do, which was stop it. We ate, standing, the rationed bread. We changed the bandages of the ones hurt in the clearing of the recoil-lines. We waited, over and over, beneath the next inevitable number.\par

And all the while, above the sealed Hall, the Mountain we were doing it for felt every stroke and did not know what it felt. I learned afterward how the dark had gone for the five thousand. They had felt the floor jump, over and over, in fear at first, and then, because people cannot stay afraid for three days, in a kind of dreadful worship. Someone in the deep wards had begun to count the strokes aloud, the way Faith had counted every drill in living memory; and the count had spread, ward to ward, and passed from throat to throat as throats gave out --- an old woman carrying it while the young slept, and a child taking it up when she dozed, so that no one voice ever counted for three days but the number never once stopped climbing, handed on in the dark like a lamp. They had begun at the first proving stroke, nine strokes before Balance's wheel began to count the full loads; so the number the Mountain called ran always nine ahead of the machine's, and where the wheel stopped at two hundred and eleven the wards would say two hundred and twenty --- both true, for they counted different things. People slept, and woke, and asked the number, and were told a number far past any they had slept on, and understood, slowly, ward by ward, over the first day and into the second, that this was no succession drill, because a succession drill stops at five, and this had not stopped. They did not know what it was. They knew only that the stone was speaking, and that their people had always, always answered the stone; and so they answered it, and became, without ever being told, the choir of their own ending.\par

The hundredth load fell some four-and-thirty hours in, though there was no noon down there, only the count and the fatigue. By then the Hall had changed. The whole body of the Mountain held a low sustained note now, a ringing that no longer died between the strokes but stood in the stone and the teeth, because the swing had grown strong enough to keep the rock moving on its own between one blow and the next. Dust came down from the bell of the roof in a steady fall and we stopped brushing it off. Ka-Cheryn sat by her silent wheels with nothing to do, her whole patient strong office reduced to watching a machine perform the restraint she had spent her life embodying, and I think that was the cruelest thing the three days did to any of us, to make Balance idle at the end.\par

The two-hundredth load fell near the end of the third day, and by then the difference between the living and the machine had worn very thin. We were husks attending an engine. And near the very end, at a load I could no longer name by its number, only feel, the swing came to its full --- the world swung as far as the world would go, the water coming down to meet it exactly true --- and that was the load where the floor did not lean and did not jump.\par

The floor \emph{left}. For a moment that had no length the whole Hall of Cataclysm was in the air, all of us, the lamps, the wet tablet, the cold, and then the world caught us again. It was not the far side of the world breaking; no single load did that. It was the near thing: the deep coupling chamber and the throat directly beneath us recoiling at the very height of the accumulated swing, the whole gathered violence of three days coming back up the shaft at once into the floor we stood on and throwing the Hall a hand's breadth into the dark. The far crust was breaking, but by the whole train of blows landing one behind another across the three days, not by this one. This was only the load at which my own lifted body finally knew the size of the thing we had made.\par

The two hundred and eleventh load fell not quite seventy-two hours after the first, in the grey of a dawn we could not see, and the count wheel turned its last tooth, and the feed closed itself for ever with a small final sound, almost polite, a valve seating home that no hand had touched. The refill engines, with no gate left to charge, ran down. The steam in the great exhalation gallery thinned and stopped. And where there had been, for three days, a hundred-pace cube of water falling a little past every twenty minutes, there was now only the ringing the falling had built, standing on in the stone with nothing more to feed it. The engines had ceased. The Earth had not.\par

For a while after the last load no one in the Hall moved or spoke. The lamps had guttered in the great final exhalation, and someone trimmed them, slowly, and the small ordinary sound of a wick being trued was almost past bearing, the sound of a person doing a small careful living thing in the first minute after the end of everything. Ka-Leth set down the last of the reckoning. Sa-Tavan pressed the final mark into the wet clay and laid the stylus beside it, square, the way you set down a tool you know you will not need again. Ka-Cheryn of Balance closed her dead valves one by one out of habit, slowly, the way you put a great animal to sleep, and then sat down on the floor of the Hall of Cataclysm, an old woman at the end of her office, and put her face into her two hands, dry-eyed, only resting the weight of her head as though the neck could no longer hold it. And I stood in the middle of the four offices whose every work I had held in one sight at once, and felt the seeing drain out of me and leave me a single tired man in a cold room. I have never been so grateful to be small again, nor so certain that I would never be entirely small again.\par

Paul was beside me in the dark, and he had started the little machine again, quietly, and I asked him why, when there was nothing to hear. ``There is always something to hear,'' he said. ``And if anyone is ever going to know what a thing like this was, they will need to know that mostly it was quiet, and that a tired old woman sat down on the floor. Not the fire. The old woman sitting down.''\par

Then we waited, because the far side of the world had not yet said the last of it back. Each load we sent had gone out toward the far pole in its own moment, launched rather than stored beneath us, so that all through the three days the far crust had been taking the train of them, load on load, a little behind our own count. What was left, when the engines stopped, was the tail of it: the last load still had its long crossing to make, to a pole already struck two hundred and ten times, and then the pole's answer had its crossing home to us. So there was a wait, after the machine fell silent, with nothing to do in it but breathe, and no one able to think of a thing the right size to say. It was the longest such wait there will ever be.\par

Once in that waiting Sarah spoke to me, without turning from the stone. ``You felt it,'' she said. ``When it was building. When it answered the word. You felt what I felt at the bomb, at twenty-seven.'' I said that I had. ``Now you know,'' she said. ``Now you know it was never wickedness in me. It was the best feeling there is: the whole of a great thing moving because you understood it and it did not. That is the thing your Mountain should have taught, instead of teaching fear --- that the reaching for the whole feels exactly like being alive, and that is why no wall of terror was ever going to hold it, and why, in the end, they were right to build a Beast instead of a wall, because you cannot frighten a person out of the one pleasure that feels to them like the meaning of their life. You can only make certain that the day a hand closes on the whole is the day the whole is taken away from everyone.'' She took her palm off the stone a moment, and laid it back. ``And do you see the joke of it, at the very end? We got our hands on the whole. The three of us. For nine strokes and three nights we held it entire, the way no one is ever to hold it --- and the one thing we built with it was a machine that did not need our hands at all. We reached the whole, and it walked out of our fingers and finished itself in the dark. I have wanted, before I died, to say that to one living person who would understand it. I did not think it would turn out to be a boy from the inside of the very thing I spent my life trying to steal.''\par

It came as a line. The ink received it before our bodies did: Faith's instrument, the one with no operator, drew it by itself off the returning wave, a single long stroke climbing the drum higher than any mark any living Keeper had ever seen. It came from the exact far pole of the shot, with nothing of below us or around us in it, arriving home after its long crossing to tell Sarah, who alone could read what it meant, that the far crust had answered; that the thing we aimed had struck the roots of the Earth where the Earth was already weakest, and that the roots had given way. That much the stone showed, and she named it observation, and would call it nothing grander: a rupture at the far pole, of a size the drum could measure. What she said next she was careful to name for what it was. ``A tear that size, along that ring, will put a made winter into the sky over the whole far world, and in a season over this one,'' she said, ``and I cannot show you that on any stone in this hall, and no one living will ever stand on the far skin of the world to prove me right or wrong. That part is not the instrument's. That part is mine, and it is a reckoning and not a reading, and you will write it down as mine.'' We wrote it down as hers. None of us doubted her.\par

``It is done,'' Sarah said, and took her hand off the stone at last, and looked at it, her own hand, as though it belonged to someone she had spent her life hunting and had, in the end, caught, and become. ``It is done. We have struck the far side of the world.''\par

And then, a moment after the ink had it, the same answer reached our bodies: the far side's reply came all the way home, up through the floor of the Hall, a slow swell that lifted the whole ground the way the deck of a ship lifts on a sea, and settled, and lifted again. It did not go still. The Beast had finished; the engines were cold; not one drop more would ever fall down that throat --- but a world struck like a bell does not stop when the hammer stops. It stands on its long low note and fades by a hair at a time, over days and longer. Somewhere on the far skin of the world, at the one point exactly opposite us, the blows we had carried upon that note had already become fire, and wave, and a darkness climbing into the sky. Under us it was only the slow rise and fall, gentler each hour and never quite done: the Mountain no longer striking, and still no more still than the ringing Earth it was a part of.\par

Paul reached over and, for the second time in my knowing of him, stopped the machine, because there was nothing left that hour worth the reel. And in the silence after it there was no sound at all from the world outside --- no charge, no drill, no soldier, no sky --- only, under everything, the slow toll of a struck planet, which we would feel for many days, and which was the one witness that would not stop; because the outside had, a little while ago, without knowing it, without ever being told, begun to end.\par
